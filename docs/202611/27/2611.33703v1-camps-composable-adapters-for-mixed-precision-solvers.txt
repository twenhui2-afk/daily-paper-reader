# ABSTRACT 

Mixed-precision solvers usually assign a fixed precision to each algorithmic component. This makes it hard to reuse a lower-precision solver at different depths inside a higher-precision solver. We introduce CAMPS (Composable Adapters for Mixed-Precision Solvers), a framework that uses iterative refinement to connect solvers across precisions. An adapter computes residuals and updates in the precision of its parent and passes each correction solve to a child solver in a lower precision. The child can be a direct solver, an iterative solver, or another adapter. The adapter checks every correction and keeps it if the residual does not increase. We prove three results. Adapters can be placed at any compatible set of precision boundaries in a solver tree without breaking the solver interface. Every output that the root solver reports as successful satisfies a backward-error bound set by the stopping tolerance and the root precision. Convergence follows from one sufficient condition per boundary, computed from the leaves of the tree to its root. We test five configurations on dense and sparse systems in 64-bit, 32-bit, and 16-bit floating-point arithmetic (FP64, FP32, and FP16). On dense well-conditioned systems, CAMPS is up to 9.6× faster than an FP64 direct solver for a single solve that includes the factorization. On sparse nonsymmetric systems, a three-precision hierarchy is the fastest method we tested. Compared with our implementation of the nested solver F3R, CAMPS is as fast and uses about 3.4× less peak memory. CAMPS does not always win. Plain conjugate gradient is faster on sparse symmetric positive definite systems. On very ill-conditioned systems, FP32 corrections fail, and the adapter detects the failure and switches to an FP64 fallback. 

Keywords mixed precision · iterative refinement · FGMRES · linear systems · preconditioning · composable solvers 

# 1 Introduction 

Numerical linear solvers face two bottlenecks: data movement and arithmetic. Sparse iterative solvers, with their matrix-vector products, preconditioner applications, and Krylov-basis accesses, are largely memory-bandwidth bound. Dense direct solvers, on the other hand, have high arithmetic intensity and are largely compute-bound [2, 92]. Mixed precision attacks both bottlenecks: sparse iterative solvers end up moving fewer bytes because each value occupies less memory, and dense factorizations can exploit faster lower-precision arithmetic units [110, 83]. However, mixed precision can introduce numerical cost. Rounding, a compressed dynamic range, and inexact lower-precision correction solves can each prevent a lower-precision method from ever attaining the requested accuracy [89, 38]. Mixed-precision iterative refinement (IR) is the standard response to this problem. It relies on the observation that obtaining an accurate final solution and obtaining a useful approximate correction are different requirements with different precision needs. Starting from an approximation xk, refinement forms the residual rk = b − Ax k in a higher (parent) precision, solves the correction equation Ad k = rk only approximately in a lower (child) precision, and applies the update xk+1 = xk + dk in the parent precision. This way, the residual and update carry the accuracy of the parent precision, while the expensive solve is delegated to a cheaper child precision. Moler analyzed iterative refinement in floating-point arithmetic in 1967 [127]. He proved that a system factorized in a lower precision can still be solved to full working accuracy, provided the residual is formed in a higher precision and the factorization is reused to solve for the correction. In particular, he proved that the error converges geometrically whenever the factorization is accurate enough arXiv:2611.33703v1 [cs.MS] 25 Nov 2026 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters and that the attainable accuracy is set by the precision of the residual rather than by the precision of the factorization. The two precisions therefore play separate roles: the low one pays for the expensive work and controls the rate of convergence, while the high one sets the accuracy the final answer can reach. The modern analysis by Carson and Higham [37] reformulated iterative refinement around a general correction solver rather than reused factorization, and they demonstrated that convergence depends on the accuracy of the computed correction, not on the factorization itself. Their GMRES-IR variant uses the low-precision factors only as a preconditioner for GMRES on the correction equation, which keeps refinement converging on matrices where the factorization alone is too inaccurate. In three precisions [38], one each for the factorization, the working arithmetic, and the residual, they gave the exact conditions required for the variant to reach working-precision accuracy. With a half-precision factorization, GMRES-IR extends the usable condition-number range by about eight orders of magnitude over plain refinement. Amestoy et al. [7] went further and let the matrix-vector products and the preconditioner applications inside GMRES-IR run in their own precisions, with five precisions in total. They proved error bounds for every combination, so the cost of the inner iteration can be weighed against the range of condition numbers it tolerates. Earlier, Buttari et al. [30] had demonstrated for sparse systems that a reduced-precision iterative solver can take the place of the factorization as the correction solver and still deliver 64-bit accuracy. The five-precision analysis explains when this works and when it fails. This paper offers a different interpretation of the mixed-precision iterative refinement algorithm. Instead of treating the precision transition as a loop, we treat it as a first-class solver object. Given a solver Sq that accepts and returns vectors in a lower precision q, we define an adapter IR p←q [Sq ]. This adapter accepts and returns vectors in the parent precision q, stores its state and forms residuals and updates in p, internally converts a safely scaled residual to q,invokes Sq , and promotes the returned correction back to p. Very importantly, the parent precision does not just round the problem down and cast the answer up. It continually measures and corrects the error the child introduces. The central property of this abstraction is that it is closed under composition. Because an adapter takes and returns vectors in the parent precision, the parent cannot tell it apart from any other solver in that precision. It can therefore be used anywhere a solver is expected, including as the child of another adapter. For example, an FP32 solver may wrap an FP16 solver, and the result may in turn be wrapped by an FP64 solver. One such example hierarchy is

Each adapter forms its residuals and updates in its own parent precision, and only the innermost preconditioner runs in FP16. Additionally, lowering the precision of a correction solve does not lower the precision of the problem around it, as the parent’s matrix-vector products, residual norms, updates, and Arnoldi process all stay in the parent precision. The main contribution of this paper is to precisely define how refinement could appear at several levels. Theorem 1 states that, under a residual contract at the parent level, a refinement adapter can be inserted at any compatible set of edges of a finite flexible solver tree. The tree is well defined at every depth, and the residual at each replaced edge satisfies a geometric bound after J corrections whose constants depend only on that edge, not on where the edge sits in the tree. We call this framework CAMPS, Composable Adapters for Mixed-Precision Solvers. The theorem is stated in terms of the residual that is actually achieved, so it still applies when the child solver is nonlinear or changes from call to call, which is the case that requires a flexible outer Krylov method [151]. We prove two further theorems, which say what a tree built this way guarantees. Theorem 3 states that any output the root reports as successful satisfies a backward-error bound set by its tolerance, regardless of what lies below it. Theorem 4 states that convergence needs one checkable condition at each edge, with the constants built up from the leaves to the root and the order of the limiting accuracy set by the root precision. CAMPS is a generalized framework, not a single algorithm, so it can be applied to any matrix class. Specifically, we claim that for each class of matrix, there is a tree in the CAMPS grammar that composes correctly and either beats a uniform-precision solver of the same type or demonstrates where it fails. We therefore test five named instances: CAMPS-Direct the bare adapter, CAMPS-Krylov the adapter inside FGMRES, CAMPS-Cascade the full FP64/FP32/FP16 hierarchy with refinement at both precision edges, CAMPS-Apex an adapter at the top edge only with plain casts below it, and CAMPS-Ladder which degrades precision when the contract fails. We run them on dense well-conditioned, dense ill-conditioned, sparse SPD, and sparse nonsymmetric matrices, and we compare them against uniform-FP64 direct, Krylov, and CG solvers. We also test against our owned tuned reimplementation of the F3R algorithm of Suzuki and Iwashita [165]. All methods use the same relative-residual stopping rule with residuals recomputed in FP64, median timings after warmup, both analytic and measured memory, and matrix-vector counts per precision. The experiments also test CAMPS’ own assumptions. The per-correction contraction that Lemma 3 bounds is measured on every instance, and the ill-conditioned sweep demonstrates where the instance crosses one. We therefore bring the following contributions: • A new interpretation of mixed-precision iterative refinement as a two-precision adapter IR p←q [Sq ]. Its residuals and updates are allocated in the parent precision, even when the child itself is an adapter. 2CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters • A recursive solver grammar. Adapters may compose across any finite solver tree, and any correction solver, whether it is direct, iterative, stationary, or another adapter, can fit behind the same interface. Additionally, a residual contract at the parent level describes each edge on its own, independent of the rest of the tree. • A theorem for insertion at arbitrary level. Refinement may be inserted at any compatible set of edges while the interfaces stay closed and each edge keeps a local residual bound which does not depend on depth. The propagation bound expresses the parent’s constants through conversion error, child solve quality, representation mismatch, and correction size. • Guarantees for arbitrary hierarchies. Every output that the root reports as successful satisfies a backward-error bound set by the tolerance and the root precision, whatever the subtree is, convergence follows from one checked condition per edge with explicit rounding constants, and a cost rule demonstrates when lowering precision is worse. • A family of named instances. We implement the bare adapter, the Krylov-wrapped adapter, the fully recursive two-edge hierarchy, the adapter-topped hierarchy, and the contract-fallback ladder, with each defined separately as an algorithm. • An evaluation across four matrix classes, including the recursive FP64/FP32/FP16 hierarchy, a condition-number sweep with measured contraction, an edge-placement ablation, a multi-right-hand-side amortization study, and a comparison with our tuned F3R reimplementation. • Two findings about our contract. We discover that past a computatable conditioning threshold, the sufficient condition for contraction no longer holds and low-precision children we tested stop producing verifiable corrections. Additionally, verifying each application does not itself control the quality of the outer Krylov basis. 

# 2 Related Work 

CAMPS closely aligns with several areas of research: iterative refinement and its mixed-precision analysis, mixed-precision solver implementations on accelerators, flexible, inexact, and nested Krylov methods, and composable solver software. This section surveys each area and states what CAMPS builds upon and what CAMPS contributes. Table 10 in the appendix summarizes the comparison against the closest prior work. 

Iterative refinement and its analysis. Let A ∈ Rn×n be nonsingular and b ∈ Rn. A step in iterative refinement forms rk = b − Ax k, computes an approximate correction dk to Ad k = rk, and updates xk+1 = xk + dk. Wilkinson analyzed this loop for Gaussian elimination with residuals computed in extended precision [180]. Moler’s floating-point analysis distinguished accurate residual computation from a lower-accuracy correction solve, creating the basis for convergence conditions of the form κ(A)u ≪ 1 [127]. Jankowski and Wo´ zniakowski demonstrated that refinement can make even an unstable solver backward stable if its corrections are sufficiently accurate [97]. Skeel established a component-wise result for Gaussian elimination [160], and Higham developed a unified analysis for general correction solvers [88, 89]. Later work addressed practical reliability of refinement. Demmel et al. studied the convergence rate of extra-precise refinement in LAPACK and reported failure when that rate indicated unreliable convergence [50]. Arioli, Demmel, and Duff examined how sparsity affects backward stability, demonstrating why only analyzing relative residuals may not sufficiently determine solution quality[15]. CAMPS uses the same residual correction update procedure and allows general correction solvers, like Higham’s analysis. Its main difference is how it implements correction accuracy. Prior analysis assumes sufficient accuracy in advance for a fixed refinement loop, but CAMPS instead checks the corresponding residual condition for every correction in every refinement loop in its solver tree. If a correction fails this check, CAMPS discards it and switches to another child solver. The failure flag of Demmel et al. is the closest precedent for this runtime check, but it monitors a single loop and does not replace the solver when a failure occurs. 

Mixed-precision refinement and the GMRES-IR family. Early mixed-precision methods combined single-precision factorization with double-precision refinement on CPUs and the Cell processor [110, 29, 109]. This approach was later extended to sparse direct solvers and inner-outer Krylov methods [30] and surveyed in [17]. Arioli and Duff demonstrated that FGMRES preconditioned by a single-precision LU factorization can achieve double-precision backward stability in the case where standard refinement fails [14]. Carson and Higham introduced GMRES-IR and analyzed refinement with a general correction solver. Their three-precision framework allows different precisions for factorization, working arithmetic, and residual computation [37, 38]. Later research extended this framework to five precisions [7] and to least-squares problems [41, 34, 35, 40]. Other extensions use approximate sparse factorizations [6], sparse approximate inverse and adaptive-precision precondi-3CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters tioners [39, 102], and low-rank corrections to factorization errors [90]. Related methods address symmetric positive definite systems using shifted low-precision Cholesky factorization [93], split-preconditioned FGMRES with four precisions [33], and Newton’s method [99]. Additional analyses explain how to scale matrices to fit within the range of half precision [94] and how to bound rounding errors in block fused multiply-add units [26]. These results provide the representation and rounding-error models needed for half-precision child solvers. Kelley describes conversions between precisions within a refinement loop [100]. Abdelfattah et al. [2] go through mixed-precision methods across dense and sparse linear algebra, including factorizations, iterative solvers, preconditioners, and multigrid, and report the speedups measured on GPUs with low-precision units. Higham and Mary [92] take the theory side and collect the rounding-error results that say when each scheme is stable and how accurate it can get. Buttari et al. also developed a modular backward-error framework that derives error bounds for a GMRES variant from bounds on its individual components [31]. These methods implement a fixed two-layer structure. An outer refinement loop calls an inner correction solver, which can then use several precisions internally. While their convergence guarantees depend on conditions imposed in advance on κ(A) and the chosen precisions, CAMPS extends this structure in three ways. First, it represents a precision transition explicitly as IR p←q [Sq ]. This allows the correction solver to contain another refinement loop, so refinement can be nested to any depth (Theorem 1). Second, it supports arbitrary correction solvers, including methods beyond factorization and factorization-preconditioned GMRES. Third, it checks an accuracy condition at runtime for each correction, rather than only relying on an accuracy assumption made in advance. CAMPS does not establish a new error bound for two-level refinement. For that situation, the five-precision analysis gives sharper bounds. Instead, CAMPS tackles how error bounds combine across nested levels. Theorem 2 describes how the constants at one level affect the next, and Theorem 4 extends this relation through the solver tree. For an LU leaf solver, the resulting condition reduces to the requirement κu f ≲ 1.The framework of Buttari et al. [31] offers a complementary approach. A CAMPS adapter’s contract specifies the component accuracy that their framework requires. Combining the two frameworks would be future work. 

Adaptivity, fallback, and escalation. Several methods already incorporate responses to low-precision solver failures at runtime. Buttari et al. limit the number of refinement iterations and refactorize in double precision if needed [29]. Hogg and Scott’s sparse symmetric solver first factorizes in single precision. It then refines the solution using IR or FGMRES in double precision. If refinement fails to converge, it falls back to a double-precision factorization [95]. NVIDIA’s cuSOLVER provides standard and GMRES-based refinement, with an optional fallback to a full-precision solver [47]. Oktay and Carson’s multistage refinement starts with the cheapest variant and observes its convergence rate. When progress stalls, it escalates through SGMRES-IR to GMRES-IR [136]. Wu et al. add a line search to each refinement step and prove that this prevents divergence even when the inner solves are very inaccurate [181]. Carson and Chen formulate runtime precision selection as a contextual bandit problem [32]. The QUDA lattice-QCD solvers use reliable updates within a Krylov solve. They perform iterations in single or half precision and periodically recompute the true residual in double precision [46]. CAMPS does not introduce fallback, residual recomputation, or escalation as a novelty. Its contribution has to do with where and when these mechanisms operate. CAMPS checks each correction at every adapter in the solver tree, and it rolls back rejected corrections instead of just counting them. Its grammar also includes fallback as a composition rule (Algorithm 6). This rule can work at any edge of the tree and wrap any child solver. Multistage refinement is the closest precedent for CAMPS-Ladder . It uses the convergence rate to decide when to switch between a fixed sequence of refinement variants. CAMPS-Ladder can escalate after a single rejected correction. Its child solvers can also use methods other than refinement. Section 5.4 evaluates residual-minimizing damping, following the approach of [181]. In these experiments, damping allows corrections to pass the acceptance test without restoring progress. It therefore prevents rejection from triggering escalation. To address this, damping must be paired with a threshold that requires sufficient progress. 

Mixed precision on accelerators. Early research evaluated half-precision factorization for iterative refinement on GPUs without tensor cores [81]. Later studies used tensor cores with FP32 accumulation. These studies showed that FP16 LU within GMRES-IR can achieve FP64 accuracy several times faster than FP64 LU [82, 83, 84]. The HPL-AI and HPL-MxP benchmarks established this two-level configuration as a community standard [107, 53]. Lopez and Mary store parts of the factors in FP16 to reduce data movement [118]. Batched half-precision kernels provide building blocks for these methods [1], and MAGMA provides the library foundation for vendor routines [169]. Error-free 4CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters transformations and integer-unit emulation can produce products with FP32 or FP64 accuracy using low-precision hardware [141, 139, 140, 142]. Thus, low-precision hardware can still support high-accuracy computation. The performance benefits vary. Zounon et al. found that single precision often speeds up sparse solvers by much less than the ideal factor of two [186]. Energy studies provide another reason to use low-precision child solvers: they can reduce energy use as well as runtime [8]. CAMPS uses generic PyTorch kernels and no tensor-core-specific factorization. It treats the methods above as potential child solvers. The dense experiments in this paper first reproduce the benefits of standard two-level refinement (Section 5.3). They then study how solver composition changes those benefits. Specifically, they test what happens when an additional adapter wraps the child solver, when the child occupies a different edge in the solver tree, or when an iterative solver replaces it through the same interface. 

Mixed precision inside sparse and Krylov solvers. Another line of research reduces precision within individual solver operations. Anzt, Heuveline, and Rocker use an outer error-correction loop with a low-precision inner Krylov solver. They analyze the tolerance that the inner solver must satisfy [9, 10]. Lindquist et al. interpret restarted GMRES as refinement with a single-precision inner cycle [114, 115]. Loe et al. compare GMRES-IR, low-precision preconditioners, and reduced-precision orthogonalization on GPUs in Trilinos. They note that Belos requires wrappers to combine precisions because it does not support it directly [116, 117]. Other methods reduce precision at specific components. Gratton et al. progressively lower the precision of inner products in GMRES [71] and operator evaluations in conjugate gradients [72]. Compressed-basis GMRES uses Ginkgo’s memory accessor to store the Krylov basis in reduced precision [5, 12, 77]. Adaptive-precision block-Jacobi [11, 62] and reduced-precision sparse approximate inverses [67] reduce preconditioner storage precision block by block. Adaptive-precision sparse matrix–vector products partition the operator according to entry magnitude [4, 70]. Scott and T˚ uma prevent breakdown during the construction of half-precision incomplete factorizations [155]. Sparse direct libraries also provide modes that combine single-precision factorization with double-precision refinement [6, 111]. AmgX offers mixed-precision storage for multigrid [131]. These methods change precision within a solver at a single level. While they target the basis, preconditioner storage, inner products, or operator, CAMPS controls precision across solver levels. It retains the parent-precision operator for residual computation and performs updates in the parent precision. It uses lower precision only for the correction solve. Each method above can therefore serve as a child solver within CAMPS. 

Mixed-precision multigrid. Early research used defect correction with a single-precision multigrid solver before GPUs became common [163, 68]. Later studies placed single-precision algebraic multigrid inside a double-precision Krylov loop. They evaluated this approach on clusters [57], accelerators [164, 170], and in matrix-free finite-element codes [106]. McCormick, Benzaken, and Tamstorf analyze refinement with multigrid as the inner solver, measuring error in the energy norm. They also introduce progressive precision, which sets each level’s precision based on the discretization [122, 168]. Progressive precision and CAMPS both build a precision hierarchy, but they differ in how they make decisions. Progressive precision assigns precisions in advance using discretization theory, while CAMPS checks each correction at runtime using the measured residual. A progressive-precision multigrid cycle could serve as a child solver in a CAMPS tree. We leave this combination for future work (Section 6). 

Flexible, inexact, and nested Krylov methods. GMRES minimizes the residual over a Krylov subspace. Its standard right-preconditioned form assumes a fixed linear preconditioner [153, 152]. Flexible GMRES stores each preconditioned basis vector separately, allowing the preconditioner to change between Arnoldi steps [151]. Flexible variants also exist for short-recurrence methods [176, 166, 175, 43]. For SPD systems, Axelsson and Vassilevski, Golub and Ye, Notay, and Knyazev and Lashuk study variable precondi-tioning [16, 69, 133, 104]. Knyazev and Lashuk show that CG with variable preconditioning may not converge faster than steepest descent [104]. A precision adapter therefore needs a parent solver that supports variable preconditioning. Local stopping rules, scaling, and failure handling can change the adapter’s behavior between iterations. They can also make the adapter nonlinear. The theory of inner-outer flexible methods [156] and inexact Krylov methods [157, 171, 27, 65, 158, 159] provides conditions for relaxing the accuracy of inner solves or operator applications as the outer residual decreases. Van den Eshof, Sleijpen, and van Gijzen develop relaxation strategies for nested Krylov methods [172]. Their work is the closest precedent for choosing inner tolerances that avoid unnecessary computation. Other work studies nested Krylov methods for extreme-scale systems [123]. 5CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters CAMPS does not introduce solver nesting or variable preconditioning. Inexact-Krylov theory derives accuracy requirements and proves convergence when they hold. CAMPS assigns an accuracy contract to each edge in the solver tree and checks each correction at runtime. Adapters could also use relaxation to request less accuracy from a child as the outer residual decreases, but we do not explore this extension here. Axelsson and Vassilevski require an inner solve to reduce its residual by a prescribed factor [16]. This requirement is an early form of the contraction contract used by CAMPS: CAMPS checks that contract at each step and rolls back corrections that violate it. 

Residual reliability, finite-precision behavior, and recycling. Krylov methods often update the residual recursively. Rounding errors can create a gap between this residual and the true residual. This gap limits attainable accuracy [73, 74, 126, 113]. Residual-replacement strategies periodically recompute the residual to reduce this gap [161, 174, 36]. Despite this issue, GMRES with modified Gram-Schmidt is backward stable [54, 143]. CAMPS checks each correction using r − Ad , which it computes directly in the parent precision. It does not rely on the child’s recursively updated residual. This check costs one parent-precision matrix-vector product per correction. It keeps the child’s residual gap from affecting the acceptance test. The parent precision determines the accuracy of this check (Section 4.3). Greenbaum, Pták, and Strakoš show that GMRES can follow any nonincreasing convergence curve [75]. Thus, eigenvalues alone cannot predict how much a Krylov child will reduce the residual in a given call. CAMPS measures this reduction directly. Recycling and augmentation preserve subspace information across restarts or related linear systems [58, 42, 49, 129, 19, 144, 66, 63, 162]. Oktay and Carson show that mixed-precision GMRES-IR can use GCRO-DR to recycle subspace information across successive correction solves [137]. A CAMPS adapter also solves a sequence of correction equations with the same matrix. However, the children tested here discard their subspaces after every correction (Section 4.2). Our SPD experiments measure the cost of this choice (Section 5.5). The SPD results suggest that a child that keeps its Krylov subspace between corrections would close most of this gap. Block methods solve systems with multiple right-hand sides [87, 138, 78]. Our study in Section 5.3 does not include these methods as baselines, and this remains a gap in the evaluation. 

Composable solver software. Solver composition is an established software pattern. PETSc represents solvers, preconditioners, and matrices as objects with abstract interfaces [20, 21]. Users can assemble composite, field-split, multigrid, and nested-Krylov preconditioners at runtime through option strings [28]. Kirby and Mitchell extend this approach across the boundary between PDE models and linear algebra [103]. Trilinos connects interoperable packages through templated interfaces [86, 121]. Belos provides pluggable status tests, while Amesos2 provides a common interface to direct solvers [22]. Other examples include hypre [59], AmgX [131], and the AMReX multilevel solver, which supports interchangeable bottom solvers [184]. Kokkos Kernels provides reusable computational kernels [146]. Krylov.jl provides Krylov methods that support multiple numerical types [128]. Ginkgo represents matrices, preconditioners, and solvers through a common linear-operator interface. It also provides portable GPU backends [13]. Related work proposes a modular precision ecosystem at the kernel level [12]. These systems establish the software foundations for solver composition. Using one solver as another solver’s preconditioner is therefore not a contribution of CAMPS. The distinction concerns how composition handles precision boundaries. Ginkgo and Trilinos specify value types per object. Ginkgo also documents iterative refinement in double precision around a single-precision inner solver, with conversions inserted where value types differ [64]. PETSc option strings do not specify precision transitions. Mixed-precision studies using these libraries report that developers must implement precision boundaries manually through wrappers [116]. CAMPS makes each precision boundary an explicit, typed edge in the solver tree. Each edge specifies a residual contract and defines how to handle failure. Our closure theorem establishes when these components can be composed. Belos status tests are the closest precedent for the contract, and PETSc option strings are the closest precedent for the CAMPS grammar. 

Algorithm selection and learned solver components. Rice formulated algorithm selection as choosing an algorithm from problem features [147, 148]. For sparse solvers, early work includes the self-adapting numerical software program [52], machine-learned selection among PETSc configurations [25, 55], and the Lighthouse framework [130, 98]. 6CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters Recent work explores parameter autotuning [145], learned matrix embeddings [179], and benchmark datasets for solver selection [182]. Surveys cover learned iterative methods [185] and automated algorithm selection more broadly [101]. Researchers have also developed learned components for individual solvers. These include multigrid prolongation and coarsening [76, 119, 167] and conjugate-gradient preconditioners [112, 85, 183]. Other work automates precision assignment. At the variable level, approaches include dynamic search [149], rigorous static bounds [44], and algorithmic differentiation [125]. For neural networks, hardware-in-the-loop reinforcement learning assigns precision by layer [177]. Carson and Chen use a contextual bandit to select solver precisions at runtime [32]. Several existing methods could train a selector over solver trees. Policy-gradient and actor–critic methods could learn from solver runs [154, 79]. Offline reinforcement learning could use logged solves [108, 105]. Constrained and Lyapunov-based policy optimization could impose constraints during learning [3, 45]. Other options include planning with a learned world model [80] and sampling diverse, high-reward candidates with generative flow networks [23]. Bandit-based hyperparameter optimization provides a non-sequential baseline for evaluating such policies [60]. Rein-forcement learning combined with correctness checks has already discovered faster matrix-multiplication and sorting routines [61, 120]. Control-theoretic approaches to learning [132, 178, 18, 24, 124, 56] and port-Hamiltonian interconnection theory offer a conceptual analogy for adapter composition. Power-conserving interconnections of port-Hamiltonian subsystems preserve the port-Hamiltonian structure [173]. This suggests viewing adapters as ports and asking which guarantees their composition preserves. This paper implements neither a learned component nor a selector. It defines a search space for future selectors: a grammar of precision-typed solver trees with runtime accuracy checks. These checks detect corrections that violate the residual contract, including those produced by a poor policy choice. They operate after each correction rather than only at the end of a solve. Each learned preconditioner cited above is a potential child solver, given that it satisfies the interface in Section 4.2. 

The closest prior art. Four works are especially close to CAMPS. Buttari et al. interpret mixed-precision refinement as preconditioned Richardson iteration. They also use a reduced-precision iterative solver as the preconditioner for an outer FGMRES method. This establishes the inner-outer mixed-precision pattern that CAMPS generalizes [30]. Hogg and Scott incorporate runtime fallback to a double-precision factorization in a production sparse solver [95]. Oktay and Carson use the convergence rate to decide when to escalate between refinement variants [136]. Suzuki and Iwashita combine FP64, FP32, and FP16 arithmetic in a deeply nested CPU/GPU Krylov solver. Their evaluated F3R configuration uses three FGMRES levels and an innermost Richardson level. Only the outermost FGMRES level checks convergence [165]. Their general framework also allows Richardson iteration at multiple levels in principle. However, they do not define a mixed-precision adapter contract, and they also do not prove that independently stopping refinement layers can be inserted at arbitrary nesting depths. We therefore do not claim solver nesting, progressive precision reduction, or runtime fallback as new contributions. Nor do we claim to introduce the general idea of using Richardson iteration at multiple levels. 

Positioning of CAMPS. CAMPS makes both mathematical and architectural contributions. It represents each precision transition as a typed edge IR p←q . Each edge can use a different child solver family. Every application must satisfy a residual contract evaluated at the parent level. Our arbitrary-layer theorem establishes when refinement layers can be composed at any depth. It states explicit hypotheses and a residual bound for each edge (Section 4.4). Our propagation bound describes how the constants at one level affect the next (Section 4.5). A CAMPS hierarchy can place an independently stopping adapter at any or every precision boundary. This contrasts the published F3R configuration, which places Richardson iteration only at the innermost level. CAMPS also supports escalation after an individual correction fails its contract. It rolls back that correction and switches child solvers. This mechanism can operate at any edge with any child solver that satisfies the interface. Section 5.8 evaluates these design choices. We test adapters at one edge, at two edges, and in configurations that combine adapters with precision casts. A cast edge connects a parent to a lower-precision child like an adapter does, but it accepts the child’s correction without checking the residual. We also reimplement the F3R structure in the same experimental framework and apply identical tuning. These experiments place F3R, the fully instrumented CAMPS hierarchy, and the intermediate configurations within a common design space. 7CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters The best measured configuration checks corrections at exactly one edge. # 3 Grammar 

Let F0, F 1, . . . , F L denote the supported floating-point formats, ordered from highest to lowest precision, with unit roundoffs u0 < u 1 < · · · < u L. Let Qp(z) denote rounding or conversion to format p. After any required equilibration, 

Ap = Qp(A) denotes the coefficient values stored at level p. A solver node Sp maps a right-hand side in F np to an approximate solution in F np . The following conceptual grammar generates CAMPS solver trees: 

Sp ::= Base p(parameters ) | IR J,τ p←q [Sq ] | FGMRES m,τ p [Sp] | Cast p←q [Sq ], up < u q .

Here Base p may be ILU(0), a sparse approximate inverse, Jacobi, or any operator whose input and output formats are p.The type rule for IR p←q [Sq ] requires the child to accept precision q and guarantees that the adapter returns precision p.This allows recursive construction while stopping implicit or undocumented casts between incompatible nodes. A flexible Krylov node calls a child of its own precision, so a lower-precision child must sit under an adapter or a cast. An edge from p to q also requires Fq ⊂ Fp, so that promoting the child’s output adds no error. FP16 and BF16 are not nested in either direction, so no edge connects them, and both sit below FP32. An adapter may also call a fallback child in its own precision (Algorithm 1), which is the one case with q = p. Figure 1 shows a representative recursively typed hierarchy.

The fourth production, Cast p←q [Sq ], is a plain precision boundary. It converts the parent’s vector to the lower precision, invokes Sq , and converts the result back to the parent precision. It performs no residual computation, scaling, or acceptance test. It is type-correct but unchecked since it carries no residual contract. Since a cast edge checks nothing, it should only sit below a parent that does not rely on a check, such as a flexible Krylov solver, which can absorb a poor correction, or an adapter higher in the tree, which checks the combined result. Throughout the paper, we distinguish checked corrections (adapter edges) from unchecked directions (cast edges and the guard substitutions of Section 4.8). These four productions cover the node types we use in this paper, but the list is not complete. What decides whether a node can appear in a tree is the contract in Section 4.2, not the algorithm it runs. Any node that meets the contract can sit at any position. For example, the experiments in Section 5.5 use a conjugate gradient child, which has no production of its own. A multigrid cycle or a sparse triangular solve would work the same way, with no change to the adapter.

4.2 The residual-reducing correction contract 

Our composition results rest on one requirement. We state it first in exact arithmetic. It applies to any nonsingular A,whether dense, sparse, or structured. Fix an iterate xk with residual rk = b − Ax k. We call a map C a residual-reducing correction if, starting from the zero vector, it returns a correction dk = C(A, r k) with 

∥rk − Ad k∥2 ≤ ∥ rk∥2 . (1) In words, the correction never makes the residual larger. Nothing else about C is assumed. Applying the correction, 

xk+1 = xk + dk, gives 

rk+1 = b − Ax k+1 = ( b − Ax k) − Ad k = rk − Ad k,

so (1) means directly that ∥rk+1 ∥2 ≤ ∥ rk∥2. If the inequality is strict, the residual drops at every step. If it holds with equality, the residual may stay the same. Every node that meets this condition looks the same to the solver above it. Given the matrix, a right-hand side, and a zero initial guess, it returns a correction that does not increase the residual it was given: 

solve (A, r, 0) −→ d such that ∥r − Ad ∥2 ≤ ∥ r∥2

A solver can therefore use any such node as a correction step or as a preconditioner. Two properties of this condition shape the rest of the framework. 

Why the child starts from zero. When the child starts from d = 0 , its initial residual is the parent’s current residual 

rk. When it returns dk, its final residual rk − Ad k is the parent’s next residual rk+1 . Thus, the child’s own stopping test measures the parent’s progress directly, with no change of variables between levels. This makes (1) automatic for many children. A residual-monotone method such as GMRES or MINRES never ends with a larger residual than it started with, so it returns d with ∥rk − Ad ∥2 ≤ ∥ rk − Ad 0∥2, where d0 is its initial guess. 10 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters With d0 = 0 , the right-hand side of this inequality is ∥rk∥2, so (1) holds without any extra check. At worst, the residual stays the same. A nonzero start can also work. It satisfies (1) whenever d0 itself does, that is, whenever ∥rk − Ad 0∥2 ≤ ∥ rk∥2, and a good initial guess can reduce the residual further. But no fixed nonzero start works for every right-hand side. For any 

d0̸ = 0 , take r = −ϵAd 0 with ϵ > 0. Then ∥r − Ad 0∥2 = (1 + ϵ) ∥Ad 0∥2, which is larger than ∥r∥2 = ϵ ∥Ad 0∥2. For a residual-monotone child, starting from zero is thus sufficient for (1), as it is the only fixed start that works for every right-hand side, but it is not a necessary condition. Children that reuse earlier work still fit the interface. The identity b − A(x + d) = r − Ad holds however the child produces d, so a child may warm-start, keep a recycled or augmented Krylov subspace [144], or keep the history of a variable-preconditioned conjugate gradient method [133, 104]. It only has to return the complete correction d0 + ∆ d,measured against the r it was given. The adapter tests that complete correction, so d0 does not need to pass the test on its own. 4.4 Arbitrary-layer composition 

An adapter returns a vector in its parent’s format. Thus, it can be used anywhere a parent of the same format expects an approximate inverse, including as the child of another adapter. For example, the tree 

IR 64 ←32 [IR 32 ←16 [ILU(0) 16 ]] 

behaves like an FP64 approximate inverse to whatever calls it. Its outer adapter computes residuals and updates in FP64, and its inner adapter computes them in FP32. The innermost base preconditioner is the one part of the tree that runs in FP16. Theorem 1 gives conditions under which a tree of nested adapters is a well-defined solver at any depth. 14 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters 

Theorem 1 (Arbitrary-Layer Adapter Closure) . Let T be a finite solver tree whose internal Krylov nodes accept variable right-preconditioner applications. At any subset E of compatible edges, replace the child application at edge 

e = ( p, q ) by an adapter IR Je,τ e 

> p←q

[Sq ]. Assume each inserted adapter returns a vector in F np . Then the modified tree is well defined at every depth. Additionally, assume that at edge e the exact parent residuals of the accepted corrections satisfy 

∥rt+1 ∥2 ≤ ηe ∥rt∥2 + Ω e, 0 ≤ ηe < 1,

where Ωe bounds the whole one-step error in (3) , including the residual-evaluation and update-rounding terms. Lemma 3 gives an example of such a recurrence, with ηe = ˆ η and Ωe = f (g). After the je ≤ Je corrections that were accepted, the local residual satisfies the geometric bound of Lemma 1 with η = ηe, ω = Ω e, and J = je. The bound has the same form at every position in T , but its constants depend on the subtree below the edge. Every enclosing node that admits variable correction applications, such as another adapter or FGMRES, can use the adapter as its child. 

The proof appears in Appendix A.3. Theorem 1 states the interface, the assumptions, and the local residual bounds needed to place adapters anywhere in a solver tree. Suzuki and Iwashita informally note that Richardson iteration could appear at several levels of a nested solver [165], but the theorem makes this idea precise. The theorem assumes contraction at each edge where an adapter is inserted. It does not derive the parent’s contraction from the child’s behavior. Theorem 2 gives that relation, using child constants that are measured or assumed, and Theorem 4 combines the two results from the leaves up to the root. The local bound has the same form at every depth, with no factor that grows with depth. Its constants can still change from edge to edge. The values of (ηe, ω e) depend on the whole subtree below the edge, through (5). “Well defined at every depth” means that each node returns a vector of the required type and an explicit status after a bounded amount of work. Adapters stop after J attempts or at one of the earlier exits in Algorithm 1, and Krylov nodes stop at their iteration caps. The two degenerate cases are handled differently. If the Arnoldi process breaks down ( hj+1 ,j = 0 ), the Krylov node ends the cycle, commits the minimizer over the directions it has, and recomputes the residual. If a child returns a zero direction, our implementation substitutes the identity direction and records each substitution. Stopping does not mean the residual is small. Contract (1) allows a zero correction at every step, so non-increase does not imply convergence. Progress requires a strict reduction in the residual, as in (2), or a strict decrease in energy for SPD parents. When progress stops, the stagnation check returns an explicit status. Composition also does not guarantee better performance. It creates a space of possible designs. Each layer adds casts, residual evaluations, reductions for the stopping tests, and child calls. A deeper tree helps when the cheaper work in the child outweighs the added overhead and the parent still receives a sufficiently contractive correction. Section 5.7 shows a case where an extra layer makes the solver slower. 

4.5 Child-to-parent propagation

4.6 Outer FGMRES and reliable residuals 

The contract also gives a reason to use FGMRES as the outer solver. At the start of a restart cycle, let r0 = βv 1 with 

∥v1∥2 = 1 . Suppose the first adapter application z1 = P (v1) satisfies ∥v1 − Az 1∥2 ≤ ηP . Then x0 + βz 1 lies in the FGMRES search space, and its residual norm is at most ηP ∥r0∥2. In exact arithmetic, FGMRES minimizes the residual over this space, so it reduces the residual by at least this factor. This bound links the measured quality of the adapter to progress in the outer solver. It does not guarantee fast convergence in later iterations. The preconditioner Pj can differ between applications. Its output depends on the input vector, on when the inner solvers stop, and on the scale chosen for each call. Thus, CAMPS stores every vector zj that it produces, which is what FGMRES requires. In finite precision, the cheap residual estimate from the Hessenberg matrix can drift away from the true residual. Thus, our implementation recomputes r = b − A64 x in FP64 at every restart and uses this recomputed residual for the final convergence check. It reports both the residual at which it stopped and a residual recomputed independently after the solve. 

4.7 Guarantees for arbitrary hierarchies: stability, convergence, and cost 

Theorem 1 is a local composition result, and Theorem 2 carries a contract across one edge. This subsection combines them over a complete solver tree. The results answer three questions. First, Theorem 3 bounds the error of every output that the root reports as S UCCESS ,under the rounding model and the root’s stopping test, with no assumptions on the subtree. Second, Theorem 4 gives sufficient conditions for convergence. It computes the required constants one level at a time, starting at the leaves of the tree and ending at the root. Third, Proposition 3 gives a criterion for deciding whether a hierarchy costs less than an alternative. Neither convergence nor a speedup holds for every hierarchy. In Section 5.4, the trees that only rely on an FP32 LU child fail to converge once κu 32 exceeds about one. In Section 5.5, every tree we tested is slower than CG in uniform precision. Scope of these results. These results have four limits. First, the conditions in Theorem 4 are sufficient, not necessary. A tree may converge even when its computed θ is larger than one. Theorem 3 still bounds any output that passes the root’s stopping test. Second, the constants are worst-case bounds. The factors γ(p) 

> n+1

and κ′ 

> p

can overestimate typical rounding effects by orders of magnitude [91]. For a half-precision parent, the rule gives no usable bound once n u 16 ≥ 1, unless the matrix is sparse enough to allow a smaller factor. Section 5.9 measures the gap between the bounds and the observed behavior. Third, (R4) bounds the progress of a restart cycle by its first Arnoldi direction. Thus, it ignores the extra acceleration that later directions provide. It also gives no bound when the child is not itself a contraction, which can happen with a single ILU(0) application. In that case, the Krylov node has to be treated as a base node under (R1), with its factor taken from GMRES convergence theory or from measurement. Fourth, (R4) assumes exact arithmetic for the Arnoldi process, the projected solve, and the update. Existing work analyzes the attainable accuracy of flexible GMRES in finite precision [14, 31], but a self-contained analysis with a nonlinear adapter as the preconditioner is still open. A forward-error analysis of nested refinement is also open, though Carson and Higham give such analyses for refinement in two and three precisions [37, 38]. These limits apply to the recursive convergence result. 4.8 A construction strategy and the evaluated instances 

CAMPS defines a family of methods. We build and evaluate each instance one precision edge at a time. 1. Choose a highest-precision parent. Its precision sets the accuracy of the final residual and of the updates. 2. Insert one adapter at a candidate edge, and measure its contraction at the parent level, its cost, and its memory use. 3. Recurse inside the child if the one-level adapter still contracts and the next lower precision reduces the total work or storage. Otherwise, stop at one level. 4. Compare each instance against the parent without nesting, against a one-level alternative, and against a fixed nested baseline such as F3R [165]. This strategy follows the local structure of the theory. Measurements at each parent decide whether to keep an edge, and composition assembles the full tree from the edges that are kept.

# 5 Experiments 

The contract of Section 4.2 does not factor in sparsity, symmetry, or conditioning. Thus, the experiments are not trying to show that one CAMPS instance is fast on one matrix. We test a broader claim: that for each matrix class, the CAMPS grammar should have a tree that composes correctly and either beats a comparable uniform-precision solver or shows where it stops helping. We test this claim on four matrix classes: dense well-conditioned, dense ill-conditioned, sparse symmetric positive definite (SPD), and sparse nonsymmetric. We use the named instances of Section 4.8, and Figure 10 draws the solver tree of each one. We also test two placements that Theorem 1 permits beyond a single edge. The first is CAMPS-Cascade , an FP64/FP32/FP16 hierarchy with refinement at two precision edges. The second is an ablation that moves one child across different sets of edges. Throughout this section, we denote ˆη as the measured ratio ∥rt+1 ∥2 / ∥rt∥2 of parent residuals across one correction. This is what contract (2) bounds, so we check the hypothesis of the theorem on every run instead of just assuming it. The measured ratio comes from one run and one right-hand side. It is not the uniform factor ˆη of Lemma 3, which has to hold for every right-hand side. All the raw results, operation counts per precision, environment records, seeds, configuration files, and scripts are lo-cated in the CAMPS repository at https://github.com/CVC-Lab/CAMPS (commit b44dc2de32fb ). In the repository, the file REPRODUCIBILITY.md links every result file to the command, seeds, protocol, and environment that produced it. 

5.1 Implementation 

CAMPS is written in Python on PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA 12.8. All timings in this section come from an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti Laptop GPU. On this GPU, FP64 arithmetic runs at 1/64 of the FP32 rate, which strongly favors lower-precision factorizations. However, on a datacenter GPU with full-rate FP64, the dense speedups below would be expected to be smaller. The core package is about 1,700 lines. The precision conversions in it are explicit, and nodes that receive a vector in the wrong format raise an error instead of casting it. The adapter is based on Algorithm 1 step by step and records every 24 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters attempt and its outcome. We also turn off TF32 and reduced-precision reductions, so FP32 means IEEE FP32 multiplies. Half-precision matrix products and norms accumulate in FP32, and code records each of these precision promotions. There is a hardware gap that influences the dense experiments. cuSOLVER does not expose a standalone half-precision factorization or triangular solve. Its mixed-precision gesv drivers can factorize in half precision internally [47], but they are complete solvers with their own refinement loop, and our code does not use them. Thus, our FP16 dense children are approximate inverses, computed in FP64 and applied in half precision through tensor-core matrix products. Our FP16 sparse children are half-precision Krylov iterations with Jacobi or Richardson preconditioning. On the same GPU, the reruns reproduce iteration counts, acceptance decisions, and residuals bit for bit. We also ran the full verification suite on an NVIDIA A100 node of the Lonestar6 system at the Texas Advanced Computing Center, and it passed every check. Appendix A.6 describes the software, the verification suite, and the A100 results in full.

Timing and memory. We time setup (casts, factorizations, and inverse construction) separately from solves. If two methods are close, we also record the interquartile range, and all the interquartile ranges are in the archived tables. We also report memory in two ways. We record the count of the bytes in every object the solver owns, such as operator copies, factors, inverses, and Krylov bases. We also record the CUDA allocator’s own measurement, which provides resident memory after setup and peak memory during a solve. 

Convergence plots. For each experiment we plot the relative residual ∥b − Ax ∥2 / ∥b∥2 against the outer iterations of the root solver. When the root is an adapter, one outer iteration is one accepted correction, and the plotted value is the residual that the adapter recomputes in FP64. When the root is GMRES or FGMRES, one outer iteration is one Krylov iteration. The plotted value is the residual estimate from the Arnoldi recurrence, replaced by the recomputed FP64 residual at each restart and at the last iteration. One outer iteration of a CAMPS tree can contain a full inner solve in lower precision. Thus, these plots show how the residual decreases, and the tables give the cost. 

Baselines. For each matrix class, we compare the CAMPS instances against the uniform-FP64 direct solve and against FP64 Krylov with a matching FP64 preconditioner. This is FGMRES 64 (LU 64 ) for dense problems and CG 64 for SPD problems. We also include GMRES 64 with a fixed preconditioner, because a flexible method should not be assumed to be faster (Section 6). CAMPS-Direct is classical mixed-precision iterative refinement. CAMPS-Krylov is the same solver with an outer FGMRES added, and both share the same children, scaling, and contract. Thus, comparing them shows what the FGMRES layer contributes, with no differences in implementation mixed in. 

Child families. The same adapter accepts six different kinds of child, with no change to its code and no special cases. • Dense LU ( LU 32 , with LU 64 as a fallback child). • An approximate inverse applied in half precision through tensor cores ( INV 16 , INV b16 ). • Symmetric Krylov ( CG 32 ). • Nonsymmetric Krylov ( FGMRES 32 , FGMRES 16 ). 25 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters • Stationary sweeps (Jacobi, and fixed-count Richardson inside CAMPS-Apex ). • Other adapters. The adapter only asks each of them for the contract stated in Section 4.2, and the parent never knows which kind of child it called.

Failure is fast and has a clear cause. For κ ≥ 10 8, the acceptance test stops CAMPS-Direct at the first correction that increases the residual. On the other hand, the FGMRES-wrapped variants keep iterating for 3.2-4.6 s until they hit the iteration limit, and they only report that they did not converge, not indicating why. We also tried a fix suggested by GMRES-IR, which keeps using low-precision LU factors as a preconditioner even when they no longer reduce the residual on their own. We let the outer FGMRES accept these corrections as search directions, allowing the residual to grow by up to 100 ×. The table calls this loose acceptance, and it does not help. At 

κ = 10 8, a single FP32 LU correction increases the residual of a random unit right-hand side by about 300 ×, and each further correction multiplies it again. Classical GMRES-IR runs GMRES on the correction equation, with the low-precision LU factors as its preconditioner. CAMPS-Krylov is built differently. Its outer FGMRES takes the adapter’s whole correction as one search direction. Thus, the failure marks the limit of the configuration we tested on a matrix class built to be hard for it. Going further in experimentation would mean either applying the preconditioner in higher precision, as the Carson–Higham analysis assumes [37], or having a stronger child. 

A fallback child makes the solver robust. The fallback version of CAMPS-Direct has a second child, an FP64 LU factorization. The adapter uses the cheap FP32 child as long as its corrections pass the acceptance test. Up to κ = 10 7,the fallback solver behaves the same as plain iterative refinement. As soon as a correction fails, the adapter switches to the FP64 child. With this switch, it converges at every κ from 10 4 to 10 12 . For κ ≥ 10 8, where every FP32-only instance fails, it needs three corrections. The first FP32 correction is accepted and the second is rejected. A single FP64 correction after the rejection reaches the tolerance. The solve takes 6-7 ms, and the normwise backward error is 10 −14 

or smaller. The cost is in the setup, which has to build both factorizations and takes 28 ms, versus 5 ms for just the FP32 factorization. A lazy version could only build the FP64 factors when the first correction fails. 

CAMPS-Ladder (Algorithm 6) also converges at every κ, in 2-7 outer iterations. Its last resort, an unchecked FP64 LU direction, was never needed because the checked FP64 fallback always worked. Damping needs a progress threshold. Damping scales each correction by the factor that minimizes the new residual (Section 4.3). At κ = 10 8, the damped ladder therefore needs 6 outer iterations and 34 ms, against 2 iterations and 10 ms without damping. With the stricter test (2) and a progress threshold of ϱ = 0 .9, any step that reduces the residual by less than 10% counts as a failure. For such a step, the damped fallback then switches to FP64. For κ ≥ 10 8 it converges in three corrections, which is the same as the undamped fallback (the progress-threshold row of Table 4). So when damping is on, the acceptance test must check that the residual drops enough, not only that it does not grow. This shows why contract (1) alone does not guarantee convergence.

5.8 Comparison with F3R 

F3R [165] is the closest prior method to CAMPS. It nests three FGMRES solvers, in FP64, FP32, and FP16, and ends in a fixed number of Richardson sweeps. Each inner solver runs for a fixed number of iterations, the precision boundaries are plain casts, and only the outermost solver checks for convergence. The authors’ code is public 1, but it runs hand-optimized C++ and CUDA kernels, so its timings would mostly measure kernel speed. We instead rebuilt the F3R algorithm structure in our own code, using the same kernels, matrices, and stopping rule as the CAMPS solvers. One difference matters. The authors’ released solver uses a block ILU(0) preconditioner inside the Richardson sweeps, and we use Jacobi, which is the preconditioner shared by every method in this section. We compare F3R with three CAMPS trees. The first is the sparse CAMPS-Cascade . The second is CAMPS-Apex 

(Algorithm 5), which keeps F3R’s FP32 and FP16 levels but replaces its outer FGMRES with an adapter. The third is a hybrid with an adapter at the outer boundary and a cast at the inner one. All four trees go through the same tuning procedure. For each matrix class, we run a grid search over the inner iteration budgets on the 32 3 problem, keep the fastest setting that converges, and reuse it at 48 3. F3R gets the largest grid, with 12 settings.

The F3R algorithm works well in our implementation. Our reimplementation is 1.9-3.0× faster than the FP64 flexible baseline and 1.1-2.1× faster than CAMPS-Cascade , which checks both of its edges. 

Checking every edge costs extra work. On Poisson at 32 3, F3R performs 5 FP64, 26 FP32, and 360 FP16 products, while the cascade performs 19 , 148 , and 434 . The cascade does this extra work for two reasons. First, each adapter recomputes the residual in the parent precision before it accepts a correction. Second, the cascade’s inner solvers keep iterating until they reach a tolerance, while F3R’s stop after a fixed, small number of iterations. Our measurements do not show how much each reason contributes. The hybrid, which has a single adapter, is also much slower than 

CAMPS-Apex , which has one too. The two differ in three ways. The hybrid keeps F3R’s outer FP64 FGMRES, lets its FP32 solver run to a tolerance, and preconditions its FP16 solver with Jacobi instead of Richardson sweeps. On Poisson at 32 3 it performs 13 FP64, 47 FP32, and 455 FP16 products, against 4, 26 , and 360 for CAMPS-Apex .

CAMPS-Apex is as fast as our F3R implementation and uses less memory. CAMPS-Apex keeps F3R’s FP32 and FP16 levels but replaces its outer FP64 FGMRES with a plain CAMPS adapter. Each correction then costs one FP64 residual, a power-of-two scaling, a cast, and an acceptance check, with no FP64 Arnoldi process, orthogonalization, or stored basis. On Poisson, the two are within 1% of each other, and their interquartile ranges overlap. On convection-diffusion, CAMPS-Apex takes about 6% less time, and at 32 3 the interquartile ranges do not overlap. It also uses about 3.4× less peak memory in every case, because it stores no FP64 Krylov basis. And unlike F3R, it checks every correction at its top edge, so it measures the contraction, detects stagnation, and reports why a correction was rejected. On Poisson, CAMPS-Direct [CG 32 ] from Section 5.5 is 2.1× faster than F3R at 48 3 (169 .0 against 351 .2 ms) because its CG child takes advantage of the symmetric matrix and F3R’s general nesting does not. CG 64 is still faster than every mixed-precision tree we tested on Poisson. 

Every tree here depends on its inner settings. F3R’s inner budgets matter a lot. In our tuning grid, doubling its FP16 budget from 5 to 10 iterations made it 2.1-2.4× slower, and the worst setting was up to 7.6× slower than the best setting. Every setting still converged, so nothing in the solver signals a poor choice. CAMPS-Apex keeps F3R’s fixed budgets and is just as sensitive. On Poisson, its worst setting was 3.5× slower than its best. On the other hand, 

CAMPS-Cascade has no fixed budgets. Its levels stop on their own tolerances, and its default settings converged on every problem in Sections 5.5 and 5.6. However, converging is not the same as being fast: on Poisson at 32 3, the cascade took 923 ms with its default settings during tuning versus 419 ms with its best setting. A cast is a legal part of a CAMPS tree that skips the residual check (Section 3). 5.10 Summary 

• Dense, well-conditioned. CAMPS-Direct is 9.6× faster end to end than the FP64 direct solve at n = 8192 .It stays ahead at every tested block size up to k = 16 , and the extrapolated crossover is at k ≈ 100 or later. 

CAMPS-Cascade also converges, and it does so in a single outer iteration. • Dense, ill-conditioned. From κ = 10 4 to 10 7, the measured contraction of the FP32 child follows 0.6 κu 32 within a factor of about two. Once the child stops reducing the residual, the adapter rejects it at once and reports why. The fallback converges at every κ up to 10 12 with about 7 ms of solve time, and the ladder also converges everywhere. Damping lets every correction pass without making progress, so it needs a progress threshold to keep the fallback working. • Sparse SPD. Every instance converges, with forward errors 30 to 5 × 10 6 times smaller than those of CG 64 .However, plain CG 64 is still faster than every tested instance. This is because on this problem, FP32 barely saves any time per iteration, and the last correction runs past the tolerance. We recommend CG 64 for this class. • Sparse nonsymmetric. CAMPS-Cascade , with adapters at both precision boundaries and FP16 products at the leaf, is the fastest method of Section 5.6 in all four cases. It is 1.15 -1.59 × faster than the best FP64 baseline and reads 2.9× fewer bytes of matrix values, or 1.3× fewer when the 64-bit indices are counted. The same settings converge over a 50 × range of wind strengths. • Placement. Every tested placement converges when the children are accurate enough. Adding an FP32 level above the BF16 child cuts its FP64 work by 20 × but makes the whole solve slower. Whether a child works depends on κ

times its own unit roundoff, which is what the per-edge bound predicts. • F3R. Our reimplementation confirms that the F3R algorithm works well. CAMPS-Apex is within 1% of it on Poisson and about 6% faster on convection-diffusion, with about 3.4× less peak memory, and CAMPS-Direct [CG 32 ] beats it by 2.1× on Poisson. CAMPS-Cascade is 1.1-2.1× slower than F3R. It converges on every problem with default settings, but like every tree here, it is faster when tuned. • Recursive bounds. On a chain of FP64, FP32, and FP16 adapters, the measured factors never exceed the bounds wherever the bounds give a usable number. Each extra inner correction multiplies the top-edge factor by the lower edge’s factor, until it levels off at 0.44 -0.96 u32 amp( b). Neither the leaf nor FP16 affects where it levels off. In all 

57 configurations, the root’s final backward error is between 0.43 u64 and 0.88 u64 .These results support the claim we stated at the start of this section. No single tree wins everywhere, and on SPD Poisson, none of ours wins at all. However, for every class, our CAMPS grammar contains a tree that either beats the FP64 baseline or shows clearly why it does not. The measured contraction tells us in advance which children will converge and which will not. The same interface accepted direct, approximate-inverse, stationary, symmetric Krylov, nonsymmetric Krylov, and adapter children without any changes. Every placement we tested gave a working solver, as Theorem 1 predicts, and on the dense chain where we checked them, the bounds of Theorem 4 held. 

# 6 Conclusion & Future Extensions 

This paper presented CAMPS, an interface for composing mixed-precision correction solvers. An adapter computes residuals and updates in the precision of its parent, and it checks every correction before it keeps it. We showed that 39 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters adapters can be placed at any compatible set of edges in a solver tree. We gave a backward-error bound for every output that the root reports as successful, and sufficient conditions for convergence, with constants computed from the leaves of the tree to its root. We tested five instances on four matrix classes. The rest of this section discusses what the experiments show, where the method falls short, and what remains to be done. 

Where adapters help. An adapter moves part of the computation to a lower precision. It does not make the correction solve easier because the child still has to reduce the residual. An adapter helps when the FP64 work it replaces costs much more than the extra work it adds, and our experiments show three cases of this. In the dense experiments, the adapter replaces an FP64 factorization with an FP32 factorization that is 5–13 × faster (Section 5.3). In the sparse nonsymmetric experiments, the cascade replaces most of the FP64 GMRES iterations and the orthogonalization that comes with them, with cheaper iterations in FP32 and FP16 (Section 5.6). In CAMPS-Apex , the adapter replaces the outer FP64 FGMRES, so no FP64 Krylov basis is stored, and the peak memory is about 3.4× lower than that of our F3R implementation (Section 5.8). Adapters did not help on SPD Poisson, where no CAMPS instance was faster than plain CG 64 (Section 5.5). On that problem, FP32 saves almost no time in each CG iteration. The last correction also runs until it reaches its own tolerance, well past the point where its inner residual falls below the parent’s tolerance. Restarting CG from zero at each correction adds few iterations. A child that stops at the parent’s tolerance could save part of this work, but we have not tested one. Every adapter also adds work in the parent precision. Each correction needs one residual evaluation and two conversions. The cascade, which checks both of its edges, is 1.1–2.1× slower than our F3R implementation (Section 5.8). It is slower for two reasons. Its adapters perform these checks, and its inner solvers run until they reach a tolerance instead of stopping after a fixed number of iterations. Adapters can also use more memory than expected. When a child keeps its own copy of the matrix to measure its residual, that copy can cancel the storage savings of the lower precision (Section 5.3). With many right-hand sides, the advantage of a cheaper setup becomes smaller, because the FP64 factorization is shared across more columns. CAMPS-Direct is still the fastest method at every block size we tested, up to k = 16 .Extrapolating our measurements puts the crossover at k ≈ 100 or later (Table 3). 

Outer refinement versus outer FGMRES. An outer refinement loop applies corrections one after another and converges when each one contracts. An outer FGMRES uses the corrections as search directions, so it can tolerate corrections of uneven quality, but it pays for basis storage and orthogonalization. In our tests, FGMRES reduced the number of outer iterations but never the time. On the ill-conditioned sweep at κ = 10 4, 10 6, and 10 7, CAMPS-Krylov 

needs 1, 2, and 5 outer iterations, against 3, 5, and 13 corrections for CAMPS-Direct . Its solves still take longer, 8.0,

15 .2, and 38 .2 ms against 6.5, 10 .4, and 26 .0 ms. CAMPS-Direct is also faster on dense well-conditioned systems, on SPD systems, and on blocks of right-hand sides. In the F3R comparison, CAMPS-Apex , with an adapter on top, is as fast as our F3R implementation, which has FGMRES on top. Once the child stops contracting, with ˆη > 1, neither outer loop converges, and a stronger child is needed, as in the fallback and ladder instances. Flexibility also has a small cost when the preconditioner is fixed. In the sparse nonsymmetric runs, GMRES was about 4–5% faster than FGMRES with the same Jacobi preconditioner and used less memory. 

What the acceptance test does and does not provide. The test measures progress. Every instance we report records 

ˆη for each correction, and on the ill-conditioned sweep ˆη follows 0.6 κu 32 from κ = 10 4 to 10 7. The test also detects failure early. ˆη crosses one where convergence stops, and the test replaces seconds of iterating without progress with a rejection that arrives within milliseconds. The test has limits. Beyond κu 32 ≈ 1, the classical bound no longer implies contraction for the FP32 child (Section 4.3). In our runs, the child’s first correction is accepted and its second is rejected. With damping, its corrections pass the test without making progress. A rejection does not show that contraction is impossible, and the FP64 fallback child converges at every tested κ. A rejection also does not say which hypothesis failed. The cause can be an inaccurate child, a lossy conversion, or cancellation in the residual. In the other direction, a few accepted corrections do not prove a contraction bound for all inputs. Checking each correction separately also does not control the quality of the basis that an outer Krylov method builds from those corrections. We did not measure the conditioning of that basis or its loss of orthogonality, and we make no claim about either. Theorems 3 and 4 extend the per-edge results to whole trees. Every output that the root reports as successful satisfies a backward-error bound set by the stopping tolerance and the root precision, with no assumptions on the subtree. Convergence follows from one sufficient condition per edge. Section 4.7 lists the theoretical questions that remain open. 

Limitations and open experiments. Several experiments are still missing. 40 CAMPS: Composable Mixed-Precision Adapters • A control for the nested trees. We did not run the cascade’s tree entirely in FP64. That control would show how much of its speedup on nonsymmetric problems comes from lower precision and how much comes from nesting. • F3R. Our implementation uses Jacobi where the authors’ code uses block ILU(0). We have not run the authors’ public code on our matrices to verify our implementation, and we have not compared at their problem scales. • Children. We did not test a child that stops at the parent’s tolerance, a child that keeps its Krylov space between corrections, or a short-budget CG child on which the residual and energy tests disagree. • The energy test. Its error estimate is not a proven enclosure (Section 4.3). A rigorous version needs verified bounds on each rounding error and new timings. • Baselines. The missing baselines include optimized algebraic multigrid, incomplete factorizations, recycled Krylov methods, FGMRES with longer restarts, and block FGMRES beyond 16 right-hand sides. • Problems and hardware. Our sparse matrices are stencils whose entries FP16 stores without error. A broader study would use SuiteSparse matrices with irregular sparsity, anisotropy, and nonnormality [48, 75], larger sparse problems, several architectures with different FP64 speeds, and performance profiles across matrix families [51]. • Protocol. The diagnostic products should be separated from the timed work, the CSR indices could be stored in 32 bits, and timings should be reported as distributions over independent matrices. 

Choosing a tree automatically. We chose every tree in this paper by hand, guided by measurements. Choosing the tree from features of the problem and the hardware, and choosing the precision, budget, damping, and fallback during a solve, is a solver-selection problem in the sense of Rice [148]. Learned comparators [98] and bandit-based precision selection [32] are the natural baselines. We plan to study a reinforcement-learning policy for these choices. The acceptance test would check every step that such a policy takes, independently of how the policy chose it.

A.6 Software and verification Core package. The core package has about 1,700 lines and separates five concerns. • Precision specifications record the unit roundoff, range, subnormal threshold, and accumulation format of each storage format. Every conversion is explicit, and an operator or solver node rejects an input of the wrong format instead of casting it. • Operators carry their format and symmetry. For the energy test they also provide the entrywise absolute product 

|A| | x|.• The adapter implements Algorithm 1 step by step. This covers the stored accepted state, power-of-two scaling and casting, the zero-start child call, promotion, optional damping, the recomputed residual of the committed iterate, the residual energy acceptance test, rollback, the fallback pair, stagnation detection, and the attempt budget. For every attempt it writes (ρ, ρ ′, α, c∆ϕ, E ) and the outcome to a contract record. • The residual contract and scaling carry the comparison tolerance and report every conversion. • The solver nodes are FGMRES with stored directions and FP64 restart residuals, GMRES, CG, dense LU, stored approximate inverses, and Jacobi. 

Backend settings. A diagnostics module fixes the backend settings. TF32 and reduced-precision reductions are turned off, so FP32 means IEEE FP32 multiplies. FP16 and BF16 matrix products accumulate in FP32, as the platform specifies. Norms and Krylov scalars over half-precision vectors also accumulate in FP32. We record each of these as an explicit promotion, since the storage precision of a vector is not the same as the precision of the arithmetic on it. Dense operators are stored matrices. Sparse operators are in CSR format, with native matrix–vector products in all four storage formats. Krylov scalars are computed on the host, and this overhead is the same for every method in every comparison. 

Experiment layer. A further 1,800 lines contain the matrix generators, the counting operators, the named instances and baselines, and the measurement harness. The generators build dense U ΣV T matrices with a chosen κ2 and the 3D Poisson and convection–diffusion stencils. The counting operators record every product by storage format. The harness handles warmups, medians with interquartile ranges, separate setup timing, residuals recomputed in FP64, normwise and componentwise backward errors, analytic and measured memory, and an environment record in every result file. The verification scripts add another 850 lines. Dense LU nodes use partial pivoting without pivot modification. A zero-pivot boost is kept only for incomplete factorizations, for the reason given in Section 5.4. 

Determinism. Every result file records the GPU, driver, CUDA and PyTorch versions, and backend flags under which it was produced, and every problem is generated from a fixed seed. On the same GPU, reruns reproduce iteration counts, acceptance decisions, and residuals bit for bit. We confirmed this for the fallback, damped-fallback, and ladder rows of Section 5.4 and for the sweep of Section 5.9 after every revision of the adapter. 

Verification suite. The repository has a one-command verification suite. It reruns the check of Theorem 4, the energy contract with its counterexample, the fallback and ladder rows, and a reduced-size pass over all seven experiments. It then writes a report that compares the outcome with the archived results. On a different GPU, the report requires three things. Exit statuses, attempt counts, and the split between accepted and rejected corrections must agree. Every rigorous bound must hold. Residuals and contraction factors must agree within a factor of ten. Timings are reported but not checked, because they depend on the hardware. Dense matrices. A dense test matrix has no zero entries and no symmetry, and its entries are of order 1/√n. The one property we control is the condition number, which we set through the singular values. The standard method for a dense system is LU factorization. It costs O(n3) operations, and each later triangular solve or matrix-vector product costs 

O(n2). CAMPS-Direct and CAMPS-Krylov use this difference. They compute the factorization in FP32 and keep the cheaper residual in FP64. The condition number decides whether the FP32 factors are accurate enough (Section 5.4). When they are not, the fallback child and CAMPS-Ladder switch to an FP64 factorization. 

Poisson. The Poisson matrix is sparse, symmetric, and positive definite. For a grid of g3 points, each row has at most seven nonzero entries, which lie on the diagonal and at distances 1, g, and g2 from it. A matrix-vector product costs 

O(n) operations, so no factorization is needed. Symmetry and positive definiteness allow CG, which stores a fixed number of vectors. CAMPS-Direct with a CG 32 child uses this structure (Section 5.5). The entries are 6 and −1, and FP32 and FP16 store both without error. 

Convection–diffusion. The convection–diffusion matrix has the same sparsity pattern as the Poisson matrix. The entry next to the diagonal is −1 − w on one side and −1 on the other, so the matrix is not symmetric and CG does not apply. GMRES and FGMRES apply, but they store a Krylov basis and orthogonalize against it, and that work grows with the number of iterations. CAMPS-Cascade and CAMPS-Apex use the sparsity to move most of the matrix-vector products and most of the Krylov iterations to FP32 and FP16 (Sections 5.6 and 5.8). For the wind strengths we test, the entries are small integers, and FP16 stores them without error.

A.10 Residual and energy contracts on SPD problems 

The Euclidean acceptance test can reject valid CG steps that lower the energy norm of the error while the residual grows (Section 4.3). On the reported runs at 32 3 and 48 3, every correction was accepted under both the residual contract and the energy contract on the committed displacement. Both contracts gave identical iterates and residuals, with three corrections each, because the child ran to its own tolerance before returning. The measured energy decrease exceeded its error estimate (4) by factors between 10 6 and 5 × 10 10 .The energy test needs three extra products per correction. In paired runs these cost 12 –27% of the solve time on the laptop GPU and 2% on the A100. The energy contract therefore did not screen out any CG progress here. A short-budget CG child on which the two contracts disagree would show whether the energy contract has a real advantage. Section 6 lists that test as open. 56
