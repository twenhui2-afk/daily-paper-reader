Abstract 

Character-level OCR for low-resource Manchu often produces near-miss spelling errors. Lexicon-constrained decoding can correct such errors with lexical knowledge, but a fixed dic-tionary cannot cover the many unseen word forms produced by agglutinative word forma-tion. We study whether morphological ex-pansion alleviates this limitation, and how the expansion size and confidence fallback affect recognition. We segment the training transcrip-tions with a dictionary-based stem list, extract roots and suffix chains from them, generate about 510k candidates, select the expansion size through frequency ranking on the valida-tion set, and decompose accuracy under hard lexicon constraints into the product of coverage and in-lexicon precision. Across three CRNN training seeds, adding 250k candidates to a base dictionary of about 29k words raises the type accuracy on unseen combinations of seen roots from 63.6% to 86.3%, and overall token accu-racy from 82.4% to 84.2%. Adding all candi-dates lowers overall token accuracy to 81.5%, showing that the coverage gain comes with a loss of in-lexicon precision. A further compar-ison shows that the base dictionary with con-fidence fallback performs best overall among the CRNN systems tested, reaching 76.1% type and 85.5% token accuracy and outperforming the expanded lexicon both with and without fallback. A Transformer recognizer shows sim-ilar trends. These results indicate that morpho-logical expansion relieves the constraint of a fixed dictionary, but a larger lexicon does not guarantee better recognition; in the present low-resource Manchu OCR setting, a selective com-bination of reliable lexical constraints and open recognition is more effective. 

1 Introduction 

Large collections of Manchu archives and books record the history, administration and social life

of the Qing dynasty, but the text in their scanned images must be converted into searchable and col-latable text before it can support further organiza-tion and research. Optical character recognition (OCR) is the basis of this process. The vertical, cursive script of Manchu and the limited amount of annotated data make reliable recognition diffi-cult. Under low-resource conditions, the outputs of character-level recognizers are often close to the correct answer but contain a few spelling errors: on our test set, 73–78% of the greedy errors on the two main generalization tiers are a single edit away from the correct word. How to bring in usable lin-guistic knowledge beyond the limited training data is therefore an important question for improving Manchu OCR. Lexicon-constrained decoding is a direct way to use such knowledge. Restricting decoding to a lexicon rules out some implausible strings and lets the system reselect among candidate words on the basis of image evidence. The lexicon thus pro-vides an explicit lexical prior, so that recognition no longer relies entirely on character-by-character prediction. The constraint, however, has a clear boundary: the system can output the correct word only if the lexicon already contains it. This boundary is particularly pronounced in ag-glutinative languages such as Manchu. Manchu builds different word forms by attaching one or more suffixes to a stem, and some suffixes vary with the vowel-harmony class of the stem. For example, the perfective participle suffix has the forms -ha , -he and -ho : ara- ‘write’ with -ha gives 

araha ‘wrote’, and gene- ‘go’ with -he gives genehe 

‘went’. araha can be further followed by -ngge , giv-ing arahangge ‘that which was written’. Stems, suf-fix variants and their multi-layer combinations to-gether widen the range of word forms found in real documents (Gorelova, 2002; Möllendorff, 1892). Moreover, some affixes in Qing-dynasty Manchu documents can be written either joined to the stem 1arXiv:2611.23900v1 [cs.CL] 18 Nov 2026 or separately, so morphological membership does not always coincide with written word boundaries. A fixed dictionary can therefore hardly exhaust the word-form combinations in real documents, and its coverage in word-level OCR also depends on the transliteration and segmentation conventions of its entries and of the image labels. In our test set, only 0.8% of word images correspond to a form seen in training. Even when the root has been seen, a new root–suffix combination may be missing from the base dictionary. Based on this observation, we seg-ment the training transcriptions using the base dic-tionary, extract roots and suffix chains from them, and generate and filter candidate word forms, to ex-amine how far morphological expansion can make up for the limited coverage of a fixed dictionary. Does expanding the lexicon, however, necessar-ily keep improving recognition accuracy? New candidates may add correct answers, but they also add word forms that compete with the correct ones. For inputs the base dictionary already covers, more candidates may bring no further benefit and may in-stead lead the recognizer to a wrong result. The cov-erage gain of expansion and the loss of in-lexicon precision therefore need to be measured together. Furthermore, when the correct word is probably not in the lexicon, should the system still enforce the lexicon constraint, or should it retain the ability of the character-level recognizer to output unlisted forms? Together, these two questions determine at what scale, and in what way, lexical knowledge should take part in low-resource OCR. In this work, we address these questions by com-paring morphological expansion with confidence fallback. We first decompose accuracy under hard lexicon constraints exactly into the product of lexi-con coverage and in-lexicon precision, which sepa-rates whether the correct answer is in the candidate space from whether the recognizer can select it from the candidates (Figure 1). Starting from a base lexicon of 29,024 words, we then generate about 510k candidates from the roots and suffix chains in the training data, rank them by frequency and control how many of them enter the lexicon. In parallel, we introduce confidence fallback, which compares the CTC (Graves et al., 2006) scores of the constrained result and the greedy result and adopts the open output when the constrained result lacks sufficient support. 2 Background and Related Work 

Lexicon constraints in text recognition. Word lexicons have long been used to constrain the out-put of handwriting and scene-text recognizers. One line of work restricts CTC decoding to a closed lexicon (Graves et al., 2009), while another keeps decoding open and adds a character or word lan-guage model through prefix beam search (Hannun et al., 2014). Attention-based recognizers such as TrOCR (Li et al., 2023b) and PARSeq (Bautista and Atienza, 2022) learn an implicit language model from their training text, which is of limited help when most test words are unseen. Our work com-pares closed and open decoding in such a setting and quantifies where a closed lexicon loses accu-racy. 

Manchu and related scripts. Earlier work on Manchu OCR has addressed character seg-mentation and recognition (Xu et al., 2016), segmentation-free recognition of printed words with synthetic data and convolutional features (Huang et al., 2017; Zheng et al., 2018), handwrit-ten word recognition with pretrained and Trans-former models (Li et al., 2023a; Guo et al., 2025), and the restoration of damaged word images (Bi et al., 2026). Zhang et al. (2005) used language-model post-processing for handwritten Manchu characters. For traditional Mongolian, a related ver-tical script, sub-word units (Fan et al., 2019) and multi-task learning (Wei et al., 2021) have been applied to word recognition. More broadly, Rijh-wani et al. (2020) showed that post-correction can substantially reduce OCR errors on endangered-language texts. None of these studies separates lexicon coverage from in-lexicon precision or com-pares lexicon expansion with fallback to open de-coding. 

Open vocabularies and morphology. Text mod-els commonly handle open vocabularies through sub-word units (Sennrich et al., 2016) or unsu-pervised morphological segmentation (Creutz and Lagus, 2007). We instead generate whole-word candidates from roots and suffix chains. This choice keeps decoding at the word level, where near misses can be corrected, and makes lexicon coverage directly measurable. 

3 Method 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

Given the image of a single word, a recognizer trained with CTC produces a distribution over char-acter sequences. Greedy decoding takes the most likely label in each frame, and open beam search explores several character sequences, optionally scored by a character language model. Lexicon-constrained beam search instead keeps only hy-potheses that are prefixes of words in a lexicon V ,so that the output is always an element of V . We are interested in how the choice of V , and the deci-sion whether to constrain at all, affects recognition accuracy.

Coverage is a property of the lexicon and the test labels alone, whereas preci-sion depends on the recognizer and on how many plausible competitors the lexicon offers for each correct word. The decomposition therefore pre-dicts that changing the recognizer leaves coverage unchanged, and that enlarging the lexicon can raise coverage while lowering precision. We test both predictions in §6. 

3.3 Lexicon Expansion 

Our base lexicon is the word list of a dictionary-based morphological analyzer, which we refer to as the dictionary . It contains 29,024 entries: 23,277 derived from external dictionary resources, includ-ing the headwords of the electronic New Manchu– Chinese Dictionary (Hu, 2020), and 5,747 word forms added from an external corpus of Manchu archival texts. To cover word forms the dictionary lacks, we segment the training words into roots and suffix chains with the analyzer, collect 2,879 roots and 181 suffix chains, and pair every root with every chain. The 521,099 pairs yield 508,588 distinct surface forms; together with 1,574 training word forms that concatenation does not produce, this gives 510,162 candidates. We score each can-didate as P (root ) P (chain ), with both probabilities estimated from frequencies in the training images, and add the N highest-scoring candidates to the dictionary. We select N on the validation set (§4), which gives N ∗ = 250 ,000 ; adding all candidates gives the full union of 530,025 words. 

3.4 Confidence Fallback 

Fallback obtains coverage without changing the lexicon. Each image is decoded twice, once with lexicon-constrained beam search and once greedily. If the score of the best constrained hypothesis falls more than a threshold τ below the score of the greedy string, the system outputs the greedy string, and otherwise the constrained one. The output can only change when the greedy string lies outside the lexicon, so the rule effectively acts as a detector for inputs the lexicon does not cover. We select τ on the validation set.

Manchu experts segmented the word images, sup-plied the transliterations and reviewed their quality; ambiguous samples were resolved through review, and samples that could not be identified reliably were excluded. The training set contains 55,240 im-ages of 6,485 word types, the validation set 4,767 images, and the test set 5,389 images of 792 word types. All four sources occur in every split, since the split holds out word forms rather than source documents. 

Test tiers. To measure generalization beyond the training vocabulary, we assign each test word to one of four tiers according to its morphological analysis (Table 1). Tier A contains words that occur in training. Tier B contains words whose root occurs in training but whose root–suffix combination does not; within B, we further distinguish forms whose suffix chain was seen in training (B1) from forms with an unseen chain (B2). Tier C contains words whose root never occurs in training, and words that the segmenter cannot analyse form a separate unresolved tier. Tiers B and C account for 95% of the test images, and we report results for all tiers as well as for the full test set. 

Recognizers. Our main recognizer is a CRNN trained with CTC (Shi et al., 2017) with 14.3M parameters. To test whether our findings depend on the architecture, we also train a Transformer recognizer with 19.6M parameters. Both models are trained for 30 epochs with a batch size of 384 using AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 10 −3, weight decay of 10 −4, one warm-up epoch, cosine decay to 2% of the peak rate, gradient clipping at 5.0 and bf16 precision, without data augmentation. We keep the check-point with the best validation type accuracy. We train the CRNN with three seeds (42, 43 and 44) and report the mean and sample standard deviation; 4the Transformer is trained with one seed. Several analyses in §6 use the original model , an earlier exploratory CRNN run with the same architecture and hyperparameters that was trained with data parallelism on three GPUs (128 images per GPU, the same effective batch size); all decoding experi-ments were first run on this model, so its numbers differ slightly from the three-seed means in Table 2. 

Decoding. All beam searches use a beam width of 50; on tiers B and C, widths of 10, 50 and 100 change accuracy by at most 0.1 points. For open beam search, we optionally add a character trigram language model trained on the training transcrip-tions, with weight α = 0 .25 and length bonus 

β = 0 .25 (Hannun et al., 2014). We also test two variants of the full union: a conservative vowel-harmony filter that removes 40,439 candidates, and a soft frequency prior added to the beam score with weight λ = 0 .01 . All decoding hyperparameters (N , τ , α, β and λ) are selected on validation scores only, as are the recognizer checkpoints. The test set is not entirely untouched, however: it was decoded in earlier exploratory experiments, and accuracy on tier A was logged during training. 

Evaluation. We report two exact-match metrics. Token accuracy is the fraction of test images whose word is recognized correctly. Type accuracy aver-ages the per-type image accuracy over word types with equal weight, so that frequent words do not dominate the score. We compute 95% confidence intervals with a cluster bootstrap over word types (10,000 resamples, keeping all images of a type together; Efron and Tibshirani, 1994), and com-pare systems with exact McNemar tests (McNe-mar, 1947) and cluster sign-flip tests with Holm correction (Holm, 1979). 

5 Main Results 

Table 2 summarizes the results of all systems. We organize the discussion around the three groups of systems in the table. 

Open versus constrained decoding. Greedy CTC decoding reaches 74.0% type and 79.8% to-ken accuracy over all images. Its errors are typi-cally near misses: among erroneous greedy outputs on tiers B and C, 73–78% are one edit away from the gold word and 64–81% are not valid words (Ap-pendix Figure 4). A character language model cor-rects few of them, raising token accuracy by only 0.3 points. Constraining the output to the dictionary has a very different effect on the two tiers. On tier C, token accuracy increases from 77.3% to 88.0%, because the dictionary covers most of these unseen-root words and corrects their near misses. On tier B, by contrast, type accuracy drops to 63.6%, since the dictionary contains only 68.5% of the tier-B word types. Once decoding is constrained, the lexi-con rather than the recognizer limits accuracy. 

Lexicon expansion. Adding the N ∗ highest-ranked combinations to the dictionary resolves the coverage problem on tier B, where type accuracy rises from 63.6% to 86.3%. The same expansion brings little benefit on tier C, where generated can-didates cannot reproduce unseen roots: token accu-racy falls slightly from 88.0% to 86.8%, and drops to 81.5% when all 510k candidates are added. Nei-ther the vowel-harmony filter nor the soft frequency prior changes this outcome. Over all images, N ∗

improves token accuracy by 1.8 points over the dictionary, but its type accuracy (66.5%) remains well below that of greedy decoding, because the unresolved words and part of tier C stay outside the lexicon. 

Confidence fallback. Adding fallback to the dic-tionary gives the best CRNN system on both met-rics, with 76.1% type and 85.5% token accuracy over all images. The gain is concentrated on tier C, where type accuracy reaches 83.8%, compared with 78.1% for greedy decoding and 75.0% for the dictionary alone, while tier-B token accuracy matches that of N ∗. On the original model, fall-back improves the dictionary by 19.0 type points (95% CI [16.2, 21.9]) and 3.0 token points ([1.3, 5.4]). In contrast, combining fallback with N ∗ is less effective and lowers tier-C token accuracy by 5.6 points relative to N ∗ alone; we analyse the reason in §6.5. 

Transformer recognizer. The Transformer is weaker than the CRNN overall, with 71.9% type and 74.6% token accuracy under greedy decoding, but the relative ordering of the decoding strategies is largely the same. The dictionary with fallback again achieves the highest type accuracy (75.4%), and its token accuracy is within 0.1 points of N ∗.

6.1 Coverage and Precision Are Separable 

The decomposition predicts that coverage depends only on the lexicon, whereas precision also depends on the recognizer. To test this, we train CRNNs on nested subsets containing 25%, 50% and 100% of the training images and decode them with the same lexicons. As expected, coverage is identical across the three models for every lexicon and tier. In-lexicon precision, in contrast, increases with the amount of training data for every lexicon (Table 3). Larger lexicons have lower precision at every train-ing size, but the gap between the dictionary and N ∗

narrows from 6.5 to 3.9 points as the training data grows. A stronger recognizer is therefore better at rejecting the additional competitors, which makes lexicon expansion more attractive as recognition quality improves. Across the 14 tier-B and tier-C lexicon config-urations of the original model, type accuracy lies close to a line through the origin with a slope of 0.895 ( R2 = 0 .989 ; Appendix Figure 3). Since these points share the same model and test images, we treat the fit as descriptive. It nevertheless illus-trates that, within this range of lexicons, differences in coverage account for most of the differences in accuracy. 

6.2 Sources of Coverage and the Choice of N

Generated combinations and the dictionary cover largely different words. The combinations cover 96.7% of tier-B types but only 19.7% of tier-C types, whose roots are unseen by definition, while the dictionary covers 68.5% and 82.2%, respec-tively. Together they cover 97.6% of tier B and 87.1% of tier C. Figure 2 shows how accuracy changes as more ranked combinations are added. On the validation set, type accuracy increases steadily up to N ∗ = 250 k, whereas adding all 510k candidates lowers token accuracy from 84.0% to 82.4%. On the test set, the same expansion helps tier B and hurts tier C.

6.4 The Precision Cost of Expansion 

The cost of expansion is most visible on tier C, where the dictionary already covers 97.6% of the images. For these words, generated candidates add competitors rather than answers, and token accu-racy falls from 88.0% with the dictionary to 81.5% with the full union. One expla-nation is that frequency-ranked candidates more closely resemble real word forms and are there-fore harder for the recognizer to reject. 6.5 Why Does Fallback Work? 

Since fallback can only change the output when the greedy string lies outside the lexicon, its ef-fect depends on how well it identifies uncovered inputs. Table 5 analyses this routing decision on the original model. With the dictionary, fallback sends 76.1% of the uncovered images to greedy decoding and misroutes only 3.4% of the covered images. With N ∗, the misrouting rate doubles to 7.7%. In a 250k-word lexicon, an incorrect greedy string is more likely to be a valid entry by chance, which makes the score difference less informative and explains why fallback lowers the accuracy of 

N ∗ on tier C. To separate the quality of the routing rule from the choice of lexicon, we also evaluate oracle rout-ing, which uses the constrained output whenever the gold word is in the lexicon and the greedy out-7put otherwise. Even under oracle routing, the dic-tionary reaches a higher token accuracy than N ∗

(88.3% vs. 87.1%). In other words, once uncovered words can be passed to the open decoder, the ad-ditional coverage of a generated lexicon no longer compensates for its lower precision. The remaining 2.3-point gap between actual and oracle routing for the dictionary indicates how much a better routing rule could still gain. 

7 Conclusion 

We studied how the lexicon affects constrained decoding for low-resource Manchu OCR, where almost all test words are unseen in training. By decomposing accuracy into lexicon coverage and in-lexicon precision, we showed that coverage is a property of the lexicon alone, while precision depends on both the recognizer and the size of the lexicon. Expanding the dictionary with ranked root– suffix combinations raises coverage substantially, with suffix-chain frequency as the key ranking sig-nal, but it lowers precision where the dictionary already covers most words. Confidence fallback provides the missing coverage at a much smaller cost, and a dictionary with fallback outperforms a generated lexicon ten times its size, even under oracle routing. We therefore recommend a precise lexicon combined with a validated fallback as the default for agglutinative low-resource scripts, with lexicon expansion preferred only when the recog-nizer is strong enough to keep its precision cost small. In future work, we plan to test whether these findings extend to other agglutinative scripts such as traditional Mongolian. 

Limitations 

Our study covers a single language and script, and we have not verified that the findings hold for other agglutinative scripts. The Transformer and the training-fraction models were trained with one seed each, so their results do not reflect seed variance. The character language model is a trigram trained only on training transcriptions, and a stronger lan-guage model could narrow the gap between open and constrained decoding. The fallback threshold was selected from a coarse grid of six values, and the oracle analysis covers token accuracy of the original model only. The three newly trained seeds use a different batch configuration from the original model. Our explanation of the precision cost rests on weak correlational evidence. Finally, the test tiers depend on a dictionary-based segmenter that cannot analyse 158 of the test types. Because the split holds out word forms rather than documents, test images share publication sources, and possibly pages, with training images; our results therefore measure generalization to unseen word forms, not to unseen collections or writers. 

Ethics Statement 

This work is intended to support the retrieval, tran-scription and collation of historical Manchu docu-ments. The images are taken from previously pub-lished historical materials (Appendix A), which may contain perspectives specific to their period. OCR outputs should not be treated as authoritative transcriptions: when they are used for archival or-ganization or collation, researchers with expertise in Manchu should review them against the origi-nals, especially historical spellings, word bound-aries and out-of-lexicon outputs. Word segmenta-tion, transliteration and quality review were carried out by Manchu experts, all adult researchers who were informed of the purpose and intended use of the data and participated voluntarily; the protocol received institutional ethics approval. Copyright-restricted source scans and image derivatives will not be redistributed. We plan to release code un-der the Apache 2.0 license and research-created transliterations, splits and metadata under CC BY 4.0, subject to the rights of the source materials.
