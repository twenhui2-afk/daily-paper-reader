## CariesBench: A Hierarchical Multi-view Benchmark for Dental Caries Screening and Diagnostic Reasoning from 3D Intraoral Scans

Abstract 

Dental caries assessment is a structured clinical pro-cess requiring lesion localization, identification of the af-fected tooth and surface, and severity grading rather than simple image-level recognition. 3D intraoral scans (IOS) preserve detailed dental geometry and surface morphology and support standardized rendering from complementary viewpoints, enabling fine-grained visual assessment while introducing a distinct need for anatomically grounded multi-view reasoning. Despite recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and dental vision-language benchmarks, existing evaluations do not jointly assess hi-erarchical arch-to-tooth-to-surface reasoning, cross-view evidence integration, and surface-level caries assessment. To address this gap, we introduce CariesBench, a hierarchi-cal benchmark constructed from 5,932 3D IOS, comprising 85,455 standardized multi-view renderings and 133,994 clin-ically grounded visual question answering instances with expert quality control. CariesBench comprises three comple-mentary task families: Arch-level Clinical Screening (ACS), Tooth-level Diagnostic Reasoning (TDR), and Structured Clinical Decision-Making (SCD). Using the CariesBench training set, we further fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct to obtain IntraoralGPT. Extensive evaluation of 24 represen-tative baseline MLLMs reveals a substantial benchmark-level performance disparity between arch-level screening and tooth-level diagnostic reasoning, with higher accuracy on ACS than on TDR. CariesBench-specific supervision sub-stantially improves the Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct baseline, in-creasing the 19-task macro-average clinical accuracy from 52.7% to 69.5% and TDR Overall from 34.4% to 44.7%. Despite these gains, fine-grained TDR remains challenging, with controlled ablations revealing task-dependent benefits from complementary visual observations. 1. Introduction 

Dental caries is a common oral disease whose clinical management depends on accurate lesion localization and severity assessment. Caries diagnosis is therefore not merely a binary recognition task. Clinicians must identify the af-fected tooth and surface and grade lesion severity accord-ing to standardized diagnostic criteria. The International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) [10, 19], for example, defines caries status at the tooth-surface level and stratifies lesion severity according to visible structural changes. Reliable caries assessment therefore requires vi-sual evidence to be anatomically localized and interpreted at the appropriate clinical severity, making the task inherently tooth-specific, surface-aware, and fine-grained. 3D intraoral scans (IOS) provide a particularly informa-tive representation for such assessment. By preserving de-tailed dental geometry and surface morphology, IOS enable individual teeth and surfaces to be examined from comple-mentary viewpoints, with previous clinical studies demon-strating their potential for detecting and classifying carious lesions at tooth and surface levels [11, 29]. However, this richer representation also introduces a distinct reasoning challenge: clinically relevant evidence may be distributed across complementary views of the same anatomy. Reli-able interpretation therefore requires localizing findings to the correct tooth and surface, maintaining anatomical cor-respondence, and integrating evidence across views before severity assessment. IOS-based caries assessment is there-fore not merely a single-image recognition problem, but an 

anatomically grounded multi-view reasoning problem. Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in medical visual understanding and reasoning across diverse imaging modalities and clinical tasks [17, 18, 20, 23], motivating growing research interest in dentistry. Traditional resources such as DENTEX [14] primarily fo-cus on recognition or localization from 2D dental images, whereas recent vision-language benchmarks extend to dental VQA [21], arch- and tooth-level understanding [15], mul-timodal dental reasoning [28], and native 3D IOS model-ing [38]. Nevertheless, these benchmarks individually cover only subsets of the combined capabilities targeted by IOS-based caries assessment. As summarized in Table 1, none of the compared resources jointly evaluates hierarchical arch-to-tooth-to-surface reasoning, anatomically corresponding multi-view evidence integration, and surface-level caries assessment within a unified benchmark. Consequently, it re-mains unclear whether current MLLMs can maintain anatom-ical correspondence and integrate complementary evidence as diagnosis progresses from global screening to localized tooth- and surface-level assessment. To address this gap, we introduce CariesBench, a hierar-chical benchmark for evaluating clinically grounded multi-view reasoning over observations derived from 3D IOS. Con-structed from 5,932 3D IOS, CariesBench comprises 85,455 standardized multi-view 2D renderings and 133,994 clini-cally grounded VQA instances. A stratified 20% subset of benchmark instances underwent expert validation by two dentists with 8 and 3 years of clinical experience, respec-tively, under a standardized protocol designed and super-vised by a senior dentist with 15 years of clinical experience; disagreements were resolved through adjudication under se-nior clinical supervision. Unlike conventional image-level VQA, CariesBench derives complementary observations from the same underlying 3D anatomy, thereby preserv-ing correspondence across views. Evaluation spans three complementary task families: Arch-level Clinical Screen-ing (ACS) for global dentition-level understanding, Tooth-level Diagnostic Reasoning (TDR) for localized reasoning over individual teeth and surfaces, and Structured Clinical Decision-Making (SCD) for objectively scored clinical deci-sions across anatomical granularities. This hierarchy enables systematic evaluation of MLLMs as the diagnostic scope 2narrows from the entire arch to individual teeth and surfaces. Extensive evaluation of 24 representative baseline MLLMs reveals a substantial benchmark-level performance disparity between arch-level screening and localized tooth-level diagnostic tasks. Across model families, performance is consistently higher on arch-level screening than on local-ized tooth-level diagnosis, indicating an observed global-to-local performance disparity across the evaluated tasks. The lowest performance is observed on TDR tasks involv-ing fine-grained anatomical localization and surface-level clinical-status prediction. For example, GPT-5.5 achieves 76.9% on Sound Tooth Identification and 65.9% on Tooth Enumeration in ACS, but only 31.8% on Surface Analy-sis and 30.5% on Full Tooth Scan in TDR. These results highlight the difficulty of localized diagnosis despite strong arch-level performance. To investigate whether these limitations can be miti-gated through benchmark-specific supervision, we fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct on the CariesBench training set to obtain IntraoralGPT. Under the same patient-level held-out protocol, IntraoralGPT improves the 19-task macro-average clinical accuracy of Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct from 52.7% to 69.5% (+16.8 points). On TDR, its overall accuracy in-creases from 34.4% to 44.7%, with Surface Analysis improv-ing from 24.3% to 30.6% and Full Tooth Scan from 23.0% to 29.1%. These gains demonstrate the value of CariesBench-specific supervision for hierarchical intraoral reasoning; how-ever, the resulting 44.7% TDR Overall remains slightly be-low the strongest zero-shot baseline of 45.9%, underscoring the continued difficulty of fine-grained anatomical localiza-tion and cross-view evidence integration. To summarize, our contributions are threefold: 1) We introduce CariesBench, a large-scale benchmark built from 5,932 3D intraoral scans, 85,455 standardized multi-view renderings, and 133,994 clinically grounded VQA instances, enabling hierarchical evaluation from arch-level screening to tooth- and surface-level reasoning over anatomically corresponding views. 2) We comprehensively benchmark 24 representative MLLMs and identify a substantial performance disparity between arch-level screening and tooth-level diagnostic tasks, while providing controlled within-task evidence on the task-dependent contribution of complementary visual observations. 3) We introduce IntraoralGPT, obtained by fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct with CariesBench supervision, and demonstrate substantial improvements in tooth-level multi-view reasoning and structured clinical decision-making. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. General Medical Multimodal Benchmarks 

General medical multimodal benchmarks have estab-lished visual question answering as a widely used paradigm for evaluating medical image understanding. PathVQA [17] focuses on pathology images with open-ended questions grounded in pathological findings, whereas OmniMed-VQA [18] broadens evaluation across diverse imaging modal-ities and anatomical regions. These benchmarks provide important testbeds for medical visual understanding, but their evaluation is primarily organized around individual image–question pairs rather than anatomically linked multi-view observations. They therefore do not explicitly capture the hierarchical anatomical structure or cross-view evidence dependencies required for dental diagnosis, in which assess-ment may progress from the entire dentition to individual teeth and specific tooth surfaces. 

2.2. Dental Multimodal Benchmarks 

Dental benchmarks have progressively evolved from 2D pathology recognition and localization to 3D anatom-ical modeling and vision-language reasoning. Tradi-tional datasets such as DENTEX [14], CariesDet [34], Al-phaDent [33], and DentalAI [36] primarily support recogni-tion, localization, or segmentation of dental abnormalities from panoramic radiographs, bitewing X-rays, or intrao-ral photographs, while Caries-DETR [25] further investi-gates DETR-based caries lesion detection in 2D RGB in-traoral photographs. In parallel, 3DTeethSeg [5] and Tooth-Fairy2 [6] introduce native 3D IOS or CBCT data, but pri-marily target anatomical segmentation rather than clinical vision-language reasoning. More recent dental vision-language benchmarks extend evaluation toward clinical question answering and increas-ingly diverse multimodal reasoning settings. MetaDent [21] introduces dental VQA over 2D dental images; MMOral-OPG [15] focuses on panoramic X-ray understanding; MMOral-Uni [16] further extends evaluation across five den-tal imaging modalities and five task types; DentVLM [28] broadens dental reasoning across seven imaging modalities and 36 clinical tasks; and IOSVLM [38], the closest setting to ours, investigates generative VQA directly over native 3D IOS. However, as summarized in Table 1, these benchmarks cover complementary aspects of dental multimodal under-standing, but none of the compared resources jointly eval-uates arch-, tooth-, and surface-level reasoning, multi-view evidence integration, and surface-level caries assessment. CariesBench targets this missing intersection by com-bining hierarchical anatomical evaluation with multi-view observations derived from the same underlying 3D anatomy. 3. Construction of CariesBench 

3.1. Benchmark Overview 

Figure 1 summarizes the construction of CariesBench, which converts 3D IOS into standardized arch- and tooth-level multi-view 2D representations for existing MLLMs. Based on these representations, CariesBench organizes evaluation into three complementary task families: Arch-level Clinical Screening (ACS) for global understanding of the dentition, Tooth-level Diagnostic Reasoning (TDR) for fine-grained reasoning over individual teeth and their surfaces, and Structured Clinical Decision-Making (SCD) for objectively scored clinical decisions across anatomical granularities. Together, these task families form a hierarchi-cal evaluation from global arch-level screening to localized tooth- and surface-level reasoning and structured clinical decision-making. The 5,932 IOS were obtained from 2,966 patients, each contributing one maxillary and one mandibular scan. Using a fixed patient-level split, the 2,966 patients were partitioned into 2,372/297/297 patients for training, validation, and test-ing, respectively. All IOS and their derived arch-level render-ings, tooth-level views, surface observations, and VQA in-stances from the same patient were confined to the same split before downstream VQA sampling or training-data rebal-ancing. This patient-disjoint partition prevents patient-level information leakage across training and evaluation sets. 

3.2. 3D-to-2D Multi-view Representation 

Arch-level Multi-view Rendering. Each complete max-illary or mandibular IOS is rendered into six standardized views covering complementary anatomical directions. Max-illary scans include right buccal, frontal, left buccal, right palatal, occlusal, and left palatal views. Mandibular scans in-clude right lingual, occlusal, left lingual, right buccal, frontal, and left buccal views. The six views are arranged in a fixed arch-specific 2 × 3 layout, providing consistent anatomical orientation and complementary observations of the same dental arch. 

Tooth-level Multi-view Rendering. Each complete IOS is first segmented using the pretrained 3DTeethSAM [27] model, and each segmented tooth is rendered into a stan-dardized multi-view panel. Panel 1 provides an overview of the target tooth, followed by anatomically defined surface-specific views. Incisors contain labial, mesial, inner, distal, and incisal views; posterior teeth contain buccal, mesial, inner, distal, and occlusal views; and canines use four corre-sponding surface-specific views without an additional incisal or occlusal view. Inner surfaces are designated as palatal for maxillary teeth and lingual for mandibular teeth, while ante-rior and posterior outer surfaces are termed labial and buccal, respectively. Mesial and distal orientations are mirrored be-tween the right and left quadrants to preserve tooth-relative anatomical orientation. 

Anatomical Correspondence. Unlike independently ac-quired 2D dental images, all views associated with a given arch or tooth are rendered from the same underlying 3D anatomical instance. CariesBench therefore preserves ex-4plicit anatomical correspondence across views, enabling eval-uation of whether an MLLM can associate findings with the correct tooth and surface, integrate complementary evidence, and maintain a consistent diagnosis across viewpoints. 

3.3. Modified ICDAS-II-Based Clinical Status Tax-onomy 

Based on the ICDAS-II caries coding scheme, we de-fine a task-oriented clinical status taxonomy for multi-view IOS assessment. ICDAS-II codes 1 and 2 are merged into a unified early-caries category to reduce ambiguity be-tween subtle early-stage manifestations in rendered IOS views, while the remaining caries categories follow the ordinal progression of ICDAS-II codes 3–6. Because restoration-related conditions are also clinically relevant to dental screening, we additionally introduce two cate-gories representing restored teeth without and with sec-ondary caries. The resulting taxonomy contains eight clin-ical states, L ={L0,L1,L2,L3,L4,L5,L6,L7 }. Specifically, L0–L5 denote Sound, Early Caries, Localized Enamel Break-down, Underlying Dark Shadow, Distinct Cavity, and Exten-sive Cavity, corresponding to ICDAS-II codes 0, 1–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively; L6–L7 represent restoration without and with secondary caries and are categorical extensions rather than ordinal severity levels. This eight-state clinical status taxonomy serves as the standardized clinical label space for benchmark construction and evaluation. 

3.4. Hierarchical Clinical Task Construction 

Arch-level Clinical Screening (ACS). ACS evaluates global understanding of a complete dental arch through six task types: Global Screening (GS), Tooth Enumeration (TE), Specific Region (SR), Differential Diagnosis (DD), Severity Comparison (SC), and Sound Tooth Identification (SI). 

Tooth-level Diagnostic Reasoning (TDR). TDR evalu-ates fine-grained reasoning over an individual tooth through Surface Analysis (SA), Full Tooth Scan (FS), and Sound Tooth Verification (SV). These tasks require models to as-sociate observations with the correct anatomical surfaces, integrate complementary views of the same tooth, and de-rive a clinically consistent diagnosis. For reasoning-oriented instances, the diagnostic process follows a five-step progres-sion: View Identification (VI), Morphology Observation (MO), Texture Details (TD), Cross-view Consistency (CC), and Final Diagnosis (FD). 

Structured Clinical Decision-Making (SCD). SCD complements open-ended reasoning with objectively scored true/false (TF) and multiple-choice (MC) questions spanning surface, tooth, and arch granularities. These tasks evaluate caries and restoration verification, severity classification, and identification of clinically relevant surfaces or teeth. 

3.5. VQA Generation and Quality Control 

We construct CariesBench using a structured-annotation-grounded, semi-automated pipeline that explicitly separates structured clinical annotations from subsequent language realization. The structured clinical annotations were derived from epidemiological surveys, oral examinations, and clin-ical data curation at a university-affiliated stomatological hospital. Annotation and data entry were conducted by five postgraduate dental trainees, three standardized-residency trainees, and one attending dentist under senior clinical super-vision. The resulting annotations were organized by patient, tooth, anatomical surface, and clinical status and served as the reference clinical labels for benchmark construction and evaluation. For the open-ended ACS and TDR tasks, GPT-4o was used only for language realization of annotation-grounded questions and responses. Gemini-2.5-Pro subse-quently audited each generated instance for consistency with the structured annotations, anatomical correspondence, and unsupported clinical claims. For SCD, questions and tar-get answers were instantiated directly from the structured annotations rather than generated by an LLM. Across all task families, evaluation targets are derived from the struc-tured clinical annotations rather than from LLM-generated reference prose. Expert validation was further performed on a stratified subset comprising 20% of the full benchmark, covering all clinical-status categories and task families. Two dentists with 8 and 3 years of clinical experience, respectively, re-viewed the selected instances using a standardized five-point clinical evaluation rubric under a protocol designed and su-pervised by a senior dentist with 15 years of clinical expe-rience. Overall response quality was assessed along five dimensions: Tooth/Surface Mapping, Evidence Grounding, Target Completeness, Cross-view Consistency, and Conclu-sion Support. For TDR, the five-step diagnostic explanations were additionally assessed in terms of VI, MO, TD, CC, and FD. Across the reported evaluation criteria, mean expert ratings ranged from 4.26 to 4.95 on the five-point scale. Of the expert-reviewed subset, 800 stratified instances spanning all task families and clinical-status categories were indepen-dently assessed by both dentists for inter-rater reliability analysis. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using linearly weighted Cohen’s κ, yielding κ = 0 .86 . Disagreements were resolved through adjudication and consensus under senior clinical supervision; 5.2% of reviewed instances re-quired adjudication, while 1.8% were excluded because of unsupported visual claims or unresolved clinical ambiguity.

4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setups 

Benchmarked MLLMs. We evaluate 24 representative multimodal large language models (MLLMs) grouped into three categories: commercial, open-source, and medical-specialized models. Commercial models include GPT-5.5 [31], GPT-5.4-Nano [30], GLM-5.2 [41], Claude-Sonnet-5 [2], Claude-Haiku-4.5 [1], Gemini-3.6-Flash [13], and Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview [12]. Open-source mod-els include LLaVA-1.5-7B [24], ShareGPT4V-7B [8], InternVL2-8B [9], InternVL3.5-8B [37], Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [4], Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [3], Qwen3.5-9B [32], DeepSeek-VL-7B [26], Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking [35], and MiMo-VL-7B-RL [40]. Medical-specialized models in-clude HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B [42], LLaVA-Med-7B [20], DentalGPT-Mid [7], HealthGPT-Pro-8B [22], Lingshu-7B [39], OralGPT-Omni-7B [16], and DentVLM [28]. 

Supervised Fine-Tuning. We further fine-tune Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct on the CariesBench training set using LoRA implemented with LLaMA-Factory [43] for two epochs, with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, rank r = 16 , scaling factor 

α = 32 , and BF16 precision. We refer to the resulting model as IntraoralGPT. 

Evaluation Metrics. Consistent with the hierarchical structure of CariesBench, our primary metric is Clinical Ac-curacy, which evaluates structured diagnostic correctness after task-specific response parsing, reducing sensitivity to linguistic variation. For ACS, model responses are parsed according to the expected output structure of each question type, with clinical predictions compared against the corre-sponding structured reference annotations. For TDR, Clini-cal Accuracy is computed at the task-appropriate anatomical granularity: exact clinical-status matching for the queried surface in SA, mean surface-level exact-match accuracy across the target tooth in FS, and binary sound/non-sound correctness in SV. For SCD, true/false responses are eval-uated by binary correctness, whereas multiple-choice re-sponses are evaluated by exact class match. Each task-family Overall is the macro-average of its question types, and Avg. is the macro-average across all 19 question types. 

Implementation Details. For all benchmarked baseline models, we use zero-shot prompting with deterministic de-coding (temperature = 0 .0, top-p = 1 .0, and repetition penalty = 1 .0). Commercial models are evaluated through 6their official APIs using equivalent decoding settings where supported. All models receive the same task-specific instruc-tions and corresponding rendered inputs. Open-source and medical-specialized models are evaluated locally using Py-Torch 2.4.1, Transformers 5.4.0, and CUDA 11.8 with FP16 inference on a server equipped with eight NVIDIA A40 46 GB GPUs. 

4.2. Evaluation Results 

Based on Tables 2 and 3, we summarize four key findings on CariesBench. 

Finding 1. Surface-specific tooth-level reasoning is the most pronounced bottleneck observed on CariesBench. 

The lowest performance is observed on SA and FS, which require fine-grained surface-level clinical-status prediction from tooth-specific visual observations. For example, GPT-5.5 achieves 31.8% on SA and 30.5% on FS, compared with 75.4% on SV. Across all 24 baselines, SA and FS peak at only 31.8% and 30.5%, respectively, whereas SV reaches 75.4%. This contrast suggests that the largest performance degra-dation is associated with tasks requiring surface-specific lo-calization and cross-view integration rather than tooth-level input alone. 

Finding 2. MLLMs exhibit a pronounced benchmark-level global-to-local performance disparity. Across Caries-Bench, models perform consistently worse on TDR than on ACS under the respective task-specific evaluation protocols. Across the 24 baselines, the mean ACS Overall accuracy is 67.3%, compared with 38.2% for TDR Overall, yielding a descriptive benchmark-level performance difference of 29.1 percentage points. Because ACS and TDR differ in anatomi-cal scope, prediction targets, diagnostic complexity, answer formats, and scoring units, this observed difference cannot identify the specific contribution of anatomical granularity, cross-view integration, or other task-related factors. 

Finding 3. Dental or medical specialization does not consistently translate into stronger multi-view intrao-ral reasoning. Under the same zero-shot evaluation proto-col, medical- and dental-specialized models do not consis-tently outperform general-purpose MLLMs on CariesBench. DentVLM, for example, achieves competitive results on TE (69.7%) and FS (26.9%), but its TDR Overall remains 40.8%, below several general-purpose commercial models. OralGPT-Omni-7B and DentalGPT-Mid achieve 22.5% and 30.5% TDR Overall, respectively, whereas GPT-5.5 achieves the strongest zero-shot baseline TDR performance at 45.9%. These results suggest that domain specialization alone is insufficient to ensure strong performance on the multi-view reasoning requirements evaluated by CariesBench, and that targeted supervision explicitly representing anatomical cor-respondence and cross-view evidence may be beneficial. 

Finding 4. CariesBench-specific supervision substan-tially improves hierarchical clinical reasoning. To inves-tigate whether the limitations exposed by CariesBench can be mitigated through benchmark-specific supervision, we compare IntraoralGPT with its Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct base model under the same evaluation protocol. IntraoralGPT improves the 19-task macro-average clinical accuracy from 52.7% to 69.5%, corresponding to a gain of 16.8 percentage points. The gains vary substantially across task groups: ACS improves by 0.4 points, TDR by 10.3 points, SCD-TF by 21.7 points, and SCD-MC by 35.5 points. Within TDR, SA im-proves from 24.3% to 30.6%, while FS improves from 23.0% to 29.1%. These results show that CariesBench-specific su-pervision yields particularly large gains on structured clinical decisions while also improving tooth-level multi-view rea-soning. Nevertheless, IntraoralGPT reaches 44.7% TDR Overall, remaining slightly below the strongest zero-shot baseline, GPT-5.5, at 45.9%, highlighting the remaining dif-ficulty of fine-grained anatomical localization and cross-view evidence integration.

5. Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce CariesBench, a hierarchical benchmark for evaluating clinically grounded multi-view reasoning from 3D intraoral scans in multimodal large lan-guage models (MLLMs). By organizing evaluation into Arch-level Clinical Screening (ACS), Tooth-level Diagnostic Reasoning (TDR), and Structured Clinical Decision-Making (SCD), CariesBench evaluates the progression from global arch-level screening to localized tooth- and surface-level reasoning and structured clinical decisions. Extensive eval-uation of 24 representative MLLMs reveals a pronounced benchmark-level performance disparity between arch-level screening and tooth-level diagnostic tasks. Controlled multi-view ablations further provide task-dependent evidence for the contribution of complementary visual observations to tooth-level diagnosis.
