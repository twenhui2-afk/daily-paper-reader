Abstract: We study equilibrium selection in asymmetric pure coordination games with finitely many players and actions. A logarithmic regularization defines a tracing-type path of interior equilibria from the uniform strategy profile. We reduce the equilibrium conditions to single-peaked scalar functions and show that, when one associated payoff index is uniquely minimal, the connected equilibrium branch from the uniform profile converges to the corresponding coordinated outcome. With two players, this condition is equivalent to maximizing the sum of square-root payoffs, extending the known two-action result to finitely many actions. A three-player example shows that the square-root rule does not generally extend to more players. 

Keywords: coordination games; equilibrium selection; logarithmic regularization; tracing procedure. 

1. Introduction 

Coordination games often have multiple Nash equilibria. Although each equilibrium is self-enforcing, equilibrium conditions alone do not determine which outcome players will coordinate on. Equilibrium selection procedures address this indeterminacy by specifying how a particular equilibrium is selected. Harsanyi’s tracing procedure approaches this problem by following equilibria of auxiliary games from an initial prior toward an equilibrium of the original game (Harsanyi, 1975; Harsanyi and Selten, 1988). Related homotopy methods study equilibrium paths generated by varying a parameter, including paths based on quantal response equilibria (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995; Turocy, 2005; Zhang, 2016; Zhang, Hofbauer, 2016). 

This paper studies a tracing-type selection method for asymmetric pure coordination games with finitely many players and actions. Players receive positive, potentially different payoffs when all choose the same action, and zero payoffs otherwise. We consider a family of games in which each player’s material payoff is combined with a logarithmic regularization term. At the initial parameter value, the unique equilibrium is the uniform strategy profile. As the weight on material payoffs increases, we examine the connected component of the equilibrium graph containing that initial equilibrium. This construction is related to logarithmic tracing (Harsanyi, 1973; Zhang, Hofbauer, 2016), but is distinct from Harsanyi’s original tracing procedure (Harsanyi, 1975). 

Our analysis reduces the equilibrium conditions to a family of single-peaked scalar functions, one for each coordinated outcome. Each function has a maximum that defines an associated payoff index. We show that if one outcome uniquely minimizes this index, the equilibrium component originating at the uniform profile converges to that outcome. For two players, minimizing the index is equivalent to maximizing the sum of square-root payoffs. This extends the two-action result of Zhang and Hofbauer (2016) to any finite number of actions. For three or more players, the square-root rule generally fails, as illustrated by a three-player example. 

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 introduces the logarithmically regularized game and defines the equilibrium component used for selection. Section 3 establishes the selection result, derives the two-player rule, and gives the three-player example. Section 4 discusses the scope of the criterion and possible extensions.

4. Discussion 

This paper gives a selection criterion for asymmetric pure coordination games under logarithmic regularization. The equilibrium graph is traced from the uniform profile, where material payoffs have no weight. When one action uniquely minimizes the index 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖 , the connected component 𝐶𝐶0 converges to coordination on that action. For two players, minimizing 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖  is equivalent to maximizing the sum of square-root payoffs. With three or more players this simplification generally fails, and 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖  rather than the square-root sum determines selection under the unique-minimizer condition. 

The index 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖  is related to, but distinct from, several established equilibrium selection criteria. In asymmetric pure coordination games, an outcome that Pareto dominates every other coordinated outcome necessarily has a strictly smaller index 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖 . The same implication holds for a majority dominant outcome in Zhang (2016), which is an equilibrium selection criterion for a broad class of quantal response functions. In asymmetric pure coordination games, majority dominance is equivalent to strict Pareto dominance, which in turn implies that 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖  is uniquely minimized at 𝑗𝑗 . By contrast, the criterion is nevertheless distinct from aggregate-payoff maximization and risk dominance. In the two-player, two-action case, the square-root ranking can differ from both the ranking by total payoffs and the ranking by the product of deviation losses, the latter characterizing risk dominance in this setting (Zhang and Hofbauer 2016). 

Our results rely on the structure of the game and the logarithmic regularization. In particular, the scalar representation used in the proof exploits the fact that only coordinated action profiles yield positive payoffs. The analysis therefore does not yield a comparable rule for general coordination games or for other quantal response functions such as logit response (Zhang and Hofbauer 2016). The main theorem also requires a unique minimizer of 𝑀𝑀 𝑖𝑖 .When several actions attain the minimum, the argument does not show whether 𝐶𝐶0 has a unique limit. 

Several extensions merit further study. One is to examine whether the square-root ranking provides a useful first-order approximation for two-player games with more than two actions under more general quantal response functions, and to identify conditions under which a local approximation determines the global equilibrium branch (Zhang and Hofbauer, 2016). Another is to characterize the limiting equilibria of logit and other quantal response correspondences in multi-action coordination games (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995; Turocy, 2005; Zhang, 2016). Finally, it would be useful to analyze how equilibrium selection changes under payoff transformations or the addition of dominated strategies (Zhang, 2016), and to compare logarithmic tracing with alternative selection methods, including linear tracing procedures (Harsanyi, 1975; Cao and Dang, 2022), homotopy methods (Turocy, 2005), and evolutionary dynamics (Zhang and Hofbauer, 2015).
