# Hurst Exponent Estimation with Quantum Wavelets 

Brani Vidakovic 

Department of Statistics, Texas A&M University 

Abstract 

How accurately can the Hurst exponent H of fractional Brownian motion be estimated from repeated preparations of a single amplitude-encoded path, when every measurement interrogates the same realization? We extend the ϵ-decimated quantum nondecimated wavelet transform in three directions: we remove the endpoint jump of circular filtering, derive the sampling distribution of the estimator, and compare readouts at equal query budgets. A one-qubit mirror extension removes the jump and keeps every coefficient that straddles a fold of the same order as an interior coefficient, for any compactly supported filter and circular shift. The transform for any such filter is compiled from the paraunitary lattice of its filter bank, and the energy share of a level is the success probability of one multiply-controlled reflection, which is both the controlled unitary of a Hadamard test and the oracle of amplitude estimation. For filters with at least two vanishing moments we prove a root-N central limit theorem for the log-energy contrasts, independent of the limiting path norm, so the studentized estimator is asymptotically standard normal for deterministic query budgets under which the success count at every level diverges. For sparse counts we give a working multinomial–lognormal likelihood, exact in its readout model for direct measurement, and check its coverage by simulation. For paths of length 512, mirroring reduces RMSE by a factor of 3–10 for H ≥ 0.5, and with exact shares the nondecimated db2 estimator is 6–14% above the root Cram´ er–Rao bound. Hadamard level tests are less accurate than direct measurement of the leading qubits at every budget. At H = 0 .9 and 220 queries, maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation attains RMSE 0 .0347, against 0 .0451 for direct measurement and 0 .0295 with exact shares. 

Keywords: quantum wavelet transform; Hadamard test; amplitude estimation; paraunitary lat-tice; symmetric extension; nondecimated wavelet transform; fractional Brownian motion; Hurst exponent. 

# 1 Introduction 

How accurately can the Hurst exponent H of fractional Brownian motion (fBm) be estimated from repeated preparations of a single amplitude-encoded path, and which readout uses a finite budget of preparations best? Every measurement of such a state interrogates the same realization, so the error has two sources, the record and the shots, and the shot noise depends on the record through its norm. The Hurst exponent governs the distribution of energy across scales. In a wavelet decomposition, the expected total detail energy per level grows asymptotically by a factor of 2 2H per step toward coarser levels, combining coefficient variance and the number of coefficients [1, 14]. An orthonormal wavelet transform can be implemented as a quantum unitary [13, 17, 19], now for wavelets of any order [5], and sample paths of fBm can themselves be prepared as amplitude-encoded states with gate complexity polylogarithmic in the number of time steps [8]. Applied to an amplitude-encoded path, it makes the probability of measuring a level equal to the share of energy in that level. 1arXiv:2611.24882v1 [quant-ph] 18 Nov 2026 The ϵ-decimated quantum nondecimated wavelet transform (QNDWT) of Vidakovic [27] builds the whole family of circularly shifted orthogonal transforms coherently, with a shift register in uniform superposition, controlled circular shifts and one wavelet unitary; tracing out the register gives shift-averaged level energies, expectations of level projectors recover them, and their log-spectrum carries H. That construction leaves the inferential question open, and this paper advances it in three directions: it removes the endpoint jump that circular filtering creates in an fBm path, derives the sampling distribution of the resulting estimator of H under finite query budgets, and compares readouts of the level shares at equal budgets. Prior work does not answer the question. Quantum wavelet circuits act on periodic sequences [5, 13, 17, 19], and the ϵ-decimated QNDWT inherits their circular boundary, which for an fBm path joins the two endpoints through a jump; the classical limit theory of wavelet variances of fBm assumes exactly computed coefficients and boundaries handled by discarding coefficients [6, 11, 14, 18], which has no direct quantum counterpart, and it has no notion of a finite number of measurements of a single state. Periodic filtering joins the two endpoints of the path, producing a jump with root mean square of order nH for a record of length n. A few coefficients can then account for most of the energy at a fine level when H > 1/2 (Figure 2). The mirror extension in Section 3.1 removes this jump before filtering. It uses one extra qubit and bounds the second moment of every zero-sum filter translate, including translates crossing a fold. Fold coefficients can have a different covariance from interior coefficients; the finite-record calibration accounts for this difference, and Theorem 7 shows that their contribution vanishes at the N −1/2 scale. Section 3.2 compiles the periodic transform using the paraunitary lattice of a compactly sup-ported orthonormal two-channel filter bank [25, 26]. Each rotation acts on the channel qubit, which distinguishes even and odd samples, and each delay, a one-step circular shift of the odd samples, is implemented as a decrement of the position register controlled on the channel qubit, that is, as a permutation. The construction allows the filter to be chosen for its statistical properties, and the shift-register construction in Section 3.3 produces nondecimated level shares. Figure 1 shows the complete circuit. We reuse the shift-register construction of Vidakovic [27] and build on it results of two kinds. On the quantum side, a one-qubit mirror extension makes circular filtering of a path free of boundary jumps for every compactly supported filter and every circular shift, a lattice compilation gives the transform for any such filter from single-qubit rotations of the channel qubit and controlled permutations of the position register, and a single multiply-controlled reflection, independent of the filter, gives the level share both as the success probability of a Hadamard test and as the target amplitude of amplitude estimation. On the statistical side, we prove a root-N central limit theorem for the mirrored log-energy contrasts with asymptotically negligible fold terms (Theorem 7), and we show that the fine-scale fluctuations are asymptotically independent of the path norm (Lemma 8). The second result is what single-record quantum inference needs: the path norm sets the shot-noise variance and the GLS weights, so a deterministic query budget that grows with the record yields a variance mixture of normals, and the independence makes the studentized estimator asymptotically standard normal nonetheless. For sparse counts, integrating the normalization in closed form gives a working likelihood, a multinomial–lognormal mixture over the K − 1 contrast dimensions whose multinomial readout model is exact for direct measurement and which accepts levels without successes; its calibration is checked by simulation. Finally, on the same records and at equal query budgets, we compare direct measurement of the leading qubits, Hadamard level tests, and maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation, whose oracle is the level reflection. Direct measurement is more efficient than level tests at every setting, because each of its shots informs every level, and amplitude estimation is the most accurate when 2the informative shares are rare: at H = 0 .9 and 2 20 queries its RMSE is 23% below that of direct readout on the same records. At the smallest budget it returns no estimate on 16% of records at 

H = 0 .9, and its advantage there is conditional on success. Its ideal CNOT cost per query is within 2% of sampling, but its circuits are deep: at 2 20 queries the longest one applies 256 Grover iterates coherently, about 2 .6 × 10 6 CNOT gates excluding record preparation, against about 5 × 10 3 for one shot of direct readout.

4.1 Readouts and Resources 

A level test is a projective measurement of {Pj , I − Pj } read out through one ancilla. Its statistics coincide with those of measuring the top J − j qubits and checking the leading-one pattern, so with ideal readout it carries the same information about pj . A direct measurement of all data qubits, however, returns the level of every shot, and therefore informs all levels of the window at once, whereas a level test spends each preparation on one specified level. The advantages of the test are operational: a single qubit is read, the data register survives in the measured level, and the controlled reflection is exactly the oracle needed for amplitude estimation (Section 5.3), in which the data register must not be measured at all. # 6 Numerical Study 

6.1 Design 

Paths were generated by circulant embedding [12]. The main experiment uses n = 512 and six val-ues of H between 0 .2 and 0 .9, with R = 500 paths per value. Each path was mirrored to N = 1024 14 (J = 10) and transformed with L = 6 levels; the window is the six finest levels {4, . . . , 9}. The periodic comparison transforms the unmirrored path ( J = 9, window {3, . . . , 8}). The mirrored configurations use db2 with decimated and nondecimated transforms and db4 with the nondec-imated transform. The periodic configurations use db2 with both transforms. Level tests were simulated from their exact Bernoulli law, with budgets of Q = ∞ (record noise only), 6 × 214 and 6× 218 uses split equally across the six level tests. A record with a level test of zero successes counts as a failure of the log fit; root-mean-squared errors (RMSE) are over the remaining records. The calibration e(H), Γ( H) was computed exactly by traces on a grid of 25 values of H and interpolated. The square root of the Cram´ er–Rao variance bound for H from n = 512 samples, with σ2 unknown, is given for reference. Three further experiments use the same machinery. The record-length experiment repeats the exact-share fit for n ∈ { 256 , 512 , 1024 , 2048 } and H ∈ { 0.5, 0.8, 0.9} with 400 paths per setting, the decimated db2 transform, and the same six-level window at fixed offsets from the finest level; it compares mirror extension, periodic handling, and a classical interior-only fit that applies the periodic transform to the unmirrored path and discards every coefficient whose support crosses the seam, with its own exact calibration. The readout experiment uses 300 mirrored nondecimated db2 records per value of H ∈ { 0.5, 0.65 , 0.8, 0.9} and total budgets of 2 17 , 2 20 and 2 23 queries, a query being one application of A or A†; every readout is applied to the same records, so differences between readouts are paired. Direct readout spends every query on one measurement of the data register, which yields the level of every shot. Level tests spend ⌊Q/ 6⌋ queries on each level. Amplitude estimation spends ⌊Q/ 6⌋ queries on each level with maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation [24], one representative of the amplitude-estimation methods without phase estimation [15, 16]: Grover powers m = 0 , 1, 2, 4, . . . , m max , each followed by Nsh measurements of the good-state indicator, with mmax the largest power for which Nsh ≥ 100 fits the budget. This gives ( mmax , N sh ) = (32 , 164), (256 , 169) and (2048 , 170), and the queries actually used are 99 .8%, 99 .8% and 99 .7% of the three budgets. The share is estimated by maximizing the exact binomial likelihood over the amplitude angle, and its log enters the log fit with the plug-in variance from the Fisher information of the schedule. A level whose estimated share is exactly zero, which happens only when no good outcome is observed at any power, makes the log fit fail for that record, and failures are reported separately. Both the log fit and the count fit are applied to direct readout and to level tests. Monte Carlo standard errors of RMSE ratios between readouts are computed by a paired bootstrap over records.

# 7 Discussion 

The wavelet transform maps the scale-energy distribution into the position of the leading one in the data register. A single multiply-controlled Z implements the controlled reflection for a level, and a Hadamard test reads its share through one ancilla. The lattice isolates the filter-dependent circuit operations. The mirror extension removes the endpoint jump before either decimated or nondecimated filtering, the statistical calibration incorporates the resulting boundary covariance, and Theorem 7 shows that the fold terms do not affect the first-order distribution of the estimator. A path concentrates energy at coarse levels. The fine-level shares used by the estimator decay like N −2H . Direct sampling has a preparation cost proportional to p−1 

> j

for fixed relative precision, and amplitude estimation with the same reflection scales as p−1/2 

> j

and uses coherent access to the inverse of the full preparation circuit. The simulations make the roles of the readouts precise. When the data register can be measured with ideal readout, measuring the leading qubits directly is the efficient sampling readout, because each preparation informs every level; separate Hadamard level tests spend each preparation on one level. The level reflection earns its place as the oracle of amplitude estimation without phase estimation, which needs no controlled preparation, costs within 2% of sampling per query in CNOT gates although its longest circuit applies 513 queries in sequence at 2 20 queries and 4097 at 2 23 , and at large H is more accurate with 2 20 queries than direct readout with 2 23 ; it is also a one-qubit readout on devices where reading many qubits reliably is the binding constraint. The result we would lead with is therefore twofold: mirroring makes readout of general compactly supported wavelets viable for fBm paths, and coherent amplitude estimation improves accuracy when the informative fine-level shares are rare. Future work includes amplitude estimation for several shares in a common window with a 20 shared stopping rule, joint allocation of state-preparation queries across levels, Symmlet and Coiflet experiments that test the statistical and circuit tradeoffs of the lattice construction across filter families, and multivariate records, for which wavelet eigenanalysis of operator fBm [2] suggests readouts of level covariance matrices rather than level shares. A hardware comparison of one-ancilla tests and direct leading-bit measurement would quantify how readout error affects the two protocols; the comparisons here are for ideal queries, and a practical advantage of one-ancilla readout is not claimed.
