# Exhaustive adjustment-set selection and causal e ff ects for distribution-valued outcomes, with inference under latent Gaussian spatio-temporal dependence ⋆

Hsin-Hsiung Huang ∗

> School of Data, Mathematical, and Statistical Sciences, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816, USA

Abstract 

Selecting adjustment covariates for a distribution-valued outcome must preserve dependence that scalar sum-maries can discard, and surveillance outcomes are also dependent across space and time. Under unconfound-edness and a relative-overlap condition we identify average treatment e ff ects, stochastic and incremental intervention e ff ects, and natural direct and indirect e ff ects for such outcomes, with doubly and triply robust scores. For independent data, adjustment sets are selected exhaustively by distance-covariance su ffi cient dimension reduction: we give a root-n projector expansion, a basis-invariant criterion tensor, and a simulta-neous outer confidence collection calibrated by fractional sampling with a finite-population correction. For dependence, units are functions of a latent Gaussian field, exponential in space and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck in time, plus independent noise. Such data are ρ-mixing at an exponential rate, and the covariance between any two square-integrable scores is a power series in the latent correlation with positive semidefinite coe ffi -cients. Consequences include a central limit theorem for all the causal estimators, the exact undercoverage of independence-based intervals, a consistent Mehler covariance estimator under finite chaos order, and an asymptotically conservative convex-chord estimator; block fractional sampling extends the collection un-der an assumed linearization. In simulations, fractional sampling raises weak-signal collection coverage from .69– .78 to at least .98 while retaining nearly all candidate sets, and the dependence-aware estimators substantially reduce undercoverage, although none is exact when few units fall within a correlation range. A descriptive analysis of U.S. syphilis surveillance and Medicaid expansion illustrates the estimands and compares dependence-aware with conventional inference. 

Keywords: Adjustment sets, Distance covariance, Distribution-valued outcomes, Gaussian process, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, Spatial dependence, Subsampling

1. Introduction 

A distribution-valued response can depend on covariates through location, scale or shape, so an ad-justment set adequate for its mean need not remove confounding for the distribution itself. For example, the average time of incidence can be unchanged while its temporal concentration di ff ers between exposure groups. Public-health surveillance adds a second di ffi culty: jurisdictions are neighbours, policies cluster geographically and incidence is persistent, so the independent-sampling approximations behind standard errors and resampling calibrations can fail.

Luo et al. [1] introduced exhaustive identification of su ffi cient adjustment sets through outcome and treatment central subspaces; we build on that criterion and do not propose exhaustive selection as new. Distance-covariance su ffi cient dimension reduction (SDR) [2, 3, 4, 5] supplies natural estimators when scalar inverse-regression summaries are inadequate. Its root-n theory for ordinary responses is due to Sheng and Yin [2, 3]. Our extension concerns powered and feature-distance responses, a whitening-aware projector expansion, and simultaneous inference on the adjustment collection. The contributions are as follows. (i) A basis-invariant criterion tensor and its calibration. The criterion is written through products of projectors and residual covariance matrices, a smooth vector-valued object even when its norm vanishes. Conventional subsampling with b = n0.7 undercovers badly in a weak-signal design, because of the missing finite-population factor and very small subsamples. We prove that fractional sampling ( b/n →

c ∈ (0 , 1)) with the factor {bn /(n − b)}1/2 is valid for independent data (Theorem 5). In the same design it restores coverage, partly through the finite-population factor and partly through larger subsets, but the resulting collection is conservative: it protects against excluding a su ffi cient set rather than discriminating among sets. (ii) A latent Gaussian dependence model. Units are functions of a latent Gaussian field with exponen-tial (Matérn- 12 ) spatial correlation and, for trajectories, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) temporal correlation, plus independent noise. Such data are ρ-mixing at an exponential rate uniformly in set size (Lemma 1), and the covariance of any two square-integrable scores is a power series Pm cm Mm in the latent corre-lation c with positive semidefinite coe ffi cients (Proposition 4). This gives the exact undercoverage of independence-based intervals. (iii) Dependence-aware inference. We prove a central limit theorem for augmented inverse-probability-weighted (AIPW) functional contrasts with parametric nuisances (Theorem 9). A parametric Mehler covariance estimator is consistent under finite chaos order, and a convex-chord estimator is asymptoti-cally conservative for any chaos order and any overstatement of the spatial range (Theorem 10). Block fractional sampling extends the collection calibration to dependent data (Theorem 11), under a lin-earization of the criterion tensor that we assume rather than prove; the selection procedure as a whole is therefore established for independent data only. (iv) Evidence. Simulations generate Poisson incidence trajectories from a Gaussian-process (GP)–OU la-tent field on lattices and on U.S. state geography. An analysis of state syphilis surveillance fits a Pois-son GP–OU model, reports a mixed-model event study alongside two-way fixed e ff ects, and compares dependence-aware and conventional intervals for the functional contrast. Section 3.4 connects the criterion to recent high-dimensional multivariate theory [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] through a deterministic stability bound, without claiming a high-dimensional inferential result. Two distinctions are essential throughout. An outer confidence collection protects against excluding a su ffi cient set; it does not certify every retained set, and at fixed error probability it does not support an oracle post-selection analysis. And Gaussian covariate laws within exposure groups are incompatible with nontrivial confounding under a uniform lower propensity bound, so we use strict positivity for identification and weighted conditions for es-timation. The functional results concern outcomes isometric to a closed convex subset of a separable Hilbert space, such as univariate Wasserstein distributions, and complement Fréchet regression and distributional causal inference [11, 12] and scalar adjustment-set e ffi ciency results [13, 14, 15]. The dependence theory assumes increasing-domain sampling and no interference. All proofs are in Appendix A. The causal framework is set out in Section 2: unconfoundedness and a relative-overlap condition for general stochastic interventions, the average treatment e ff ect and incremental-intervention e ff ects for distribution-valued outcomes, and natural direct and indirect e ff ects under sequential ignorability (Sec-tion 4.3). Every resulting estimator solves stacked estimating equations, so the dependent-data theory of 2Section 5 covers all of them. Section 8 compares the approach, in theory and in application, with the point-process framework of Papadogeorgou et al. [16] and Mukaigawara et al. [17], implemented in the R package 

geocausal .

5. Latent Gaussian spatio-temporal dependence

8. Comparison with point-process spatio-temporal causal inference 

The closest methodological alternative for spatio-temporal causal questions is the framework of Papado-georgou et al. [16], extended to heterogeneous e ff ects by Zhou et al. [50] and to mediation by Mukaigawara et al. [17], and implemented in the R package geocausal [51]. There the treatment in each period is a spa-tial point pattern on a region Ω0, such as the locations of airstrikes, and the estimand is the expected number of outcome events in a set B ⊆ Ω0 when the treatment patterns of the last L periods follow a stochastic intervention. Table 8 compares the two approaches. 

Theory and applications. geocausal obtains replication from many periods over one region, leaves inter-ference unrestricted and requires a correct treatment model, with a conservative variance bound because the variance is not identified. The present approach obtains replication from units, rules out interference and 24 models dependence through a latent field. In the Iraq application, strikes recur daily and spill over kilome-tres, so interference is the substance of the question and our assumptions would be untenable. Medicaid expansion is a one-time areal adoption with essentially one assignment per state, so replication must come from jurisdictions, and the outcome of interest is a distribution over time. A natural combination would take the kernel-smoothed outcome surface of geocausal , an element of L2(Ω0), as the Hilbert-valued outcome of Section 4. 

9. Discussion 

The results connect subspace asymptotics to a basis-invariant adjustment tensor, a calibrated simulta-neous outer collection and functional causal contrasts, and extend the inferential part to spatially and tem-porally dependent units. Two points may be of independent interest. For an asymptotically linear statistic, fractional sampling with the finite-population factor is as easy to justify as subsampling and behaves far better in moderate samples. And under a latent Gaussian model the covariance of any square-integrable score is a power series in the latent correlation with positive semidefinite coe ffi cients. This yields an exact undercoverage formula for independence-based intervals, a parametric covariance estimator, and a convex upper bound that remains valid whatever the nonlinearity of the score and whenever the spatial range is overstated. The same latent structure underlies Gaussian Markov random field and Gaussian-process dis-ease models, so the range can be borrowed from the outcome model that practitioners already fit. Because the ATE, incremental-intervention and mediation estimators are all solutions of stacked estimating equations, one central limit theorem and one pair of covariance estimators serve every causal estimand in the paper. Several boundaries remain. The number of covariates is fixed, exhaustive evaluation is exponential in 

p, and structural dimensions are supplied. The root-n projector expansion is proved for independent data; under dependence, linearization of the criterion tensor is an assumption of Theorem 11, so the selection procedure as a whole is established only for independent data, and in the application the collection served as an independence-based diagnostic while the prespecified full set was used. The covariance theorem covers fixed distance bins and finite chaos order; the adaptive binning of our default chord implementation and the Mehler estimator with m0 = 2 for nonlinear scores are supported numerically, not by the theorem. The dependence model assumes a common latent correlation across components, identically distributed units, increasing-domain sampling and no interference, so it is a working model when covariates such as latitude are fixed site characteristics. The covariance guarantees are asymptotic: with few units per correlation range, the Mehler and even the chord intervals can undercover, and a generous range is the safer choice. The di ff erence-in-di ff erences simulations show how each method behaves when the GP–OU model is or is not correct, not which model describes the data. In the surveillance analysis, the county association changes sign with the propensity clipping threshold, the two-way and mixed-model estimates di ff er in sign, and the mediation analysis rests on a proxy mediator and strong ignorability assumptions, so all observational comparisons remain exploratory. The causal conclusions rest on unconfoundedness given the prespecified covariates and, for mediation, on a cross-world sequential ignorability condition; with exposure-related unmeasured spatial confounding or mediator–outcome confounding, these estimands are not identified, and sensitivity analysis [31, 32] would be the next step. Natural extensions are a dependent-data version of Theorem 3, a finite-sample correction for the covariance estimators, and designs with interference. For the last, the stochastic-intervention estimands of geocausal (Section 8), combined with distribution-valued outcomes and unit-level replication, are a promising route.
