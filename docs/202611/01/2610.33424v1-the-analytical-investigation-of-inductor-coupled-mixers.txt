# The Analytical Investigation of Inductor-Coupled Mixers 

Arash Ghasemi ∗

Sumer Analog LLC, Knoxville, TN, USA University of Tennessee System, USA 

October 27, 2026 

Abstract 

Inductor- and transformer-coupled balanced mixers are usually analyzed with switching approximations, conversion matrices, harmonic balance, or circuit simulation. This paper instead derives their loaded quasistatic equations from the device laws, keeping the transformer ratios, reflected load, bias, and the connections that set the IF common mode. The functions needed to solve these equations are surprisingly varied. A Shockley single-balanced pair is solved by the principal Lambert function 𝑊 0 under a quantified one-diode-dominance condition. The double-balanced ring has a unique loaded solution that reduces to an exact inverse-hyperbolic-tangent form only for an open IF port. Vacuum diodes and triodes lead to Cardano cubics and structured sextics, whereas depletion JFET/MESFET crossbars need only square roots. Bilateral MOS channels give piecewise quadratics in strong inversion, or a Ferrari quartic when the IF winding floats. In weak inversion they give an exact 𝑊 0, the real 𝑟 -Lambert function, or the inverse of sinh 𝑥 + 𝜌𝑥 , depending on topology and IF connection. In matched BJT and HBT Gilbert cells, equal emitter resistors turn the hyperbolic-tangent current steering into an exact 𝑟 -Lambert function. Fourier projection brings in Beta/Gamma, modified Bessel, and complete elliptic functions. We checked the equations against 37 LTspice simulations using seven independent Python programs. In the 12 primary comparisons, which implement the assumed device laws exactly, every RMS waveform error was below 0.017% of the analytical RMS value; built-in and vendor device models were examined separately. Two design examples show how the closed forms size a thermionic IF load and a Gilbert-cell tail current without parameter sweeps.

# 1 Introduction and Prior Work 

In a balanced mixer the nonlinear devices perform the frequency conversion, but the circuit around them decides what actually reaches the IF port. The transformer turns ratios set the voltage swings at the device terminals, the winding polarities determine which products cancel, the IF load closes the nonlinear circuit equation, and the center taps fix the common-mode voltage. These details are what distinguish a single-balanced pair from a double-balanced ring, even when both are built from the same device. Modern broadband designs still put considerable effort into balun amplitude and phase balance and into IF filtering [1], and commercial passive-FET mixers continue to use a four-device crossbar surrounded by three coupled ports [2]. 

1.1 Prior work 

The theory of balanced rectifier modulators driven by a strong carrier is much older than modern RF CAD. Caruthers analyzed carrier-controlled copper-oxide modulators [3], and Belevitch developed a linear switched-resistance theory of bridge and ring modulators [4]. Saleh later formulated a network theory of resistive mixers as periodically time-varying circuits [5]. Subsequent work used response coefficients and conversion matrices to analyze the response, conversion loss, and noise of double-balanced mixers [6, 7], and identified finite commutation and imperfect diode switching as sources of intermodulation [8]. Most of this work starts from a switched or linearized device description. Here we instead solve the nonlinear circuit equations with the IF load in place, and only then extract the wanted frequency components from the resulting waveform. Each of the standard analysis tools answers a different question. Taylor and Volterra expansions expose low-order intermodulation, conversion matrices and harmonic balance keep track of many spectral components 5at once, and transient SPICE simulation includes compact device models and parasitics. A recent nodal Volterra analysis, for example, predicts third-order distortion in balanced, double-balanced, and triple-balanced diode mixers to within 3 dB of simulation [9]. These methods are indispensable once a topology has been chosen. What they usually do not give the designer is the loaded instantaneous waveform as a compact function of the device and transformer parameters. Closed-form inversions have appeared before, but in narrower settings. A diode that obeys Shockley’s exponential law [10] in series with a resistor can be solved exactly with the Lambert 𝑊 function [11, 12]. For the diode ring, Parker used a static polynomial reduction selected by the carrier [13], whereas Bernardini et al. derived Lambert-𝑊 wave-digital elements for a simplified ring model [14]. Lambert 𝑊 also appears in analytical MOS electrostatics [15]. To our knowledge, however, no previous work has established which loaded balanced-mixer equations reduce to the ordinary 𝑊 function, which ones require other inverse functions, and how the answer depends on the device law, the load, and the bias connections. The devices themselves have an equally long history. Frequency conversion with thermionic diodes had been characterized experimentally by 1939 [16], and the space-charge law that governs these diodes is due to Child and Langmuir [17, 18]. Driving FET switching elements in antiphase from a transformer was patented in 1968 [19]. Pucel et al. reported measurements and models of GaAs MESFET mixers in the X band, roughly 8 to 12 GHz [20]. For MOS channels, the long-channel square-law model [21] and the all-region EKV model [22] provide two useful asymptotic laws. Among active mixers, the double-balanced Gilbert mixer and its hyperbolic-tangent steering law rest on the translinear core of Gilbert’s matched bipolar four-quadrant multiplier [23]. The circuits and the special functions used here each have their own history; what is new is combining them in a way that accounts for the circuit topology. The transformers and baluns have a design literature of their own. Ruthroff’s broadband transformers [24] and monolithic-balun mixers [25] address bandwidth, balance, and matching rather than the pointwise solution of the nonlinear equations. Work on resistive FET mixers ranges from symmetric MOS mixers [26] and low-intermodulation MESFET designs [27, 28] to a simplified analysis, aimed at hand calculation, based on time-varying reflection coefficients [29]. Wideband SiGe-HBT Gilbert mixers have likewise been analyzed with conversion matrices and harmonic balance and verified by measurement [30]. Our formulas belong to an earlier stage. They give first component estimates, under clearly stated load and bias assumptions, before detailed frequency-domain and implementation work begins. We define a single coordinate system for inductively coupled mixers that keeps the turns ratios, the reflected load, the winding polarities, the bias, and the IF common mode. We derive the loaded single- and double-balanced equations for Shockley and thermionic diodes, bilateral MOS channels in strong and weak inversion, depletion JFET/MESFET and vacuum-triode crossbars, and matched BJT/HBT half-Gilbert and Gilbert cells. 3. # 7 LTspice Validation 

We tested the analytical results for all six device families with 37 LTspice netlists, each tied to a specific mixer equation or to a supplementary check. Seven Python programs read the simulated waveforms and compared them with the equations, both in the time domain and at the relevant FFT bins. Where an equation holds only under stated operating conditions, the program for that device family also checked those conditions and used only the samples that satisfied them. A separate driver script ran all netlists in sequence and confirmed that each simulation finished without error and wrote fresh output files (Appendix D). # 9 Discussion and Design Use 

Taken together, the results suggest a practical analytical workflow. 1. Refer the source voltages and loads through the actual winding ratios, and use (2) to check that the magnetizing inductance is large enough. 2. Choose a device law whose range of validity covers the intended bias and signal swing. 3. Specify how the IF load is connected and how its common-mode voltage is set. A center tap held at a fixed voltage, separate resistors from the two IF endpoints to a reference, and an isolated floating winding lead to different circuit equations. 4. Solve the monotone KCL equation, or its closed-form reduction where one is valid, and reject any candidate that violates the inequalities defining its conduction state. 5. Project the result onto the IF, and pass the estimate on to compact-model SPICE, harmonic-balance, electromagnetic, and tolerance analyses. The same steps explain why particular special functions appear. An exponential current combined with a single linear load term gives the ordinary 𝑊 0. Resistive feedback around an exponential pair gives the real 

𝑟 -Lambert function. A three-halves-power law becomes a cubic after a square-root substitution. Coupled square laws under a floating constraint produce a quartic. Keeping both 𝑒 𝑥 and 𝑒 −𝑥 together with a linear term gives the inverse of sinh 𝑥 + 𝜌𝑥 , which has no standard name. Bessel, Gamma/Beta, and elliptic functions enter 27 only later, through Fourier integration. The device type alone therefore does not determine the mathematics; the load and the IF connection matter just as much. Several limitations should be kept in mind when interpreting these results. Most of the headline comparisons test the equations against behavioral implementations of the same memoryless device law. They therefore verify the algebra and the topology, not the accuracy of any commercial device model. The native JFET and BJT runs and the vendor HBT model are useful stress tests, but the HBT was simulated only at low frequency, and we make no claim about its microwave performance. The passive MOS law assumes a body held at a fixed potential, independent of the channel terminals, and it excludes body-diode conduction. The triode crossbar is an actively biased circuit, not a passive substitution. Noise figure, compression, intercept points, thermal behavior, and port isolation all depend on parasitics and mismatch that are outside the scope of this paper. Most importantly, the paper contains no hardware measurements. It should be judged as nonlinear circuit theory supported by reproducible SPICE checks. Finally, we do not claim to have invented balanced mixers, the Lambert 𝑊 function, Cardano’s formula, or the Gilbert cell. What the paper contributes is a systematic account of which analytical solution applies to each inductively coupled circuit, for a stated load and bias connection, and of the conditions under which that solution is valid. 

# 10 Conclusion 

We have derived loaded analytical equations for inductively coupled single- and double-balanced mixers built from six families of device laws. The common framework keeps three steps separate: the finite-inductance drive, the nonlinear circuit equation under ideal transformer ratios, and the Fourier projection onto the IF. This separation makes it clear when a formula derived for one IF load or common-mode connection can, and cannot, be reused for another. The central result is a map from physical assumptions to mathematical structure. The Shockley and weak-inversion exponential laws lead to the ordinary 𝑊 0, but only under their stated conditions. Floating weak-inversion MOS bridges and degenerated bipolar cells lead to the real 𝑟 -Lambert function. Three-halves-power laws give Cardano cubics and structured sextics, and floating square-law MOS bridges give Ferrari quartics. Spectral projection brings in Gamma/Beta, modified Bessel, and elliptic functions. The depletion-FET circuits remain elementary square-root problems, and several of the complete loaded networks are best described simply as the unique root of a monotone scalar equation. The 37-case LTspice suite confirms all of the principal equations within their stated conditions of validity. The two sizing examples show the practical value of the approach. A load resistance or bias current can be chosen without a parameter sweep, and the resulting IF amplitude agrees with a simulation of the same device law to within a few tenths of a percent at the operating points shown. Future work should add measured transformer and device data, conversion loss, noise, compression, and tolerance statistics, so that the analytical front end can be tested as a complete hardware design method.
