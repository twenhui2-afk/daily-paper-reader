# Linear-Time Optimal Leaf Roots beyond 3-Leaf Powers 

Daniela Ceban and Christian Rosenke 

Institut für Informatik, Universität Rostock, Rostock, Germany 

daniela.ceban@uni-rostock.de christian.rosenke@uni-rostock.de 

Abstract. A k-leaf power for an integer k ≥ 2 is a graph G that permits a k-leaf root, a tree T with leaf set V (G) such that, for all distinct vertices x, y ∈ V (G), their distance in T is at most k if and only if xy is an edge of G. Constructing such trees is motivated by the reconstruction of phylogenetic trees from similarity data. For every fixed k, Lafond [ACM Trans. Algorithms 2023] showed that 

k-leaf powers can be recognized in polynomial time. Recently, Dupré la Tour, Lafond, and Ndiaye [SODA 2026] proved that the problem becomes NP-complete when k is part of the input and that deciding whether a graph is a k-leaf power for some k is NP-complete as well. Le and Rosenke [J. Graph Algorithms Appl. 2024] proposed a different algorithmic perspective by studying optimal leaf roots of graphs G, that is, the k-leaf roots of G with minimum possible k. This smallest k-value is called the leaf rank of G. They showed that optimal leaf roots of chordal cographs, a class of unbounded leaf rank, can be constructed in linear time. However, chordal cographs do not even contain all 3-leaf powers. This paper extends linear-time optimal-leaf-root construction to three nat-ural superclasses of the 3-leaf powers, the (bull , gem )-free, the (bull , dart )-free, and the (dart , gem )-free chordal graphs. Except for the latter case, which follows from known results, our methods are based on structural decompositions of bull-free chordal graphs. In particular, the relevant 

(bull , gem )-free graphs decompose into chordal cographs attached to a tree, while the (bull , dart )-free case reduces to trees and powers of paths. 

Keywords: k-leaf power, k-leaf root, optimal k-leaf root, chordal cographs, 

3-leaf powers, bull-free leaf powers 

# 1 Introduction 

Introduced by Nishimura, Ragde, and Thilikos [16], leaf powers model the phy-logeny reconstruction problem from computational biology: given a graph G

whose vertices represent species and whose edges represent interspecies similari-ties, reconstruct an evolutionary tree T explaining these similarities. Formally, a k-leaf root of G, for an integer k ≥ 2, is a tree T with leaf set V (G) such that distinct species x, y ∈ V (G) have distance at most k in T if and only if 

xy ∈ E(G). If such a tree exists, G is a k-leaf power. Figure 1 illustrates a graph together with a 5-leaf root. arXiv:2611.31464v1 [cs.DM] 23 Nov 2026 2 D. Ceban and C. Rosenke

Algorithmic research on k-leaf roots has primarily focused on the correspond-ing k-leaf power recognition problem : given G and k, decide if G has a k-leaf root. This computational problem has been studied extensively since the introduction of the notion. Among the first milestones are structural characterizations and linear-time recognition algorithms for 3- and 4-leaf powers [2,4] and recognition algorithms for 5- and 6-leaf powers [6,11]. For a more comprehensive overview, we refer to the chapter on leaf powers in Topics in Algorithmic Graph Theory [17]. However, two important developments after the publication of that chapter have changed the algorithmic landscape considerably. Lafond [14] showed that k-leaf power recognition can be solved in O(ng(k)) time, where g depends only on k.Hence, the problem is in polynomial time for fixed k. When k is part of the input, even with unary encoding, Dupré la Tour, Lafond and Ndiaye [12] recently proved that recognition is NP-complete. They also show the NP-completeness of deciding for a given graph G if there is an integer k such that G is a k-leaf power. In view of the severe dependency of leaf root construction on the parameter k,Le and Rosenke [15] pursued the following optimization problem: given a graph 

G, the Optimal Leaf Root problem (OLR) asks for a κ-leaf root T of G such that κ ≤ k for every k-leaf root of G, or answers No , if G is not a leaf power. The integer κ is also referred to as the leaf rank of G in the literature [1,13]. From the motivating viewpoint, minimizing k is natural because it delivers a phylogenetic tree that minimizes the maximum number of evolutionary events that may lie between similar species. The main result in [15] is a linear-time algorithm that solves OLR for chordal cographs, which are exactly the P4-free chordal graphs, a graph class of unbounded leaf rank also known as the trivially perfect graphs. For example, the 5-leaf root in Figure 1 is optimal for the given graph G,which, accordingly, has leaf rank 5. However, G contains an induced P4.Chordal cographs do not even contain all 3-leaf powers, which usefully can be described as follows: A connected graph is a 3-leaf power if and only if it is a 

(bull , dart , gem )-free chordal graph [10] if and only if it can be obtained from a tree by substituting non-empty cliques into its vertices [2]. In contrast to chordal cographs, 3-leaf powers may contain P4, but they exclude, among others, bull and gem, two natural extensions of P4. They additionally exclude the dart, which is itself a chordal cograph and, thus, explains why chordal cographs are not a subclass of 3-leaf powers. In this paper, we relax the forbidden subgraph characterization of 3-leaf powers and study the complexity of OLR on the three classes of chordal graphs obtained by forbidding only two graphs: the (bull , dart )-free, the (bull , gem )-free, and the (dart , gem )-free chordal graphs. Naturally, each of them generalizes 3-leaf powers, but only the (bull , gem )-free chordal graphs also contain the chordal cographs. Our main algorithmic result is the following. 

Theorem 1. Let G be a chordal graph on n vertices and m edges. If G is 

(bull , gem )-free, (bull , dart )-free, or (dart , gem )-free, then an optimal leaf root of 

G can be computed in O(n + m) time. 

Before we can elaborate on the ideas behind this result, we remind the reader of the well-established fact in this area that a graph G is a k-leaf power if and only if every connected component of G, after collapsing each true twin class to one representative, is a k-leaf power. Therefore, reducing recognition or leaf root construction to con nected chor dal graphs without true twins is common practice in the given research field. To avoid repeatedly defining this condition in the remainder of the paper, we call these graphs conchort .It turns out that the (dart , gem )-free case of Theorem 1 follows from known results. In fact, a chordal graph is (dart , gem )-free if and only if it is obtained from a block graph by substituting cliques into its vertices [3]. That k-leaf powers are closed under clique substitution, being the reverse operation to true twin reduction, is folklore. Thus, it suffices to consider just the underlying block graphs. Every block graph is a 4-leaf power [5] and, hence, has leaf rank 2, 3, or 4. Since 

2-leaf powers are trivial and for both 3- and 4-leaf powers, recognition and root construction are in linear time [2,4], OLR can be solved in linear time, here. For the other two classes, we develop the following main structural tool. 

Theorem 2. A graph G is a bull-free conchort graph if and only if one of the following is true: (i) G is a single vertex or a 2-connected bull-free conchort graph. (ii) G has a universal cut vertex u, and every connected component of G − u is a smaller bull-free conchort graph. (iii) There is a tree H on at least four vertices where distinct vertices in the leaf set L have different neighbors in H and G results from H by substituting every leaf x ∈ L with a bull-free chordal graph Gx without true twins. 

Theorem 2 reduces the remaining cases to the structure of their 2-connected graphs and, for the 1-separable members, to the structure of graphs attached to the leaves of the tree H underlying the decomposition. For (bull , dart )-free conchort graphs, both parts of the theorem become particularly rigid. We show that the 1-separable cases are precisely the trees on at least four vertices, while every 2-connected graph is proper interval, here. The former condition represents the 3-leaf powers after true twin reduction and the latter is much stronger than it first appears. More precisely, every connected bull-free proper interval graph G

The 1-separable graphs, with the additional exclusion of gem, make induced P4 in the substituted graphs Gx impossible and, thus, reduce them to chordal cographs. This enables the combination of locally optimal leaf roots for suitably augmented versions of the graphs Gx, obtained by the algorithm from [15], with the core of tree H without losing global optimality. # 3 The Structure of Bull-free Conchort Graphs 

Both remaining generalizations of 3-leaf powers are subclasses of bull-free conchort graphs. A meaningful way to attack OLR on both of them is to uncover tree-like substructures underlying chordal graphs that forbid bull as induced subgraph. The hope in that is to find a general method of fitting leaf roots along the branches of such a substructure. However, at the time of writing this article, it is even unknown whether all bull-free conchort graphs are leaf powers. 

3.1 Decompositions of 1-separable Bull-free Conchort Graphs 

In a bull-free graph, studying the cut vertices c is a very natural starting point. The bull itself contains two cut vertices and, therefore, forbidding induced bulls imposes strong restrictions on how different blocks can intersect in c. In fact, we can easily see that c has to be universal in each of its blocks. 

Lemma 1. If G is a bull-free conchort graph, c is a cut vertex of G, and B is a block of G that contains c, then c is adjacent to all vertices in V (B) − c.

The reason that a cut vertex c sees everything in a block B is that, otherwise, there would be a vertex u ∈ V (B) − NB (c) that shares a neighbor v with c.Together with any neighbor x ∈ NG(c) − V (B) of c outside the block, these four vertices u, v, c, x would induce a P4 in G. Moreover, that B is a 2-connected component would imply another vertex w ∈ V (B) with u, v, c ∈ NG(w) and 

wx / ∈ E(G). Now, since none of the vertices u, c, x distinguishes v and w and because G is true twin-free, there is a vertex y that is adjacent to exactly one of them, say v. Browsing through all possible neighborhood relations between y and the vertices u, c, x delivers a forbidden induced subgraph in every constellation. The local universality of cut vertices has a tremendous effect on the connec-tivity of the blocks within G. In fact, we can conclude that every block can host at most one cut vertex unless it is a bridge. 

Lemma 2. If G is a bull-free conchort graph and B is a block of G, then B is an edge or contains at most one cut vertex of G.

The lemma uses that two cut vertices u and v in a block B, which has at least one more vertex w, would first imply the existence of vertices x ∈ NG(u) − V (B) and Linear-Time Optimal Leaf Roots beyond 3-Leaf Powers 7

y ∈ NG(v) − V (B) and second that u, v, w would be a triangle due to Lemma 1. Since u and v would separate x and y from the rest of V (B), x would see u but neither v nor w and y would see v but not u or w. Hence, G would contain an induced C4, if xy ∈ E(G), or an induced bull, otherwise. Recall that it is always the case that the blocks of a connected graph G have tree structure. This is in particular demonstrated by the block-cut-tree of G.With Lemma 2, however, the block structure becomes even more restricted for bull-free conchort graphs. In fact, inner blocks, those that share more than one cut vertex with neighboring blocks, are required to be just edges, while the outer blocks with only one cut vertex correspond to the leaves of the block-cut-tree. 3.2 (Bull, gem)-free Conchort Graphs generalize Chordal Cographs 

The structure of bull-free conchort graphs G, as described in Theorem 2, is already restrictive and, yet, it suggests a recursive approach to leaf root construction: (i) decompose G into the tree H and the graphs Gx at the leaves x of H, (ii) recursively create an optimal leaf root Tx for every graph Gx, and (iii) somehow combine those leaf roots and H into one optimal leaf root for G. And while this may be the right approach for bull-free conchort graphs, the whole recursion tree collapses to at most one level for (bull , gem )-free conchort graphs. In the (bull , gem )-free setting, either the entire graph G is a chordal cograph or all substituted graphs Gx are. This happens because cut vertices c are universal in the contained blocks B. Hence, if there was an induced P4 in B then, together with the universal c, this would induce a gem in B and, thus, in G – impossible. The following lemma formally states this structural condition. 

Lemma 3. A graph G is a (bull , gem )-free conchort graph if and only if one of the following is true: (i) G is a conchort cograph. (ii) There is a tree H on at least four vertices where distinct vertices in the leaf set L have different neighbors in H and G results from H by substituting every leaf x ∈ L with a chordal cograph Gx without true twins. 

For a leaf root construction approach, this means that, without further recursion, the algorithm of Le and Rosenke [15] provides an optimal leaf root for G itself or at least all the graphs Gx, after some slight extension. The details on how to combine these leaf roots into one follow in Section 4.

# 5 Conclusion 

This paper introduces a linear-time solution to OLR for a substantial gener-alization of chordal cographs. The unrestricted OLR problem is NP-hard [12] and, since leaf powers are strongly chordal [17], hardness remains even when restricting attention to this chordal subclass. A meaningful direction for future research is to investigate the precise borderline between efficiently solvable and intractable cases of this problem. However, the gap between the classes considered here and general leaf powers is still substantial. From the standpoint established here, natural next milestones are efficient OLR algorithms for the chordal graph classes obtained by forbidding only one of the bull, dart, and gem, with the gem-free chordal graphs also known as the Ptolemaic graphs. Another non-trivial research objective, also motivated by the results of this paper, is to determine the complexity of OLR on proper interval graphs or even general interval graphs.
