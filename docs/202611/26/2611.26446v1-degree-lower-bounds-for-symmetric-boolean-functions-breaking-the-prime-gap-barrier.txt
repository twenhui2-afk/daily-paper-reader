# Degree Lower Bounds for Symmetric Boolean Functions: Breaking the Prime-Gap Barrier 

Xiaoming Sun ∗ Chengu Wang †

Abstract 

We prove that every nonconstant symmetric Boolean function on n variables has exact real polynomial degree at least n − O(n1/3(log n)2/3). This makes progress toward the conjecture of von zur Gathen and Roche that the degree is always at least n − 3. The original prime-gap argument of von zur Gathen and Roche yields a degree deficit of O(n0.525 ) using the theorem of Baker, Harman, and Pintz, or O(n0.52 ) using an estimate claimed in a recent preprint. Our 

˜O(n1/3) bound on the degree deficit improves upon the ˜O(√n) bound obtainable from the prime-gap approach, even assuming the Riemann hypothesis or Legendre’s conjecture. Notably, our proof avoids prime-gap estimates entirely. The proof combines vanishing binomial moments with the arithmetic of linear recurrences. A quantitative form of the integer Hankel determinant method in the Pólya–Carlson theorem forces the function’s values on a central interval of Hamming weights to satisfy a short rational recurrence. A second determinant argument bounds the number of characteristic roots near 

−1. Through the binomial transform, these roots can then be cancelled by a polynomial test of degree below the deficit. An integer annihilator and contour estimates make the test isolate the first failure of the recurrence, yielding a contradiction with the vanishing-moment conditions. If the recurrence agrees with the function on a sufficiently long interval, arithmetic height bounds instead force a bounded period, which is ruled out by a separate polynomial test. Every numbered result in the paper has been formally verified in Lean 4.

Disclosure of AI tools 40 1 Introduction 

Every function f : {0, 1}n → R can be represented by a unique multilinear polynomial in 

R[x1, . . . , x n], meaning that the polynomial equals f at every point of {0, 1}n, with nothing required off the cube. The degree of this polynomial is the exact real degree deg R(f ). The study of Boolean functions through their real polynomial representations goes back to the book of Minsky and Papert [MP17], first published in 1969. In 1992, Nisan and Szegedy [NS92, NS94] studied the degree as a complexity measure in its own right. They proved that a function depending on n variables has degree at least log 2 n − O(log log n), which is tight up to the lower-order term for the address function. A general Boolean function can therefore have degree logarithmic in its number of relevant variables. A Boolean function f : {0, 1}n → { 0, 1} is symmetric when its value depends only on the Hamming weight. Write 

f (x) = F (|x|), F : {0, . . . , n } → { 0, 1},

and call F the profile of f . Symmetry reduces a truth table of size 2 n to these n + 1 values. The symmetrization argument of Minsky and Papert averages a multilinear polynomial over all permutations of the variables and produces a univariate polynomial in the Hamming weight, of no larger degree. This is the step behind the following reduction: for symmetric f , the degree deg R(f )equals the degree of the unique polynomial qF ∈ Q[X] of degree at most n interpolating F on the 

grid {0, . . . , n }.Even a simple root count forces substantial degree: for a nonconstant profile F , the polynomial 

qF (qF − 1) is nonzero and vanishes at all n + 1 grid points, so deg qF ≥ ⌈ (n + 1) /2⌉. Nonconstant symmetric functions therefore lie at the opposite extreme from the logarithmic behavior above. The journal version of Nisan and Szegedy closes with three open problems [NS94, Section 4]. The third asks for a lower bound on the degree of a nonconstant symmetric function going beyond the easy bound n/ 2. That is the problem studied here. The challenge is to show that the degree is much closer to n. This requires using the equal spacing of the grid and the arithmetic of the two allowed values, beyond the number of zeros alone. Von zur Gathen and Roche [vzGR97] studied this problem as one of polynomials with two values. They describe their work as motivated by that of Nisan and Szegedy, and credit Szegedy with posing the problem. Let p(n) be the largest prime at most n + 1. Their argument, carried out modulo the prime p(n) [vzGR97, Theorem 2.6 and the proof of Theorem 2.7], gives deg qF ≥ p(n) − 1for every nonconstant profile F . Since p(n) is close to n, the natural quantity is now the remaining distance from n. For a nonconstant profile define 

δ(F ) = n − deg qF , ∆( n) = max

O(n0.525 ). Li’s preprint [Li25, Theorem 1] claims a prime-gap estimate that would give O(n0.52 ). Von zur Gathen and Roche also conjectured a constant bound, and more specifically its sharp possible value [vzGR97, pp. 346, 349, 360]: 

Conjecture 1.1 (von zur Gathen–Roche) . For every n ≥ 1, ∆( n) ≤ 3.

1Their constructions attain deficit three in two families [vzGR97, Theorems 4.2 and 4.3], described in Section 1.3. Thus no smaller universal constant is possible. Their computer search determined every profile of positive deficit for n ≤ 128, and the maximal deficit in this range is three. The reduction to a prime explains both the strength and the limitation of using primes: it gives full degree at prime-adjacent sizes, while its guarantee at a general size is measured by the distance to the preceding prime. A uniform improvement by this route would require correspondingly stronger control of prime gaps. Under the Riemann hypothesis, Cramér [Cra36] proved that the prime gap near n is O(√n log n), which would give ∆( n) = O(√n log n). Even Legendre’s conjecture, that there is a prime between m2 and ( m + 1) 2 for every integer m ≥ 1, would give only ∆( n) = O(√n). Cramér’s probabilistic model suggests gaps of maximal order (log n)2, and prime gaps are unbounded in any case, so this route cannot by itself prove Conjecture 1.1, or any constant bound. Cohen, Shpilka, and Tal [CST17] study a more general problem: the degree of polynomials mapping {0, . . . , n } into {0, . . . , m }, for an integer m ≥ 1. Symmetric Boolean functions are the case 

m = 1. In that case, they prove a square-root degree-deficit bound at sizes n = p2 − 1 with p prime, and ask for a uniform bound deg qF ≥ n − √n as an intermediate goal toward the constant-deficit conjecture. In a different direction, Sun, Sun, Wang, et al. [SSW +20] investigate the degree of symmetric Boolean functions over the finite ring Zm, rather than over R or as integer-valued polynomials. Their arguments build on earlier work of Wilson [Wil06] on Mahler expansions and periodicity properties of symmetric functions modulo prime powers. 

1.1 Main result 

Our main theorem lowers the exponent in the bound on ∆( n) from 0 .525 [vzGR97, BHP01], or the 0.52 claimed in [Li25], to 1 /3. 

Theorem 1.2. There is an absolute constant C > 0 such that, for every positive integer n,

∆( n) ≤ Cn 1/3(log n)2/3.

Theorem 1.2 goes beyond the bound O(√n log n) that Cramér’s theorem gives under the Riemann hypothesis and the bound O(√n) that Legendre’s conjecture would give, and it gives a smaller deficit than the uniform goal √n of Cohen, Shpilka, and Tal. The proof uses none of the prime-gap estimates above. Its arithmetic inputs are elementary integrality facts (a nonzero integer has modulus at least one, and p-adic valuations of denominators), the irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials, and Voutier’s explicit form of Dobrowolski’s height bound. Every numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition and definition of this paper has been formalized and checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib, but the formal proofs do not always match the written ones. The Lean code is available at https://github.com/wcgbg/sym-bool-func-deg .

1.3 Open problems 

The principal open problem is to close the gap between the bound ∆( n) = ˜O(n1/3) of Theorem 1.2 and the value 3 predicted by Conjecture 1.1. Computation supports the conjecture. Our computer search verifies ∆( n) ≤ 3 for every n ≤ 8192, extending the range n ≤ 128 of von zur Gathen and Roche [vzGR97]. Let ( fj )j≥0 be the Fibonacci sequence with f0 = 0 and f1 = 1. The search also shows that, for n ≤ 8192, ∆( n) = 3 holds exactly at the sizes given by their two constructions [vzGR97, Theorems 4.2 and 4.3]: n = 4 t2 − 1 for integers t ≥ 2, and n = f2i+2 f2i+3 + 1 for integers i ≥ 2 with i̸ ≡ 1 (mod 3). We do not know whether ∆( n) ≥ 3 holds at any size outside these two families. This computation is not part of the Lean formalization. The search code and validation records are available in the same public repository. Closing the gap may require more of the Boolean structure than our proof retains. For a profile
