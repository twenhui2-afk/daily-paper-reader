Abstract
We construct wavelet-based determinantal samplers on manifolds and geometric
graphs whose approximation rates adapt to the regularity of the observable. On a Rie-
mannian manifold of dimension m, our wavelet DPPs satisfy variance reduction of order
n1−2s/m for linear statistics of both s-Hölder and Hs observables, where 0 < s ≤1.
Using these samplers, we further construct unbiased quadrature rules with root mean
square error O(n−1/2−s/m) using at most 3n/2 function evaluations, matching the
classical optimal randomized integration exponent. For point clouds sampled from an
m-dimensional manifold, we develop corresponding wavelet determinantal samplers on
two standard classes of geometric random graphs: ε-graphs and symmetric k-nearest-
neighbour graphs. These samplers satisfy the same variance reduction for linear statis-
tics of s-Hölder observables and yield conditionally unbiased estimators of empirical
averages with root mean square error O(n−1/2−s/m), using at most 2n function evalu-
ations. In both settings, the rates are governed by the intrinsic dimension m and the
regularity s of the observable.

Introduction
Many problems in numerical integration and machine learning require approximating an
integral or a large empirical average using only a small number of function evaluations.
Formally, let µ be a probability measure on a space X, and let F be a family of integrable
functions f : X →R. For f ∈F, we seek an estimator bIn(f) of I(f) :=
R
X f dµ using
only n evaluations of f. Examples include numerical quadrature, gradient estimation using
minibatches in stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and the construction of small weighted
subsets that uniformly approximate a family of objective functions, known as coresets. For
square-integrable observables, independent sampling provides a simple and widely appli-
cable approach, with root mean square error (RMSE) of order n−1/2. This general rate,
however, does not exploit the regularity of the observable or the geometry of the underlying
space. A natural question is whether these additional structures can improve approximation
accuracy for the same evaluation budget.

Determinantal point processes (DPPs) [9, 6, 8, 11] provide a kernel-based approach to
this question [7]. Their negative dependence can reduce redundancy among selected points
and yield more accurate integration estimates [3, 1, 2, 14], while their algebraic structure
allows fluctuations to be studied through the variance of linear statistics. Kernel design
plays a central role: quantitative improvements depend on how the sampling distribution
interacts with the observables of interest. Recent work has developed two complementary
approaches for DPP kernel designs: wavelet DPPs in Euclidean spaces exploit observable
regularity, including limited smoothness [12]; spectral DPPs built from diffusion operators
provide intrinsic constructions on manifolds, point clouds, and networks, with guarantees
that reflect the underlying geometry [13].
In this work, we bring these two approaches together through localized spectral con-
structions on manifolds and geometric graphs. Spectral wavelets and heat kernels provide
localized features, from which we construct finite-rank orthogonal projections defining the
DPP kernels. The central challenge is to retain sufficient localization when passing from
these features to an orthogonal projection. We use this localization to establish variance
reduction and approximation rates that adapt to observable regularity. The resulting con-
structions provide a common approach to sampling in continuous and discrete settings,
with rates governed by intrinsic dimension and regularity.

5.1
Heat Kernel DPPs
We distinguish between the cost of computing the projection Πh and the costs incurred for
each draw from (18).
Preprocessing.
We first consider the construction of A and Z. For the ε-graph (9),
we first need to compute the pairwise distances |xi −xj|, which takes O(dN2) operations,
and the graph is then obtained by comparing these distances with ε. For the symmetric
k-nearest-neighbour graph (10), selecting the k nearest vertices to each xi costs O(N2)
operations in total, while forming Ak from these selections requires an additional O(Nk)
operations.
On the events of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, we have E = O(NK), where K is defined
