Abstract 

For every finite point set X in a Euclidean space of arbitrary dimension, we prove that there is a spanning tree of maximum degree three and total length at most (757 /500) MST( X) = 1 .514 MST( X), where MST( X)denotes the unrestricted minimum spanning-tree length. More strongly, for any root and any finite list of other points, there is a Hamiltonian path starting at the root whose length is at most 1 .514 times the sum of the root-to-point distances. The proof follows the rooted-path and variable-block framework used in earlier work, and applies Young’s weighted-distance inequality to explicit rational mixtures of paths. Eight ordered radius regions cover the normalized domain. Their local estimates require only 29 vertex comparisons, all certified by rational arithmetic. The cover uses the three thresholds 1 /4, 3 /8, and 1 /2; its largest block has nine vertices. We give complete proofs of the normalization transfer, the global induction, and the terminal cases with zero or one actual tail point. We also give a fixed-dimensional cross-polytope lower bound, present Zbarsky’s orthogonal simplex-cluster construction in explicit coordinates, and compare their finite-dimension bounds. The base cases complete the upper-bound argument before the lower-bound constructions are presented. 

Keywords: Euclidean minimum spanning tree; degree constraint; Hamiltonian path; weighted distances; geometric inequalities. 

# 1 Introduction 

A minimum spanning tree connects a finite set of points with the least possible total edge length. A degree constraint limits the number of edges incident with a vertex and may force a longer network. For a finite set X

in a Euclidean space, write MST( X) for the minimum length of an unrestricted spanning tree and MST 3(X) for the minimum length among spanning trees of maximum degree at most three. Edges are straight segments, their weights are Euclidean lengths, and no additional vertices are introduced at crossings. The dimension-free problem asks how large the ratio MST 3(X)/ MST( X) can be. A doubled-tree traversal followed by shortcutting gives an elementary factor-two guarantee, even for a spanning path in an arbitrary metric space. Improving this guarantee uses Euclidean information that the triangle inequality alone does not capture.

Chan [1] improved the arbitrary-dimensional degree-three factor to 2 √6/3 ≈ 1.632993, and obtained a planar factor below 1 .402. His conference and journal versions appeared in 2003 and 2004. The planar and dimension-free problems must be distinguished: a bound proved using planar geometry does not automatically extend to all Euclidean dimensions. Zbarsky [5] proved the arbitrary-dimensional factor 1 .559. His proof controls paths with a prescribed starting point, keeps two possible ending points, and varies the size of a removed block according to the ordered radii. He also constructed examples giving a tree-ratio lower bound of about 1 .447. Young’s weighted-distance inequality, from a 1994 technical report [4], bounds weighted sums of pairwise distances when the point radii are prescribed. That inequality is used in Zbarsky’s proof and is also the geometric tool used here. We prove the required upper-bound form directly. # 3 From rooted paths to trees and block induction 

## 3.1 Replacing child stars 

The following rooted-star replacement is the reduction used by Khuller, Raghavachari, and Young [2, Section 4]; it is also described by Chan [1, Section 2] and Zbarsky [5, Section 2]. We include its proof to make the passage from paths to degree-three trees explicit. 

Proposition 3.1 (Rooted-path reduction) . If Corollary 1.3 holds with a constant C, then MST 3(X) ≤ C MST( X)

for every finite Euclidean point set X.Proof. Root a minimum spanning tree T at an arbitrary vertex. At each vertex v, replace the star joining v to its children by a path starting at v and visiting all its child roots. The rooted-path hypothesis bounds its length by C times the sum of the original child-edge lengths. Every edge of T belongs to exactly one child star, so the sum of all replacement lengths is at most C len( T ). The replacements form a tree. Inducting upward from the leaves, the replacement at v connects the already transformed child subtrees to v, so the union is connected. If v has kv children, its replacement has kv edges, and ∑ 

> v

kv = |X| − 1. No two replacements use the same edge: an edge joins either a parent to an original child or two original siblings, and such a pair has a unique owner. A connected graph with |X| − 1 edges is a tree. Each nonroot vertex has at most two incident edges in its parent’s replacement and at most one in its own replacement. Its degree is at most three. The root has degree at most one. This gives the required degree-three spanning tree.

## 5.1 A direct proof of coverage 

The following decision procedure selects a valid region for every profile. Its branch order need not agree with the numerical order of the region labels. At a threshold equality, either applicable branch may be used. First suppose d4 ≤ 1/2. If d4 ≤ 1/4, use R1. Otherwise 1 /4 ≤ d4 ≤ 1/2. The following sections specify Vi with An ≤ ∥ Vi∥2 on Ri. The retained coordinates are always denoted by their original names d4, . . . , d n. Each Vi is affine. # 8 The global induction and the spanning-tree bound 

The local work proves Theorem 5.1: coverage was established in Section 5.1, and the path mixtures and the 29 vertex checks establish the four-extension bounds on its eight regions. 

Proof of Theorem 1.2, using the base cases below. Section 9 proves the result for 2 ≤ m ≤ 10 using only the local estimates, normalization, and direct terminal constructions. Assume now m ≥ 11 and that the theorem holds for every smaller number of points at least two. If d3 = 0, apply Lemma 9.1 below. Otherwise contract the first two radii to d3 and scale by 1 /d 3. The resulting profile satisfies the normalization for the eight-region cover. Some region supplies a block size n ∈ { 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9}

and the four normalized extensions. Lemma 4.1 restores the original radii and establishes (1). The actual tail has m − n ≥ 11 − 9 = 2 labels and fewer than m labels. The strong induction hypothesis gives its two rooted paths, ending at the two largest-radius tail labels an+1 , a n+2 . Lemma 3.2 combines them with the four local extensions. This proves the inductive step. Corollary 1.3 follows by translation and taking the shorter of the two paths, with the zero- and one-point cases handled directly. Proposition 3.1 then proves Theorem 1.1. 12 8.1 Constructivity and the extent of the result 

As in Zbarsky’s bounded-block reconstruction [5, Section 6], the argument is constructive. The cover is selected by a short sequence of radius comparisons. Every local mixture contains finitely many paths of bounded size, at most 3 · 2 · 7! = 30 ,240 for a nine-point block. For each incoming label and prescribed endpoint, one may enumerate the listed family and choose a shortest path. Positive mixture weights are used to justify its length, not to randomize the construction. Each recursive step removes at least three labels, so the recursion has a linear number of block steps. Together with sorting the radii and the star replacement, this gives a deterministic polynomial-time construction once an MST is supplied. The root-to-path constant proved here lies between 3 /2 and 757 /500. We do not establish that the latter value, the witnesses, or the number of regions is optimal. Nor does the lower limit 3 /2 for rooted paths assert the same lower limit for degree-three spanning trees. The new upper bound concerns total Euclidean edge length in arbitrary dimension; it is separate from bottleneck objectives and bounds that use a fixed planar embedding.
