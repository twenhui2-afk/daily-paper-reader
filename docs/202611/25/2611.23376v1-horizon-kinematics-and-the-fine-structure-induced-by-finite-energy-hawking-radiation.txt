Abstract. Hawking radiation can modify the horizon structure of a black hole through the accompanying loss of mass and global charges. Investigating this process through quan-tum tunneling, we develop a systematic strategy to reconstruct horizon displacements under finite-energy emission, even when their radii are not explicitly known. Local inversion of the horizon condition allows us to follow simple and degenerate roots along sequences of static geometries. As a concrete realization, we consider a nonlinearly charged extension of the Bañados–Teitelboim–Zanelli (BTZ) family, whose rich horizon structure offers an appealing setting to explore this process. Of particular interest is an initial configuration with a dou-ble inner horizon coexisting with a simple event horizon. We show that neutral emission necessarily splits this inner horizon into two simple horizons, revealing an internal horizon fine structure. For charged emission, the dependence of extracted charge on emitted energy determines the separation law and allows channels that preserve or later restore the degener-acy. We construct such channels connecting the same initial and final states through distinct internal histories and sharing the same leading semiclassical tunneling factor. As emission continues, the larger of the newly separated inner horizons can coalesce with the event hori-zon, driving the sequence towards an extremal black hole with vanishing temperature but a finite tunneling probability. Relating the duration of this approach to the radiation fluxes, we find that zero temperature cannot be attained in finite boundary time while a uniform adiabaticity bound holds. We also establish a regime where the geometric expansion and the canonical thermal approximation remain simultaneously controlled near this endpoint. Finally, applying this analysis to the sector reproducing a quantum-backreacted BTZ geom-etry reveals how the leading semiclassical corrections raise the temperature and enhance the neutral-probe horizon emission factor relative to classical BTZ.

– i – 1 Introduction 

Among all physics associated with black holes, Hawking radiation offers one of the clearest links between gravitation and quantum physics. In 1974, Hawking showed that black holes formed by gravitational collapse emit radiation at a temperature determined by their surface gravity [1, 2]. His approach treats quantum fields on a classical collapsing spacetime, showing that a state with no incoming particles gives rise to an outgoing flux at late times. The spectrum reaching infinity is modulated by greybody factors, which account for scattering by the exterior geometry. Beyond predicting radiation from an object classically unable to emit it, this result gave physical content to the analogy between black hole mechanics and thermodynamics [3]. It also fixed the normalization of Bekenstein’s proposal that a black hole possesses an intrinsic entropy directly proportional to its horizon area, an idea motivated by the apparent loss of entropy when matter falls into a black hole [4, 5]. The outgoing radiation, in turn, carries energy away, so the geometry cannot remain unchanged as the black hole loses mass and evaporates. An isolated Schwarzschild black hole offers a simple illustration of this feedback; as its mass decreases, its temperature rises and the emission accelerates. Even so, the estimated evaporation times for stellar masses far exceed the present age of the Universe [6]. The low Hawking temperature of stellar-mass black holes also makes their radiation exceedingly difficult to distinguish from the astrophysical background [2]. While Hawking radiation was originally a purely theoretical prediction, Unruh opened a way to study its analogue in the laboratory by considering sound in a transonic flow [7]. In this setting, sound perturbations propagate on an effective spacetime with a sonic horizon, and where their quanta, the phonons, play the role of the particles emitted in the gravitational case. The idea extends beyond fluids. In a nonlinear optical fiber, for instance, a light pulse can produce a moving perturbation of the refractive index that acts as a horizon for probe light, an arrangement used to observe stimulated analogue Hawking emission [8, 9]. What makes these analogues especially useful is that they bring the conversion of modes near a horizon within experimental reach, allowing a key part of the emission process to be studied under controlled conditions. In the gravitational setting, Hawking radiation also raises a question about what be-comes of the initial quantum state. If a black hole formed from a pure state evaporates completely, leaving behind radiation in a mixed state, the evolution would not be unitary [10]. A major advance in describing the emission process came with a series of seminal works [11–14] that laid the foundations of the Kraus–Parikh–Wilczek (KPW) approach, a semi-classical framework that describes Hawking radiation as quantum tunneling across the event horizon. By enforcing energy conservation, the approach accounts for the energy carried away by the outgoing shell and the accompanying shift of the horizon. It is this backreaction that gives rise to corrections to the leading thermal tunneling factor. These departures from thermality have been discussed in connection with the information paradox [15], although the emission probabilities alone do not establish whether the full evolution is unitary. This tunneling picture can be extended to anti-de Sitter spacetimes, with an early application to Schwarzschild–AdS black holes [16]. Closer to our problem is Medved’s analysis of neutral emission from the electrically charged Bañados–Teitelboim–Zanelli (BTZ) black hole, which also considered the approach to extremality [17]. For rotating BTZ, Wu and Jiang accounted for the angular momentum carried away by the emitted shell [18]. These examples bring changes in the black hole parameters into the calculation of the emission – 1 – probability. Here we turn the question around and ask what emission does to the horizon structure itself. The nonlinearly charged BTZ family of Ref. [19] is particularly well suited to this question, since it admits configurations in which simple and degenerate horizons coexist. The loss of mass and charge can then affect not only the positions of the horizons but also their number and degeneracy. A black hole can lose charge and still move closer to extremality, as Hiscock and Weems found by following the coupled evolution of mass and charge in sufficiently massive Reissner– Nordström black holes early in their evaporation [20]. In the BTZ family considered here, the charge carried away for a given loss of energy selects a path through the stationary configurations. Following that path lets us see how horizon degeneracy changes along the way and whether the black hole moves toward extremality. The rate of energy loss then sets the pace of the evolution. As extremality draws near, the effect of a single emission becomes especially important. Kraus and Wilczek showed that self-interaction corrections can substantially modify the spectrum in this regime [21]. The background may evolve slowly enough on the timescale of the outgoing modes for an adiabatic description [22], yet a single quantum can carry away an appreciable fraction of the energy above extremality, making the canonical approximation unreliable [23]. More recently, Brown et al. found that quantum fluctuations of the near-extremal geometry can substantially alter the emission spectrum of four-dimensional charged black holes [24]. 

Organization of this work. We begin in Sec. 2 by studying Hawking emission at finite energy within the KPW description and developing a local inversion of the horizon-defining equation, arriving at formal series expressions for the tunneling probabilities and finite hori-zon displacements. This construction is extended to degenerate horizons in Sec. 3, where we examine their splitting and possible subsequent coalescence as mass and charge are extracted. We then turn to the nonlinearly charged BTZ family in Sec. 4, following the horizon histo-ries selected by different emission profiles. The approach to outer coalescence motivates the analysis of Sec. 5, which examines the limiting tunneling exponent alongside the duration of the process and the conditions for an adiabatic and thermal description. A complementary application to the sector reproducing the quantum-backreacted BTZ geometry is presented in Sec. 6. We close with a discussion of the results and further directions in Sec. 7, leaving the detailed coefficient formulas and complementary calculations to the appendices.

3 Horizon splitting and coalescence under emission 

At a degenerate horizon, the linear term in the radial displacement vanishes, so the ordinary inverse of Sec. 2 must be replaced by a Puiseux-type expansion. We use it to determine the nearby real roots, when they exist, and how their separation depends on the emission channel. A sequence of quasistationary emissions can change both the positions and the number of horizons. More precisely, an initially degenerate inner horizon may split into two simple zeros, one of which can later merge with the event horizon. Figure 1 illustrates one such sequence, allowing the profile of f (r) to deform as energy and charge are lost.

7 Discussion and concluding remarks 

The KPW approach to Hawking radiation has found extensive application in black hole physics by incorporating energy conservation into the semiclassical description of emission. Revisiting the horizon condition within this framework reveals how the accompanying loss of mass and charge can affect the horizon locations, their number and causal nature. For the analytic static spacetimes of the form (2.1), wherever the local mass inversion is well defined, the Lagrange–Bürmann formula and its extension through Puiseux series provide local expressions for the motion of simple and degenerate roots without requiring an explicit solution for the horizon radii. Furthermore, composing this inverse with the appropriate gravitational entropy determines the leading KPW exponent, provided the stationary first law holds along the emission path. At the same time, the coefficient formulas developed in Sec. 2 and Appendices A and C make the corrections to the thermal exponent accessible to arbitrary order. – 34 – A natural extension of this construction arises when a horizon becomes degenerate, since an emission can then change the number of nearby real roots. The corresponding critical curve consists of the mass and charge values for which the double horizon exists. The sign of the mass difference from this curve, together with the radial curvature of the mass function, determines whether two simple horizons emerge or the nearby real pair is absent. The charge emission profile is therefore an essential part of the description. Once the pair exists, its separation is proportional to the square root of the magnitude of this mass difference, giving a square-root dependence on the emitted energy for a transverse departure and a linear dependence for a quadratic one. The double horizon itself is preserved along paths that remain on the critical curve throughout. We found a particularly well-suited case study in the recently presented logarithmic deformation of the BTZ black hole [19], which realizes these possibilities within a single matter theory. Starting with a double inner horizon and a separate simple event horizon, we show that neutral emission necessarily splits the former into two simple horizons, revealing an internal horizon fine structure. The behavior is richer for charged channels, which allow for immediate splitting, delayed recovery of an inner pair, or persistent inner degeneracy, while the event horizon remains simple. Here the analyticity of the charge emission profile has a useful consequence: the outer radius and entropy exponent remain locally analytic through these internal transitions, so the creation or loss of an inner pair produces no nonanalytic marker in the leading horizon emission factor. An instructive comparison is given in Fig. 5, where we present one channel that preserves the double inner horizon, while another separates it into two simple horizons and later brings them together again. Both connect the same initial and final states and consequently have the same leading KPW exponent, despite their different intermediate horizon configurations. Through a formal continuation of the process, we find that the larger of the newly sep-arated inner horizons can coalesce with the event horizon, driving the sequence towards an extremal black hole. Its temperature then tends to zero but, surprisingly, the entropy differ-ence and hence the leading tunneling exponent have a finite limit. For charged approaches tangent to the outer critical curve, retaining the electric work in the residue is essential to recover this behavior. Smaller positive masses below the coalescence value still admit the continuation of the innermost root as a simple event horizon. This critical mass therefore ends the tracked outer branch, with a transition to the smaller horizon requiring a dynamical description. This approach towards extremality has an interesting counterpart in Ref. [47]. The fully nonlinear evolutions of the four-dimensional Einstein–Maxwell–charged-scalar system produce near-extremal black holes from regular, horizon-free initial data, with this character independently identified through radiation at future null infinity. There, the near-extremal regime emerges during formation, whereas in our case Hawking emission can drive an already formed black hole towards a degenerate event horizon. A common feature of the two settings is the role of the relative loss of mass and charge. In their evolutions these fluxes are deter-mined dynamically, while the emission profiles considered here make their influence on the horizon motion explicit. The prospect of reaching extremality naturally raises the question of how long this ap-proach takes, since the entropy exponent does not specify the interval between successive emissions. To that end, the energy and charge balance examined in Sec. 5.2 relates the charge emission profile to the ratio of charge and energy fluxes, while the luminosity deter-mines how quickly the path is traversed. A sufficiently rapid decrease of the luminosity makes – 35 – the approach asymptotic in time. Adiabaticity imposes a stronger restriction, however, be-cause the inverse surface gravity also diverges and the geometry must evolve slowly on this increasing time scale. More generally, the cooling bound in Eq. (5.14) excludes reaching zero temperature in finite boundary time while a uniform bound on adiabaticity remains valid, independently of the particular luminosity law. Interestingly, even such a slow approach need not remain thermal. A quantum with energy of order TH can eventually carry away an appreciable fraction of the energy remaining before coalescence, so that the temperature can change substantially during a single emission. For neutral emission, this effect is captured by the exact bound (5.17), which quantifies the suppression relative to the Boltzmann estimate without truncating the entropy change at quadratic order. Near extremality, the available thermal reservoir is measured by the entropy above the limiting configuration, rather than by the total entropy [23]. Requiring this entropy excess to be large while remaining within the quadratic radial expansion gives the overlap interval in Eq. (5.23). This interval widens with increasing charge magnitude at fixed couplings, but contracts towards triple degeneracy. In the latter case, the geometric upper scale decreases faster than the thermal lower scale. To examine the thermal regime near a triple horizon, the mass–radius relation must therefore be expanded through cubic order in the displacement from the critical radius. Our final application concerns the one-horizon sector, which reproduces the quantum-backreacted BTZ geometry of NMG [41]. It offers an opportunity to examine Hawking emission from a black hole whose geometry already incorporates a semiclassical effect. At the matched background and with the same normalization of time, both realizations give the same temperature and leading horizon emission factor for a neutral minimally coupled probe. At fixed seed mass, the leading semiclassical correction raises the temperature, even when the corrected horizon is smaller than the BTZ horizon. Following a finite emission in the Einstein–NLED realization at fixed couplings gives, in addition, a positive logarithmic correction to the BTZ entropy exponent. The resulting exponent recovers the corrected temperature in the limit of small emitted energy. This enhancement relative to BTZ coexists with suppression relative to the initial thermal estimate of the corrected black hole, since the NLED configuration cools as energy is extracted. These results apply within the perturbative regime specified in Sec. 6. Following the family obtained through semiclassical backreaction in NMG [41] would instead change the quantum source with the mass and require the energy and entropy of that theory for the finite-emission calculation. An interesting question is what happens if the initial state is already an extremal black hole. In the family studied here, neutral mass loss from the outer critical curve removes the double outer horizon, leaving the previously innermost root as a simple event horizon. A charged path along that curve, on the other hand, preserves extremality within the sta-tionary family as the mass and charge magnitude decrease. To recover two distinct outer horizons, the remaining mass must lie above the critical mass at the new charge. A trans-verse departure into this region requires negative horizon energy at leading order, outside the ordinary channels considered here, while tangential departures must be decided at higher orders. The role of this energy–charge balance recalls classical absorption tests of cosmic censorship [48, 49], with the distinction that the smaller horizon survives here and the issue is a transition between horizon configurations. Determining a physical emission rate for any of these paths calls for a calculation of tunneling directly from the degenerate state [39]. One of the main remaining questions is which of these stationary histories can be realized during evaporation. This requires the radiation spectrum and an analysis of the exterior’s – 36 – linear stability. Backscattering in the exterior, or reflection at the AdS boundary, can return energy and, for charged radiation, charge to the hole, changing the net fluxes that select the emission path. Alongside this scattering problem, the coupled gravitational and NLED per-turbations would determine whether growing modes can disrupt the slow evolution assumed here. Charged-scalar studies of other three-dimensional AdS black holes illustrate the de-pendence of superradiance and mode stability on the matter model and boundary conditions [50, 51]. The fate of the internal horizon fine structure also depends on how these perturbations evolve inside the black hole. The mass inflation mechanism of Poisson and Israel [52] and the analysis of strong cosmic censorship for linearly charged BTZ [53] make this a necessary part of the dynamical problem. Semiclassical effects can also destabilize an inner horizon with vanishing surface gravity, as shown for stationary regular black holes in Ref. [54]. The role of time dependence is illustrated by the finite renormalized stress tensor obtained for trapped regions of finite lifetime in Ref. [55], within the s-wave Polyakov approximation. Bringing these effects into the present family would help determine which features of its horizon fine structure persist during evaporation. Finally, a natural extension of this study is to account for non-static spacetimes. Re-cent calculations of near-extremal Kerr–Newman evaporation at fixed electric charge show how the competition between s-wave and superradiant emission, with quantum corrections included, can slow the late-time evolution [56]. Closer to our setting, generalized Chern– Simons interactions offer a way to support charged rotating geometries by supplying force-free electromagnetic currents without contributing directly to the stress tensor [57, 58]. This approach accommodates the original charged rotating BTZ solution and extends to nonlinear electrodynamics, with further applications to rotating AdS and Lifshitz black holes [59, 60]. These constructions suggest a concrete route to rotating counterparts of the family studied here through an enlargement of its matter sector, allowing angular-momentum extraction to join the loss of mass and charge in selecting paths through the critical configurations. A sepa-rate extension to genuinely time-dependent geometries can build on the tunneling description of evolving trapping horizons in spherical symmetry [36]. In both directions, an appealing question is whether the radiation itself favors histories that preserve horizon degeneracy or lead towards extremality.
