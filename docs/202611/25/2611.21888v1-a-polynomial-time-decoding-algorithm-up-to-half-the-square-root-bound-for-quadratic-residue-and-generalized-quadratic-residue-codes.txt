# A polynomial-time decoding algorithm up to half the square-root bound for quadratic residue and generalized quadratic residue codes 

Philippe Gaborit ∗ Philippe Lebacque †

November 18, 2026 

Abstract 

Binary quadratic residue (QR) codes, introduced by Prange in 1958, are cyclic codes of prime length p ≡ ± 1 (mod 8) and dimension (p + 1) /2 whose minimum distance satisfies the square-root bound d ≥ √p. They include the Hamming code [7 , 4, 3] and the Golay code [23 , 12 , 7] , and were generalized by van Lint and MacWilliams to generalized quadratic residue (GQR) codes of prime-power length q, for which the bound d ≥ √q still holds and is attained when q is a square. In coding theory, one usually first finds a lower bound on the minimum distance of a family of codes, and then a polynomial-time algorithm decoding up to half of this bound, as for BCH, Reed–Muller or Gabidulin codes. Until now, GQR codes had resisted this approach: no such algorithm was known, apart from individual short lengths. We give a deterministic algorithm which, for every odd prime power q ≡ ± 1(mod 8) , corrects every error pattern of weight at most ⌊(√q − 1) /2⌋ in the binary GQR code of length q, in O(q9/2) operations in Fq . We start from a description of QR codes due to Helleseth, which we extend to GQR codes, and in which the syndrome gives the values of the quadratic character of the error-locator polynomial. We then recover this polynomial by adapting to GQR codes the tools that Kopparty developed for recovering polynomials from noisy character values, with the decoding of dual BCH codes as an application. Since the square-root bound is attained when q is a square, this radius is the largest that can be guaranteed uniformly on the class of GQR codes. 

# 1 Introduction 

1.1 Quadratic residue codes 

Quadratic residue (QR) codes were introduced by Prange in 1958 [30] and form one of the oldest families of algebraic error-correcting codes. The idea of the construction is elementary. Take a prime p, index the p coordinates of a binary word by the elements of Fp, and split the nonzero elements of Fp into the set Q of squares and the set N of non-squares. A binary cyclic code of length p is determined by the set of exponents a such that ζa is a root of all its codewords, where ζ is a primitive p-th root of unity; the QR code is the cyclic code for which this set is Q. The construction makes sense as soon as 2 is a square modulo p, that is for p ≡ ± 1 (mod 8) , and it gives a code of dimension (p + 1) /2, hence of rate just above 1/2.The precise definition is recalled in Section 2. Van Lint and MacWilliams [38] extended the construction from primes to prime powers

Duadic codes [25] are the cyclic codes of odd length n obtained by replacing the pair (Q, N ) by any partition of the nonzero residues modulo n into two unions of cyclotomic classes exchanged by a multiplication x 7 → ax ; they exist when 2 is a square modulo n and still satisfy a square-root bound. Double circulant quadratic residue codes [21, 14] are the codes of length 2p and dimension p with generator matrix [I | W ], where W is the circulant matrix defined by the squares modulo p; they exist for every odd prime p, in particular for p ≡ ± 3 (mod 8) where there is no QR code, and they also satisfy square-root bounds [6, 18]. In this paper we only consider QR and GQR codes.

1.3 A remarkable family of codes 

A bound in √q may look modest, but QR codes are remarkable in five respects, which we recall in turn: their parameters, their symmetries, their weight enumerators, their connection with number theory and lattices, and their relation with quantum codes. 

Parameters. At short lengths the parameters of QR codes are excellent: the Hamming code 

[7 , 4, 3] and the Golay code [23 , 12 , 7] , which are both perfect, and the [47 , 24 , 11] code are QR codes [26]. They remain good well beyond these lengths, and they exist for many lengths for which there is no BCH code. For instance the QR code of length 103 has parameters 

[103 , 52 , 19] , whereas shortening the BCH code [127 , 71 , 19] to the same length and the same minimum distance only gives a [103 , 47 , 19] code, and the QR code of length 199 improved the best minimum distance known for its length and dimension [36]. Beyond length 200 , however, the minimum distances and estimates available [36] suggest that QR codes fall behind BCH codes of comparable length and rate. 2Symmetries. Add an overall parity check to the QR code of length p, and index the p + 1 

coordinates of the extended code by the projective line Fp ∪ {∞} . By the Gleason–Prange theorem, the extended code is invariant under the group PSL 2(p) acting on the projective line [26, Ch. 16]. This group is much larger than the group of cyclic shifts, and it is doubly transitive, so that all the coordinates of the extended code play the same role. For the lengths 

24 and 48 , the supports of the codewords of any given weight even form 5-designs [1]. 

Weight enumerators. For p ≡ 7 (mod 8) the extended QR code is self-dual and doubly even, that is, all its weights are divisible by 4 [26, 31]. A doubly-even self-dual code of length n

has minimum distance at most 4⌊n/ 24 ⌋ + 4 , and is called extremal when this value is reached; by Gleason’s theorem, the weight enumerator of an extremal code is completely determined by its length [31]. The extended QR codes are extremal for all their lengths up to 48 , namely 

8, 24 , 32 and 48 , and again for the lengths 80 and 104 [31], so that their weight enumerators are known explicitly. The extended QR code of length 48 is moreover the only extremal doubly-even self-dual code of this length [20]. 

Connection with number theory and lattices. QR codes are arithmetic objects. Their weights are sums of Legendre symbols, which count the points of hyperelliptic curves over Fp,and this is how the Weil bound gives back the square-root bound [17, 18]; the asymptotically bad subfamilies of [39] are obtained from the Chebotarev density theorem. These very good codes are also connected to very good lattices: the extended Hamming code [8 , 4, 4] gives the lattice E8, the extended Golay code [24 , 12 , 8] gives the Leech lattice, and the extended ternary QR code of length 48 gives an extremal unimodular lattice in dimension 48 [10]. 

Quantum codes. Finally, for p ≡ 7 (mod 8) the QR code of length p contains its dual, which is its subcode of even weight words [26]. The Calderbank–Shor–Steane construction then gives a quantum code encoding one qubit into p qubits with minimum distance dp; for p = 7 and 

p = 23 these are the Steane code [[7 , 1, 3]] and the quantum Golay code [[23 , 1, 7]] [35]. 

1.4 Distance bounds and decoding 

For most classical families of codes, a lower bound on the minimum distance has been followed by a polynomial-time algorithm decoding up to half of this bound. BCH codes were introduced by Hocquenghem [19] and by Bose and Ray-Chaudhuri [4] together with the BCH bound, which guarantees a minimum distance at least the designed distance δ; Peterson [29] and Gorenstein and Zierler [15] then gave an algebraic decoder correcting ⌊(δ − 1) /2⌋ errors, made efficient by the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm [3, 27]. Reed–Muller codes were defined by Muller [28], and Reed [32] gave the same year a majority-logic decoder correcting up to half their minimum distance. In rank metric, Delsarte [11] introduced maximum rank distance codes as spaces of bilinear forms, and Gabidulin [13] later decoded them up to half their minimum rank distance. Gabidulin’s codes are linear over an extension field, a point of view that Delsarte did not adopt, so the two families do not coincide exactly, but the sequence is the same: first a bound, then a decoder. Until now, QR codes were an exception: no polynomial-time algorithm decoding up to half the square-root bound was known. The existing algebraic decoders treat one length at a time. They recover the syndromes that the code does not provide through Newton’s identities and then compute the error-locator polynomial; this was done by Elia [12] for the Golay code, and by Reed, Truong and their coauthors for the lengths 31 , 41 , 47 , 71 , 79 , 89 and 

97 [34, 33, 16, 8, 37]. Gröbner bases can automate the derivation of such formulas [9, 2], and permutation decoding uses the large automorphism group for selected parameters [5], but none of these methods comes with a complexity bound polynomial in the length. The BCH decoder does not help either: it needs consecutive elements in the defining set, and if the values of the quadratic character are modelled as independent balanced signs, the longest runs of consecutive squares modulo p have length O(log p), far below the square-root scale; this 3is only a heuristic, the arithmetic of such runs being itself a difficult problem [22]. Finally, generic decoders such as information-set decoding have a cost of the form 2Θ( √n) for Θ( √n)

errors at rate 1/2 [7]. There is one more reason to look for a decoder of QR codes. Kudekar, Kumar, Mon-delli, Pfister, Şaşoğlu and Urbanke [24] proved that any sequence of binary linear codes of increasing lengths, whose rates converge and whose permutation groups are doubly transitive, achieves capacity on the binary erasure channel under bit-MAP decoding. By the Gleason– Prange theorem this applies to extended QR codes, as noted in [24]. QR codes are therefore asymptotically optimal for erasures, which are corrected by linear algebra, although nothing comparable is known for their minimum distance or for the correction of errors. 

1.5 Contribution 

We give the first polynomial-time decoding algorithm valid for the whole family of binary QR and GQR codes. 

Main result (Theorem 3.1). For every odd prime power q ≡ ± 1 (mod 8) , there is a determin-istic algorithm that corrects any ⌊(√q − 1) /2⌋ errors in the binary QR code of length q when 

q is prime, and more generally in the binary GQR code of length q, in O(q9/2) operations in 

Fq.The algorithm has two ingredients. The first one is a description of QR codes due to Helleseth [17], different from the classical one, in which the error positions are elements of Fp and are the roots of a polynomial fE with coefficients in Fp; in this description, the syndrome of the received word gives the value of the quadratic character of fE at every point which is not an error position. Helleseth’s description is given for prime lengths, and we extend it to all GQR codes. Decoding then amounts to recovering a polynomial of small degree over Fq from the values of its quadratic character. The second ingredient is the set of tools recently developed by Kopparty [23] for recov-ering low-degree polynomials from noisy character values, which he applied in particular to the decoding of dual BCH codes; they combine Hasse derivatives, multiplicity estimates and Stepanov’s polynomial method. We do not apply Kopparty’s algorithm as it stands: we adapt his method to the case of GQR codes, in which the character values are known exactly outside the roots of fE and are not known at these roots. Kopparty’s algorithm recovers a monic squarefree polynomial of degree at most (ϵ/ 16) √q from the values of its quadratic character, a proportion 1/8 − ϵ of which may be wrong [23, Theorem 3.1]. In our situation the polynomial is split and the only unknown values are at its roots; with parameters chosen for this case we reach the degree ⌊(√q − 1) /2⌋ which is needed to decode up to half the square-root bound, and we do not need to factor polynomials. We write a linear system whose unknowns are a polynomial and its Hasse derivatives, we show that a suitable power of fE is a nonzero solu-tion, and that the error positions can be read on the multiplicities of the roots of any nonzero solution. This result places GQR codes in the classical situation described above. The algorithm applies to the whole class of GQR codes and decodes up to half the square-root bound; since this bound is attained when q is a square, no larger decoding radius can be guaranteed for the class as a whole, and for q a square the algorithm corrects up to half the true minimum distance. To the best of our knowledge, the existence of a polynomial-time decoding algorithm valid for the whole family had been open since the introduction of QR codes, more than sixty years ago. For QR codes of prime length, where the minimum distance is a priori larger than 

√p but no better bound is available, decoding beyond √p/ 2 errors remains an open problem.
