# Weighted Proportional Toll Sharing on General Highway Networks 

# Erfang Shan Yan Chu

Abstract 

We extend the Segments Equal Sharing (SES) method of Wu et al. (2024) from line highways to general highway networks, including networks with cycles. The input is an exogenously specified profile of route flows. We introduce the Weighted Proportional Sharing (WPS) method, which distributes each route’s mileage revenue in proportion to weighted segment lengths and its fixed-surcharge revenue in proportion to positive segment surcharges. Route SES is recovered under homogeneous weights within each revenue component. We show that WPS is the sum of two weighted Shapley allocations and that the underlying route games extend the dividend and Shapley representation of linear SES. Two axiomatic characterizations identify WPS. The first uses additivity and proportional treatment of segments used on every positive-revenue route. The second combines sub-highway efficiency and weighted average balanced contributions under a route cancellation rule. We also extend Segments Proportional Sharing to general networks, identify it with the 𝜏 value of the corresponding route game, and characterize it axiomatically. For Segments Compensated Sharing we characterize a weighted average tree allocation by consistency across rooted branch settlements and averaging under a specified root lottery. A cyclic example shows that different route splits can change the allocation even when origin–destination volumes and aggregate toll revenue are held fixed. 

Keywords Highway toll allocation, route flows, weighted Shapley value, axiomatic charac-terization, networks with cycles 

# 1 Introduction 

An integrated tolling system may collect a single payment for a trip that crosses segments operated by several concessionaires. A settlement rule specifies how the collected revenue is attributed to the participating segments. This is a revenue allocation problem among segments, distinct from the problem of setting tolls or allocating infrastructure costs among users. The 1arXiv:2611.22892v1 [econ.TH] 17 Nov 2026 distinction matters when the total payment is pooled and the agreement among operators determines which revenues they can claim. 

# 1.1 Related literature and contribution 

Highway cost allocation and highway toll settlement assign money to different agents. In the former, the users pay toward the cost of making the road available. Kuipers et al. (2013) define highway cost games for users who require consecutive sections of a common road and develop formulas for the Shapley value and nucleolus. Sudhölter and Zarzuelo (2017) characterize cost-sharing methods for highway problems, and Li et al. (2026) introduce weights for heterogeneous users in highway construction cost sharing. In our model the customers’ payments have already been collected. The players are road segments, and the two weights of a segment describe its eligibility and relative share in the mileage and surcharge components. Work on other transport systems illustrates why the player set and the characteristic function must be chosen together. Rosenthal (2017) studies the allocation of rapid-transit costs among users travelling on shortest paths in a general network. Estañ et al. (2021) studies how cities served by a tram line can share a fixed system cost, including proportional and equal division principles. These applications establish precedents for using network information and proportionality in transport cost allocation. The settlement problem here instead starts from the realised or supplied use of each highway route and asks how that route’s collected revenue is credited to the segments. Wu et al. (2024) study this problem on a line highway. Their first rule, the Segments Equal Sharing (SES) method, distributes the toll associated with each trip equally among the segments it uses. They characterize SES by additivity, efficiency, an inessential segment property and segment symmetry, and identify it with the Shapley value of a segments allocation game. Their Exits Equal Sharing and Entrances Equal Sharing methods address settlements based on exit and entrance tolls, respectively. Soto-Rodríguez et al. (2026) further study the linear setting and introduce Segments Proportional Sharing and Segments Compensated Sharing, with representations involving the 𝜏 -value and the average tree solution. The cooperative-game analysis draws on graph-restricted games (Myerson, 1977; Owen, 1986) and weighted Shapley values (Kalai and Samet, 1987). A graph restriction uses communication links to determine which groups can cooperate. In our model the route data specify directly which sets of segments generate the revenue being divided. This distinction matters when a graph has cycles. For a physical route that is disabled, an alternative connection elsewhere in the communication graph need not restore any of that route’s revenue. It also matters for fixed surcharges, because two eligible segments can lie on the same route even if a zero-surcharge segment separates them. 21.2 Approach and organization 

Two features of general highway networks motivate our extension of SES. First, a pair of interchanges can be connected by several routes. An origin–destination total may therefore be compatible with several different patterns of segment use. Second, the toll paid on a route can combine a mileage charge and fixed surcharges associated with particular segments. Equal division of the combined payment treats these two charging bases identically. A weighted rule can instead distinguish the length and attributes of the segments that support mileage charging from the facilities for which a fixed surcharge is collected. We model each route and its class-specific flow as an input. Our Weighted Proportional Sharing (WPS) method splits each route’s mileage revenue by weighted length and its surcharge revenue by the applicable positive surcharges. This extends SES in two directions. Equal sharing can be applied route by route on an arbitrary network, and component-specific weights allow that rule to distinguish segments with heterogeneous attributes. On a line, route SES agrees exactly with the rule of Wu et al. (2024) when interval toll revenues are matched. Their path-dividend formula and Shapley representation are special cases of the results below. Our first axiomatic characterization also recovers their characterization when specialized to the line and equal weights. The second characterization uses the allocation effects of cancelling specified routes that use a segment. It gives a fairness interpretation in terms of reciprocal impacts, averaged over a group of segments that shares its route revenues internally. The operation holds all unaffected route flows fixed, so it can be used on a network with cycles without modelling an equilibrium response. The resulting axioms describe alternative settlement principles for a given flow profile. Their implications concern the resulting allocation within the model. Section 2 introduces TU games, coalition dividends, Shapley values and graph restriction. Section 3 sets out the model and allocation rules. Section 4 gives the cooperative-game representation. Section 5 states the two WPS characterizations. Section 6 extends SPS and SCS. It characterizes SPS and provides a recursive characterization of a weighted average tree rule for SCS. Section 7 illustrates the effect of a route split on a cyclic network, and Section 8 discusses the scope for further extensions. Proofs are collected in Appendix A. Appendix B tests logical independence for the WPS, SPS and SCS characterizations.

# 3.3 Settlement inputs and aggregation 

There are two levels of data in the model. A tariff-derived route record lists the route, vehicle volumes by class and the attributes of its segments. It gives both components through (3.3). A revenue record can instead give the realised amounts for each route and component directly. In 7either case the network may admit unused routes. A route in the admissible family with zero recorded revenue contributes zero and remains available as an input to a different profile. A single OD entry can be used as a route entry only when the relevant route is known. When several routes are used for one OD pair, assigning the whole OD total to one route changes the segment-use information, even if the aggregate payment is preserved. By contrast, multiple records that have the same segment set can be combined by adding their component revenues. Their contributions to WPS and to coalition worths depend on the same weight ratios and eligibility sets. This aggregation can be carried out separately for each accounting period before combining the periods. Equation (3.6) also shows the data required for an implementation under additive tariffs. For mileage, it is enough to know the sum of class-weighted flows on each segment. For surcharges, it is enough to know the total count on each segment. A single pass through the records can accumulate these two quantities. Route-level records are still needed when one wants to evaluate the coalition games, compare WPS with route SES, or determine which other segment allocations change when a route is cancelled. The numerical illustration distinguishes these uses of the data. 

# 3.4 Scope of the settlement model 

The model applies to a finite connected highway network, including networks with cycles, over a specified settlement period. It takes as input the simple routes used during that period and either their class-specific flows under the tariff in (3.3) or their recorded nonnegative revenues by component. Direction-specific records can be included as separate routes. The communication graphs in this paper are undirected. The segment weights and the eligibility of each route for settlement are fixed within a profile and are common to the compared profiles in the axioms that require fixed attributes. Coalition worth is evaluated using the specified routes. In the mileage game a coalition earns the revenues of the specified routes it contains. The surcharge game evaluates claims to already generated revenue using the eligible part of each route. This interpretation allows a positive-surcharge segment to receive a settlement even if travel on the full route also required a zero-surcharge segment. For the closure comparisons, routes using the closed segment are cancelled and the other route records retain their original revenues. The resulting statements concern a settlement at given traffic and tariff inputs.

# 4.1 Physical connectivity and surcharge eligibility 

The full-route game is component additive on 𝐿 (𝐺 ), since a connected route contained in a coalition lies in a single connected component. Proposition 2.1 and the route-dividend formula agree on the implication for disconnected coalitions. The route formula gives a stronger restriction: even a connected coalition has zero dividend unless a recorded route has precisely that segment set. As a result, a connected coalition containing a physical cycle can have positive worth and zero dividend. For a route consisting of three consecutive segments with surcharges (2, 0, 1), suppose that the route generates a fixed-surcharge revenue of 90. The full-route surcharge game assigns 9this revenue to the unanimity game on all three segments. The projected surcharge game has dividend 90 on the pair of outer segments and WPS assigns them 60 and 30. The pair is disconnected in the induced graph on positive-surcharge segments. If that projected game were graph restricted on the induced graph, the worth of the pair would be the sum of two zero singleton worths. The projected game is therefore interpreted as a settlement game for specified route claims. The distinction between physical routes and communication links also explains the choice of perturbation in Section 5. As a simple graph-game example, suppose that three segment players form a triangle in the communication graph and that the game is 𝑢 {1,2}. After deletion of the communication link between 1 and 2, the grand coalition can still connect them through 3, so the resulting graph restriction has worth one for the grand coalition. Under route cancellation, deleting the only specified contribution that uses both 1 and 2 makes all coalition worths zero. The second characterization below is formulated in terms of route cancellation.

# 7.2 What the illustration identifies 

The two scenarios hold both OD demand and aggregate toll receipts fixed. Their different settlements identify the effect of the route split at the stated tariff. They also show why testing a settlement rule against aggregate receipts alone is insufficient. For example, a rule that credited the 𝑠 –𝑡 revenue to one predetermined route would give the same allocation under both scenarios and would miss the change in segment use. The illustration is a constructed comparison of allocation mechanisms. An application to a particular highway would require route records or a separately justified estimate of the route split. 

# 8 Conclusions and further extensions 

We extended the SES approach of Wu et al. (2024) from a line to a general highway network with given route flows. Route SES retains equal division of each route’s toll among the segments it uses. WPS extends that principle by distinguishing mileage weights from surcharge weights. It has a weighted Shapley representation for each component and two axiomatic characterizations. On a line with equal weights, the model recovers the 2024 SES rule, its route-interval dividend formula, its Shapley representation and its characterization based on additivity, efficiency, inessential segments and segment symmetry. Cycles enter through the coexistence of multiple routes. The settlement rule and the proofs apply after their flows have been specified. 

Extensions of the other 2024 methods. The Exits Equal Sharing and Entrances Equal Sharing methods of Wu et al. (2024) can be extended by the same allocation framework once the settlement agreement specifies the eligible segments for each exit or entrance. For example, let 𝑈 (𝑥 ) be a specified set of upstream segments eligible for revenue collected at exit 

𝑥 . Aggregate the payments collected at 𝑥 and divide that amount among 𝑈 (𝑥 ), equally or in proportion to specified positive weights. An analogous downstream set 𝐷 (𝑎 ) allocates the payments collected at entrance 𝑎 . On a directed line, choosing the prefix ending at the exit segment or the suffix beginning at the entrance segment reproduces Exits Equal Sharing or Entrances Equal Sharing, respectively, under equal weights. A general network can support different eligibility policies, especially where routes form cycles. Establishing counterparts of the original axioms for particular eligibility policies remains an open question. 

The 2026 results. We established the SPS– 𝜏 equivalence and a route-based axiomatic characterization of SPS on general networks. The pooled model recovers the SPS result of Soto-Rodríguez et al. (2026) on a line, and the componentwise model respects surcharge eligibility. For SCS, the weighted average of DFS tree allocations recovers the linear rule in 19 the pooled equal-weight case. Its recursive characterization uses efficiency conditional on a designated root, consistency across the resulting branch settlements, and averaging under a specified initial root lottery. 

Scope and open questions. On networks with several possible routes for the same OD pair, OD totals alone may leave the segment allocation undetermined. Applying these rules in that situation requires route records, route shares supplied by an external traffic assignment, or explicit scenarios. When tolls, congestion or closures lead drivers to change routes, a model of reassignment is needed to obtain the new profile. Coalition worth under alternative route choices is a different modelling problem. Extending the rules to games in which coalitions can reroute traffic, or to settlements with negative route revenues, requires separate analysis. For SCS, the recursive characterization treats the communication graph and root lotteries as inputs. A further question is whether observable changes in toll revenues or route closures can identify those inputs. The component fairness axiom of Soto-Rodríguez et al. (2026) compares the average losses on two sides of a severed connection. A single closure in a general graph may leave the communication graph connected. Even a physical three-arm tree has a triangular segment communication graph, so deleting one communication link creates no partition. Extending that fairness principle would require a different comparison and an economic justification of the surcharge shortcuts and tree probabilities.
