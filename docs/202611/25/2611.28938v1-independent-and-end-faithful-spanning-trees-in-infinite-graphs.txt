# Independent and end-faithful spanning trees in infinite graphs 

# Rodrigo Santos Monteiro 

Abstract 

Spanning trees rooted at the same vertex are independent if, for each vertex other than the root, the paths from the root to that vertex are pairwise distinct and internally vertex-disjoint. Zehavi and Itai conjectured that, for every positive integer k, every finite 

k-connected graph has k independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. We show that every 2-connected graph, finite or infinite, has two independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. For locally finite 2-connected graphs, both trees can be chosen end-faithful. We also prove that every locally finite 3-connected chordal graph has three independent end-faithful spanning trees. Counterexamples show that local finiteness is essential for the version with independent end-faithful spanning trees. We further show that every 3-connected graph with a universal vertex or a finite vertex cover has three independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Keywords: Independent spanning trees, infinite graphs, end-faithful spanning trees, chordal graphs.

# 1 Introduction 

All graphs in this paper are simple. We use the terminology and notation of [5]. For a positive integer k, a graph G is k-connected if |V (G)| ≥ k + 1 and G − X is connected for every set X ⊆ V (G) with |X| < k . For a tree T and vertices x, y ∈ V (T ), write xT y for the unique x–y path in T . A spanning tree of G is a connected subgraph T without cycles such that V (T ) = V (G). Specifying a vertex r ∈ V (T ) makes T a rooted tree , with root r.

Definition 1.1. Let k be a positive integer, let G be a graph, and let r ∈ V (G). Spanning trees T1, . . . , T k of G are independent , rooted at r, if, for every v ∈ V (G) \ { r} and distinct 

i, j ∈ { 1, . . . , k }, the paths rT iv and rT j v are distinct and satisfy V (rT iv) ∩ V (rT j v) = {r, v }.

Conjecture 1.2 (Zehavi and Itai [18]) . For every positive integer k, every finite k-connected graph has k independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

The case k = 1 follows from the existence of a spanning tree in every finite connected graph. Itai and Rodeh [15, Section 7.2] proved the case k = 2. Cheriyan and Maheshwari [3] and, independently, Zehavi and Itai [18, Theorem 1.2] proved the case k = 3, and Curran, Lee and Yu [4, Theorem 1.1] proved the case k = 4. We record these cases together; the case 

k = 3 will also be used in our proofs. 

Theorem 1.3 ([15, 3, 18, 4]) . Let k ∈ { 2, 3, 4}. Every finite k-connected graph has k

independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Conjecture 1.2 remains open in general for k ≥ 5; recent progress for random graphs appears in [8, 14]. The survey of Cheng, Wang and Fan [2] gives a broader account of independent spanning trees in finite networks and their applications. This paper studies the independent-spanning-tree problem in infinite graphs. To the best of our knowledge, the independent-spanning-tree problem has not previously been studied in infinite graphs. Our main results are Theorems 2.4, 3.4 and 4.4. The first extends the case k = 2 for any 2-connected graph. 1arXiv:2611.28938v1 [math.CO] 21 Nov 2026 Theorem 2.4. Every 2-connected graph has two independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Freudenthal introduced ends in 1931 in his study of topological spaces and topological groups [9]. Independently, Halin introduced ends of graphs and end-faithful spanning trees in 1964 [12]. A graph is locally finite if every vertex has finite degree. For connected locally finite graphs, the topological and graph-theoretical notions of an end agree. We use the graph-theoretical notion and ask how independent spanning trees represent the ends of the graph. We recall the definitions below. 

Definition 1.4. Let G be a graph. A one-way infinite path is called a ray , and its infinite connected subgraphs are its tails . We denote the set of rays of G by R(G). Two rays 

r, s ∈ R (G) are equivalent in G if, for every finite set X ⊆ V (G), r and s have tails in the same component of G − X. Each equivalence class [ r]G is called an end of G. We denote the set of ends of G by Ω( G). 

Definition 1.5. A subgraph H ⊆ G represents an end [ r]G ∈ Ω( G) if H contains a ray equivalent to r in G. For a spanning tree T of G, the inclusion R(T ) ⊆ R (G) induces a map Ω( T ) → Ω( G) given by [ r]T 7 → [r]G. The tree T is end-faithful if this map is a bijection. Equivalently, a spanning tree is end-faithful if each end of G contains exactly one ray of the tree starting at its root [7, Section 3]. The existence of a single end-faithful spanning tree has been studied extensively; see Diestel [6], Širáň [17], and the more recent work of Bürger and Kurkofka [1]. In particular, Seymour and Thomas [16] constructed an infinitely connected graph with no end-faithful spanning tree, disproving the assertion that every connected graph admits one. For locally finite graphs, we strengthen Theorem 2.4 as follows. 

Theorem 3.4. Every locally finite 2-connected graph has two independent end-faithful spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Definition 1.6. A clique of a graph is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices; it is maximal if it is not properly contained in another clique. A graph is chordal if it has no induced cycle of length at least four, where a cycle is induced when no two of its nonconsecutive vertices are adjacent. Chordal graphs are an important class in structural and algorithmic graph theory: finite chordal graphs admit efficient algorithms for minimum colouring, maximum clique and maximum independent set [10]. Their structure can be described by clique trees, which organise their maximal cliques and the separators formed by intersections of adjacent cliques [11]. Hofer-Temmel and Lehner extended this representation to connected locally finite chordal graphs [13], as stated in Theorem 4.3. For this class, we obtain the following result. 

Theorem 4.4. Every locally finite 3-connected chordal graph has three independent end-faithful spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Examples 3.8 and 4.6 show that local finiteness is essential in Theorems 3.4 and 4.4, respectively. For k = 3, we also prove a partial extension of Theorem 1.3 to infinite graphs. A vertex is universal if it is adjacent to every other vertex, and a vertex cover is a set of vertices meeting every edge. 

Theorem 2.6. Every 3-connected graph with a universal vertex or a finite vertex cover has three independent spanning trees rooted at any prescribed vertex. 

Question 4.7 asks whether this conclusion holds for every infinite 3-connected graph. In the locally finite case, we also ask whether the three trees can be chosen end-faithful (Question 4.8).

We use the standard facts that inclusion induces a function from the ends of a spanning tree to the ends of the graph and that every end of a rooted tree contains exactly one ray starting at its root [7, Section 3]. Thus the function in Definition 1.5 is a bijection when every end of 

G contains exactly one ray of T starting at the root. For locally finite graphs, representation of every end is automatic. 

Proposition 3.1 (Diestel and Kühn; see [7, proof of Lemma 3.2]) . Let G be a connected locally finite graph, let T be a spanning tree of G, and let r ∈ V (G). Every end of G contains a ray of T starting at r.

We shall also use the block structure of a connected graph. A vertex x of a connected graph F is a cutvertex if F − x is disconnected. An edge e is a bridge if F − e is disconnected. A block of F is a maximal connected subgraph with no cutvertex. For a connected graph with at least two vertices, the block-cutvertex tree is the bipartite graph whose vertex classes are the blocks and the cutvertices of F , with a block K adjacent to a cutvertex x when 

x ∈ V (K). 

Theorem 3.2 (See [5, Section 3.1, especially Lemma 3.1.4]) . Let F be a connected graph with at least two vertices. Every edge of F belongs to exactly one block. Every block is either a bridge together with its endvertices or a 2-connected subgraph. Two distinct blocks have at most one vertex in common, and any common vertex is a cutvertex of F . The block-cutvertex incidence graph is a tree. For a cutvertex x, its deletion from this incidence tree corresponds to deletion of x from 

F . For each component U of the incidence tree after deleting x, take the union of V (K) \ { x}

over the blocks K in U. These unions are the vertex sets of the components of F − x.

An edge cut of G associated with a partition V (G) = U ˙∪ W into two nonempty sets is the set of edges with one end in U and the other in W . For a spanning tree T of G and an edge e ∈ E(T ), the fundamental cut of e is the set of all edges of G with ends in the two different components of T − e.We also use the fundamental-cut formulation of the end-faithfulness criterion for locally finite graphs. 

Proposition 3.3 (Diestel and Kühn; see [7, Lemma 3.3 and the remark immediately following it]) . Let G be a connected locally finite graph and let T be a spanning tree of G. Then T is end-faithful if and only if the fundamental cut of every edge of T is finite.

Definition 4.1. A tree-decomposition of a graph G is a pair ( D, (Bt)t∈V (D)), where D is a tree and each Bt is a subset of V (G).
