# A unified closed-form theory for elastic waves in polycrystals of any crystal symmetry 

Ming Huang ∗

Department of Engineering and Design, University of Sussex, Brighton BN1 9QT, UK and Department of Mechanical Engineering, Imperial College London, London SW7 2AZ, UK 

Stanislav I. Rokhlin 

Department of Materials Science and Engineering, The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH 43221, USA 

Michael J. S. Lowe 

Department of Mechanical Engineering, Imperial College London, London SW7 2AZ, UK 

The coherent elastic wave in random polycrystals is described by the first-order smoothing ap-proximation, whose mass operator remains an integral evaluated only numerically. Here we derive its closed form for equiaxed, untextured polycrystals of any crystal symmetry with an exponential correlation function, giving an explicit dispersion equation at all frequencies. Unlike the integral form, which fails beyond one inverse correlation radius from the real axis, the closed form holds deep in the complex wavenumber plane, where heavily attenuated roots lie. It is compact, repro-duces the dispersion equations of Stanke and Kino for cubic grains, extends them to any symmetry and contains the Born and far-field approximations as special cases. It reveals the structure of the roots and their partitions of the coherent field: three roots per polarization, one from the incident wave and one from each of the two scattering channels, without and with mode conversion. It also yields closed-form limits of their velocity, attenuation and partition in the Rayleigh, stochastic and geometric regimes, which show why a single coherent wave has been observed at long wavelengths, whereas more than one can be observed at short wavelengths. These results underpin further theory and quantitative characterization of polycrystalline microstructure. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Random polycrystals, from metals and ceramics to rocks, scatter elastic waves at the boundaries between grains of different crystallographic orientation. This scat-tering gives the coherent wave, i.e. the field averaged over the random grain structure, a complex wavenumber. Its real part gives the phase velocity, and its imaginary part gives the attenuation coefficient. Ultrasonic nonde-structive evaluation uses this wavenumber to character-ize grain size and texture and to determine the smallest detectable defect [1–3], and seismology uses it to study the heterogeneous crust, mantle and inner core of the Earth [4]. Both rely on a theory that predicts the com-plex wavenumber from the microstructure. The standard theory starts from the Dyson equation for the mean Green’s function, which collects all multi-ple scattering in a mass operator [5–7]. The first-order smoothing approximation (FOSA), or Bourret approxi-mation, keeps the mass operator to second order in the fluctuations of the elastic constants [7–11], and the roots of the resulting dispersion equation give the complex wavenumbers of the coherent wave. The FOSA mass operator is a three-dimensional integral over the scat-tered wave vectors, which has been evaluated in several ways. Stanke and Kino [10] integrated it in real space for cubic grains with an exponential correlation function

and obtained explicit but lengthy dispersion equations. The Born approximation evaluates it at the wavenum-ber of the reference medium, which gives the attenua-tion [7] and the phase velocity [12] explicitly. Calvet and Margerin [13] computed the spectral function on the real wavenumber axis. The far-field approximation (FFA) of Rokhlin et al. [14] simplifies the mass operator into a ra-tional function of the wavenumber, so that the dispersion equation can be analyzed explicitly [15]. Most recently, Sha et al. [16] and Huang et al. [17, 18] reduced the mass operator to a one-dimensional integral, valid for grains of any symmetry, which they evaluated numerically to find the roots at complex wavenumbers. The FOSA dispersion equation has recently been found to have three roots for each polarization [14, 15, 19], and grain-scale numerical simulations have resolved two of them as separate coherent waves once the wavelength becomes comparable to the grain size [19, 20]. The phys-ical meaning of these roots has so far been explained with the FFA [19], because the FOSA mass operator contains a principal-value integral, which makes its dispersion equa-tion transcendental. The FFA makes the structure of the roots explicit, but its two simplifications, the omis-sion of the near-field part of the Green’s function and the choice of a representative inner product, leave open which features belong to the FOSA itself. A closed form of the FOSA mass operator is therefore needed, both to reveal the number, physical meaning and observability of its roots without further approximation and to give an explicit dispersion equation for grains of any symmetry. Such an equation is also needed in practice, to compute arXiv:2611.26674v1 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci] 19 Nov 2026 2the velocity and attenuation quickly, to interpret exper-iments and simulations, and to infer the microstructure from measurements. In this paper, we derive the FOSA mass operator in closed form for equiaxed, untextured polycrystals of any crystal symmetry with an exponential correlation func-tion. The closed form is compact, extends the disper-sion equations of Stanke and Kino from cubic grains to any crystal symmetry and contains the Born approxima-tion and the FFA as special cases. Unlike the integral form used so far, it remains valid in the whole complex wavenumber plane. It makes the structure of the FOSA dispersion equation explicit. We use it to count the roots, to give each a physical meaning and to estimate which can be observed as separate coherent waves. We also de-rive closed-form expressions for their velocity, attenua-tion and partition, i.e. their contribution to the coherent wave, from the Rayleigh to the geometric regime. To-gether, these results explain within the FOSA itself why one coherent wave has been observed at long wavelengths and more than one at short wavelengths. We focus on the theory; how well the FOSA describes polycrystals has been examined against grain-scale finite-element simula-tions elsewhere [17–19, 21–23]. The paper is organized as follows. Section II sum-marizes the FOSA, derives the closed form of its mass operator, gives the physical meaning of its terms and shows that only the closed form remains valid at all com-plex wavenumbers. Section III explains the roots of the dispersion equation, derives their velocity, attenuation and partition in the Rayleigh, stochastic and geometric regimes, and relates the closed form to the Born and far-field approximations. Section IV summarizes and con-cludes.

| Im k| ≥ 1/a , where the integral form fails (Sec. II C). > C. Validity of the integral and closed forms

Because the roots of the dispersion equation are com-plex, we now evaluate the range of validity of the inte-gral and closed forms in representing the mass operator in the complex wavenumber plane. As an example, we use transverse waves in iron, a material with cubic crys-tals ( c11 = 219 .2, c12 = 136 .8, c44 = 109 .2 GPa, ρ =7860 kg/m 3), whose Voigt velocities are V0L = 5900 m/s and V0T = 3230 m/s. The integral form of Eq. (12) is valid for complex k

only when its integrand stays finite on the whole path of integration. However, the spectral density enters Eq. (11) as the inverse square of k2 − 2kξk 0N cos θ + ξ2k20N + a−2.Whenever | Im k| ≥ 1/a , this expression vanishes at some point of the path, where the integrand becomes infinite (Appendix B). The integral form therefore gives the mass operator correctly only in the strip 

| Im k| < 1/a, (17) 5or | Im k| < 1/ max n an for the multi-term correlation function of Eq. (13). The closed form contains no integral and has no such limit. It is valid in the whole complex plane, apart from three branch points in the upper half-plane, κL, κT and j /a , and the vertical branch cuts that run from them to infinity, which do not affect the roots (Appendix B). Figure 1(a) tests the two forms at 30 MHz for a single exponential correlation function with a = 0 .2309 mm. As the wavenumber moves into the complex plane at Re k =0.95 k0T , they agree to 5 ×10 −12 % up to Im k a = 0 .99, but they differ by 90% at 1.01 and by about 100% beyond. The same failure occurs for longitudinal waves and for other materials, because the edge of the strip is set by the correlation radius alone. The failure of the integral form matters because the heavily attenuated roots lie near the edge of the strip. Figure 1(b) shows the dispersion function from the closed form in the complex wavenumber plane. It has exactly three roots (Appendix B), numbered as in Sec. III A: root 1 is the incident wave modified by scattering, and roots 2 and 3 start from the poles κT and κL of the own and cross channels. Roots 1 and 2 lie well inside the strip, whereas root 3 lies just inside its edge, at Im k a = 0 .994, near the point κL. For a multi-term correlation function, the strip is narrower, and root 3 can lie outside it. An example is the eight-term fit to the correlation function of simulated microstructures [19], with the correlation length aCL = 0 .2309 mm. Roots 1 and 2 computed from the two forms agree to 10 −8%. However, the third root found with the integral form lies outside the strip and is therefore spurious, as shown in Fig. 1(c, d). The closed form places root 3 near κL, as for the single exponential.  

> III. ROOTS OF THE DISPERSION EQUATION AND THEIR LIMITS

In this section, we use the closed form to explain the roots of the dispersion equation. Section III A shows that the dispersion equation has three roots, gives their phys-ical meaning and defines their partitions. Sections III B, III C and III D derive closed-form expressions for the velocity, attenuation and partition of the roots in the Rayleigh, stochastic and geometric regimes, and com-pare them with earlier results. Section III E shows that the Born and far-field approximations are special cases of the full theory.

> E. Relation to the Born and far-field approximations

> IV. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

We have derived the closed form of the mass opera-tor in the dispersion equation of the first-order smooth-ing approximation for coherent elastic waves in equiaxed, untextured polycrystals of any crystal symmetry with an exponential correlation function. The closed form is com-pact, reproduces the dispersion equations of Stanke and Kino for cubic grains, extends them to any crystal sym-metry and contains the Born and far-field approximations as special cases. Unlike the integral form used in existing methods, which holds only in the strip | Im k| < 1/a , it is valid in the whole complex wavenumber plane, as re-quired for the heavily attenuated roots. The closed form allowed us to show that the dispersion equation has three roots and to give their physical meaning and partitions, i.e. their contributions to the coherent wave. It also al-lowed us to derive closed-form limits of their velocities, attenuations and partitions in the Rayleigh, stochastic and geometric regimes. The work leads to four findings. 1. The incident wave M scatters through two chan-nels: its own channel, M → M (e.g. T →T), and the cross channel, M → N with N̸ = M (e.g. T →L), i.e. mode conversion. Each channel is the sum of a pole, a constant and an angular term. The pole terms give the dispersion equation three roots for each polarization, one for the incident wave and one for each of the two channels. A root can be ob-served as a separate coherent wave only if its peak in the wavenumber spectrum of the coherent field is separated from the others and high enough, which depends on both its partition and its attenuation. 2. In the Rayleigh regime, for any crystal symmetry, the dispersion equation reduces to root 1, which carries the whole coherent field. The wave is scat-tered in all directions, so the velocity and attenua-tion of root 1 are set by the angle-averaged scatter-ing factors. Its quasi-static velocity, obtained here in closed form, does not depend on the grain size, and its attenuation grows as the fourth power of frequency and is proportional to the effective grain volume. 3. In the stochastic regime, the incident wave scatters mainly forward into its own channel, M → M . As a result, the dispersion equation reduces to roots 1 and 2. Both travel at about the Voigt velocity. The 11 attenuation of root 1 is proportional to the correla-tion length, as found earlier from the Born approx-imation, and root 2 has a small negative partition, so it forms no peak of its own but only makes the peak of root 1 asymmetric. The two roots get clos-est to each other, without coinciding, near an ex-ceptional point. Above it, they separate in velocity, and the geometric regime begins. As the scattering into the own channel strengthens, the exceptional point moves down toward the end of the Rayleigh regime, so a stochastic regime exists only when this scattering is weak, as Stanke and Kino found for strongly anisotropic grains [10]. 4. In the geometric regime, the dispersion equation keeps all three roots. The wave is scattered mainly forward, and the velocities, attenuations and parti-tions of the roots have simple closed forms that do not depend on frequency, for any crystal symme-try. The velocities do not depend on the grain size, whereas the attenuations are inversely proportional to the mean chord length of the grains, which gener-alizes the classical geometric attenuation of root 1. The closed form replaces all numerical integration in the evaluation of the theory and makes the dispersion equation as cheap to solve as that of the far-field approx-imation while keeping all the terms of the mass opera-tor. Its newly derived limits express the velocity, attenu-ation and partition of every coherent wave in terms of the single-crystal elastic constants and the grain size. They provide fast and transparent forward models for the ul-trasonic characterization of polycrystals [1–3]. In partic-ular, the velocity limits depend on the grain anisotropy but not on the grain size, whereas the attenuation limits depend on both: on the anisotropy through the scat-tering factors, and on the grain size through the effec-tive grain volume, the correlation length and the mean chord length in the three regimes. Measurements of ve-locity and attenuation can therefore separate the effects of grain anisotropy and grain size and allow both to be characterized reliably.

A branch point is the end of a branch cut, which is a line across which a multivalued function jumps from one of its values to another. The correct values follow from how the coherent field is ob-tained. The field radiated by a plane source is the inverse Fourier transform of gM = 1 /D M along the real k axis. When this path is moved upward into the complex plane, the field becomes a sum of residues at the roots of DM ,which are the coherent waves, plus integrals around the branch cuts. The functions must therefore take the val-ues reached continuously from the real axis along vertical paths, with the branch cuts running vertically upward from κN and j /a (and downward from −κN and −j/a ), as shown in Fig. 1(b). Near the real axis, these are the principal values.
