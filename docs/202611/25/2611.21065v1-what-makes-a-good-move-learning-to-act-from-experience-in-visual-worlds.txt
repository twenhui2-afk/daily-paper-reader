## Abstract 

Learning visual policies requires turning interaction expe-rience into guidance for individual decisions. Trajectory outcomes obscure action quality, while unguided exploration can revisit familiar situations. We present LIGHT ACT , an experience-learning framework that connects skill-guided exploration with policy internalization. 1arXiv:2611.21065v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly serve as agents that connect visual perception and language reasoning to action in interactive environments [14, 25, 31, 32]. Video games expose the demands of this process: an agent must rec-ognize the current situation, choose an executable move, and adapt as its actions change the environment [9, 16, 17]. Inter-action also provides experience from which to improve. Prior work retains such experience as contextual guidance [21, 29] or uses it to update policy parameters through reinforcement learning [1, 8]. The central challenge is to turn this experience into guid-ance about individual decisions. A trajectory records the actions taken and their realized outcomes, leaving alterna-tives at the same decision point unobserved [24]. Its final outcome combines many choices: a useful move can be followed by a mistake, while a poor move can be rescued later. Environment scores summarize these outcomes, but do not directly reveal which action would have been prefer-able at a particular state [18]. The states encountered during exploration introduce a second limitation. Repeatedly visit-ing familiar situations concentrates experience on a narrow range of decisions [5, 15]. These observations suggest organizing experience around both the states reached and the choices available there. Sum-maries of prior interaction can guide exploration toward a broader set of decision states. At a selected state, execut-ing alternative actions under a shared continuation policy makes their subsequent outcomes comparable. Averaging score gains over short rollouts then yields an estimate of each action’s value, which can be converted into supervision for policy learning. We present L IGHT ACT , an experience-learning frame-work that combines skill-guided exploration with policy in-ternalization . We partition preliminary trajectories collected by a frozen pretrained VLM into short interaction segments. The model summarizes each segment into an experience entry describing the observed conditions, behavior, and out-come, then consolidates these entries into a game-specific textual skill. The skill guides subsequent exploration by pro-viding reusable advice on effective behavior and recurring failures. We retain distinct visited states and their visual decision contexts as a bank of decision roots. At each root, we compare the available actions through repeated short continuations under the same frozen policy. The resulting environment-reported score gains are averaged and normalized within the root to produce relative action rewards. Pairing these rewards with the collected decision contexts forms a fixed training set. Reinforcement learn-ing then updates the policy by sampling responses to these contexts and assigning rewards to their parsed actions. Related Work 

2.1. Vision-Language Game Agents 

Video games test whether visual understanding supports sequential decisions [36]. Cradle [25] combines percep-tion, planning, and memory to operate through screenshots and keyboard or mouse controls. BALROG [17] evalu-ates language and vision-language agents across games and reveals weaknesses in visually grounded interaction. VideoGameBench [34] examines play from raw visual in-put under real-time and paused interaction, separating de-cision quality from the effects of inference latency. Game-World [16] provides browser games with standardized inter-faces and state-verifiable task evaluation. Complementing these benchmarks, lmgame-Bench [8] studies perception and memory scaffolds and reports that reinforcement learning on individual games can transfer beyond the training envi-ronment. Our work examines how source-game interaction supports such policy learning. 

2.2. Experience Learning of VLMs 

VLM agents learn from accumulated interaction through contextual guidance and parameter updates. External mem-ory retains experience for retrieval at decision time [13], while parameter updates encode it in the policy [6]. JARVIS-1 [29] retrieves multimodal Minecraft experience to sup-port planning, while ELITE [30] extracts reusable strategies from trajectories and retrieves them according to task intent. ICAL [21] refines suboptimal demonstrations through ex-ecution and human feedback, annotating causal relations, state changes, and subgoals; the resulting examples support both retrieval and supervised fine-tuning. For reinforcement learning, DigiRL [1] adapts a pretrained VLM for device control through offline initialization and online interaction, using a VLM evaluator for feedback. VAGEN [27] improves explicit state estimation and transition prediction in multi-turn visual interaction through reinforcement learning. We connect experience-guided source exploration with policy internalization. 

2.3. Rollout-Based Policy Learning 

Rollouts estimate the value of an action from the outcomes that follow it. Monte Carlo policy improvement compares candidate moves by continuing each with a common base policy [26]. In combinatorial optimization and stochastic scheduling, heuristic continuations estimate the future cost of each choice [2, 3]. Sparse sampling [10] estimates ac-tion values over sampled lookahead trees, while UCT [11] uses a bandit rule to allocate simulations among branches. Rollout comparisons also provide supervision for policy learning [35]. Classification-based policy iteration [12] uses the preferred action at a sampled state as a training label. AggreVaTe [20] trains on expert cost-to-go estimates, while LOLS [4] evaluates decisions under both reference and learned policies. For language models, LATS [37] guides tree search with model evaluations and environment feed-back, and Math-Shepherd [28] uses sampled completions to assess intermediate reasoning steps. We use score gains from a shared game state to supervise action selection. 

## 3. Method 

Experience gathered during interaction can guide subsequent decisions through both contextual guidance and policy learn-ing. Building on this view, L IGHT ACT (Fig. 3) distills source-game trajectories into textual skills for exploration and com-pares action outcomes at the collected roots to construct action-level rewards for policy internalization. 

3.1. Decision-Making in Game Environment 

In GameWorld [16], an agent pursues a game-specific ob-jective through sequential interaction. At each decision, it receives the game rules, task instruction, role information, and available semantic actions, together with the current screenshot and recent observation–action history. The agent generates a response, parses it into an executable action, observes its effect on the game, and continues toward the objective. ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Benchmark and Evaluation Setting 

We evaluate L IGHT ACT on GameWorld [16], which com-prises 34 games and 170 tasks across five categories: Arcade, Platformer, Puzzle, Runner, and Simulation. Each run has a budget of 100 decisions, with the environment paused during inference. At each decision, the policy receives the game instructions, the current screenshot, and two rounds of image–text interaction history, and selects an action from the game’s interface. Action duration, episode termination, and resets follow the protocol. Published GameWorld results use greedy decoding with one run per task. Our Qwen3.5 and L IGHT ACT evaluations use temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, a 2,048-token output cap, and three runs per task. We use GameWorld’s state-based evaluator to measure success rate (SR), the fraction of successful runs, and progress (PG), normalized completion toward task-specific targets. 

4.2. Implementation Details 

We instantiate L IGHT ACT with Qwen3.5-2B, Qwen3.5-4B, and Qwen3.5-9B [19]. At each scale, experience collection, branch continuation, and skill extraction use frozen copies of the same model that initializes the trainable policy. Act-ing uses the native non-thinking mode. For each source game–task pair, we collect trajectories within a budget of 10,000 decisions and retain up to 1,024 deduplicated roots, with seed 42 for the initial reset. We equip source games with snapshot support through exportSnapshot() and 

importSnapshot() , restoring each decision root be-fore evaluating candidate actions. Collection and branch continuation use temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, and a 2,048-token output cap. 4.3. Baselines and Main Comparisons 

LIGHT ACT achieves competitive progress against propri-etary models. LIGHT ACT brings compact policies close to strong proprietary models in benchmark progress (Table 1). At 4B, it reaches 40.5% overall PG, comparable to GPT-5.2 at 40.6%; at 9B, it reaches 41.8%, approaching Gemini-3-Flash-Preview at 41.9%. On Puzzle tasks, L IGHT ACT -9B achieves 59.2% PG, exceeding the reported progress of both models. The remaining gap is clearer in task completion, where overall SR reaches 18.4% for L IGHT ACT -9B com-pared with 21.2% for Gemini. By comparing actions from a shared state, L IGHT ACT turns interaction outcomes into supervision for policy learning. These results suggest that internalizing such experience enables compact VLMs to achieve competitive game-playing performance through di-rect policy inference. 

LIGHT ACT consistently improves policy performance across model scales. LIGHT ACT increases both SR and PG in all five game categories at 2B, 4B, and 9B, indicating benefits for task completion as well as partial progress. The overall PG gains are 23.6, 29.2, and 13.0 percentage points, respectively. The 4B policy gains most on Runner tasks, where PG rises from 8.2% to 49.7%, even though its training experience comes from a puzzle game. Notably, L IGHT ACT -2B surpasses the pretrained 9B baseline in both PG (36.9% versus 28.8%) and SR (13.9% versus 10.0%), showing that adaptation through experience can improve the effectiveness of a smaller policy. 4.4. Ablations and Diagnostics 

Policy internalization yields consistent gains across RL algorithms. We compare GRPO, REINFORCE++, and DAPO, using the same reward tables for each model scale (Table 2). All nine trained configurations improve overall PG over their base policies, with gains ranging from 10.8 to 29.2 percentage points. This pattern suggests that the action-level rewards provide useful supervision across differ-ent policy-update rules. The optimizer still affects how these gains are distributed across games: at 4B, DAPO achieves higher Puzzle and Simulation PG than GRPO, while GRPO attains higher overall PG. > 5A smaller tile can block two equal larger tiles. Look for a merge on the other axis that moves the blocker out of their row or column. After that move, recheck whether the larger pair can now merge.

Source-game experience supports generalization across game categories. With Tetris as the source game, both GRPO and REINFORCE++ improve PG over the 2B base policy across all five categories (Figure 4). Arcade PG, for example, rises from 5.6% to 29.8% and 29.6%, respectively. These gains extend beyond the source game’s Puzzle cate-gory, suggesting that policy internalization helps L IGHT ACT 

apply experience to different game mechanics. Experience from a single game can provide supervision for broader vi-sual decisions. The varying gains across categories indicate that transfer is uneven, leaving room to improve the breadth of behaviors learned from source experience. 

Rollout outcomes distinguish actions with identical im-mediate gains. In Figure 6, moving left or right from the same root yields an immediate score gain of 24, but the resulting boards support different continuations. The right-ward branch produces a 64 tile, whereas the leftward branch retains a maximum tile of 32. Across four rollouts, moving right achieves a mean gain of 144 versus 67 for moving left, yielding normalized rewards of 1.000 and 0.330. The rollout comparison turns delayed action outcomes into a training preference that immediate score gains alone cannot provide. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented L IGHT ACT , a framework that turns interaction experience into guidance for exploration and supervision for policy learning. Game-specific skills broaden root-state coverage, while short rollouts from shared roots reveal the relative utility of candidate actions. Experiments on Game-World show consistent improvements across model scales and policy optimizers, with compact policies achieving com-petitive progress against proprietary models. These findings demonstrate the value of organizing experience around states and action outcomes for learning game-playing policies. 

## 6. Limitations 

Generalization across source environments remains limited, and the current framework does not directly support action spaces with continuous parameters. Our study focuses on short-horizon decisions; long-horizon decision-making re-mains to be validated. Future work will investigate integra-tion with multi-turn reinforcement learning and evaluation on games that require sustained planning. Reward shaping currently relies on scores read from environment states.
