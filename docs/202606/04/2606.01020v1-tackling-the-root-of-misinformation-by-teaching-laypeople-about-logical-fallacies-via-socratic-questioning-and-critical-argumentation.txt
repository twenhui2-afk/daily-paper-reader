# Tackling the Root of Misinformation by Teaching Laypeople about Logical Fallacies via Socratic Questioning and Critical Argumentation

shimin@student.ethz.ch, mj.shi@foxmail.com junling.wang@ai.ethz.ch jingwei.ni@gess.ethz.ch {sankalan.palchowdhury, mrinmaya.sachan}@inf.ethz.ch 

Abstract 

Identifying logical fallacies in everyday dis-course is challenging for many people. This challenge is amplified in the era of Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs), where malicious agents can deploy fallacious arguments to disseminate misinformation at scale. In this work, we ex-plore the potential of LLMs as part of the solu-tion. We introduce LFTutor, an intelligent tu-toring system which uses LLMs to tutor laypeo-ple and help them learn about logical fallacies. LFTutor integrates intent-driven Socratic ques-tioning and critical argumentation principles to actively engage learners to reflect on their reasoning. Through both automatic and human evaluations, we demonstrate that LFTutor sig-nificantly outperforms baseline LLMs lacking these pedagogical strategies. This work high-lights the promise of combining LLMs with pedagogical scaffolding to foster critical think-ing and argument literacy in the age of AI. 

1 Introduction 

The widespread presence of misinformation on-line (Vicario et al., 2016) poses a serious risk to public opinion and decision-making (Skafle et al., 2022). Research has shown that logical fallacies —flawed but persuasive and misleading reasoning patterns—are a key driver of misinformation (Jin et al., 2022). The prevalence of logical fallacies in public discourse (Risen et al., 2007) makes it crucial to develop systems that help people detect logical fallacies in public misinformation. To combat such prevalence, prior work focuses on automatic detection of fallacies (Jin et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2024; Jeong et al., 2025). Although such methods can flag potential fallacies for user’s atten-tion, they do not help people recognize them inde-pendently. Instead of passively feeding users with fallacy detection output, it can be more beneficial to focus on LLM-based educational approaches that (1) nudge users to actively discuss and reconsider logical arguments (Shahzad et al., 2025); (2) help users overcome cognitive biases reinforced by the reasoning structures in fallacies (Correia, 2011); and (3) raise users’ awareness if they lack a proper understanding of fallacies (Berkle et al., 2023). Developing an LLM-based tutor for fallacies presents several challenges: C1 . It is difficult to evaluate tutors – existing metrics for educational dialogues may not work well for logical fallacies (Wang et al., 2024b). C2 . LLMs face several chal-lenges when acting as logical fallacy educators, including diversion from the topic (Pal Chowd-hury et al., 2024), concession to persuasion (Xu et al., 2024), mirroring student texts without cri-tique (Wang et al., 2024a), and lacking reflective questions (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), etc. To address these challenges, we design an LLM-based framework for teaching laypeople to recog-nize logical fallacies along with a scalable way to evaluate such tutors. Specifically, to address C1 ,we define metrics for logical fallacy tutoring based on our findings in a pilot study and relevant liter-ature in education and argumentation theory. To address C2 , we propose LFTutor , a tutoring frame-work based on intent-based pedagogical steering (Puech et al., 2024), which aims to help learners identify fallacious arguments through structured, designated strategies in a dialogue setting, avoiding current issues of LLMs as logical fallacy tutors. We conduct both automatic and human evalu-ation to verify the effectiveness of LFTutor . For automatic evaluation, we use simulated students to generate dialogues with LFTutor and baseline LLMs with texts from Elec2Deb20 (Goffredo et al., 2023). We then use QwQ-32B (verified against human judges) to evaluate tutor responses on all metrics. For human evaluation, we recruit 20 par-ticipants who interact with LFTutor and then rate its performance against baseline LLMs. Results show that LFTutor achieves superior performance compared to baseline LLMs in both automatic and

2 Background and Related Work 

Socratic Questioning. Socratic questioning is a well-known educational technique that aims to chal-lenge underlying student assumptions and uncover faulty reasoning steps. This technique is beneficial in stimulating the student’s thinking, thus improv-ing their learning outcomes (Yang et al., 2005). Socratic questioning has been applied in a wide variety of scenarios, including critical thinking in-struction (Costa, 2001), literature seminars (Ed and Ed, 2002), and math problem solving (Shridhar et al., 2022). As logical fallacies often arise from faulty reasoning (Goffredo et al., 2023), Socratic questioning can be particularly relevant in helping learners examine and reflect on their reasoning. 

Intent-based Dialogue Strategies. Recent work on LLM agents have focused on dialogue genera-tion guided by users’ intents and structured strate-gies. Jin et al. (2024) and Xu et al. (2024) designed and utilized persuasion strategies in everyday dia-logue scenarios. Ruggeri et al. (2023) formulated debate strategies to annotate dialogues on a scien-tific paper between researchers. In the education domain, Puech et al. (2024) and Pal Chowdhury et al. (2024) developed tutoring agents that detect students’ intents and apply scaffolding techniques. 

Critical Argumentation in LLMs. Critical ar-gumentation (Walton, 2008) embodies systematic ways of analyzing arguments in dialogues, which is useful for responding to opposing viewpoints with counterarguments. In earlier work, Sinott-Armstrong and Frogelin (2015) proposed meth-ods to generate logically sound counterarguments. More recently, Ozaki et al. (2025) showed LLM’s ability to produce high-quality counterarguments when targeting implicit or critical premises. Pitre

3 Problem Formulation 

We frame the task of logical fallacy tutoring as a one-on-one interaction between a teacher and a 

student centered around a piece of potentially fal-lacious text (Fig. 1). The text contains one or more fallacies of any type (e.g. ad hominem 1, appeal to emotion 2, etc.). The teacher aims to educate the student by helping them realize that the text is logically flawed, while the student, who is unfamil-iar with fallacies, believes that it is logically valid, and defends their position by debating with the teacher. This multi-turn dialogue setting is useful as it: (1) enables deeper explorations of the fal-lacious statement using argument decomposition, and (2) allows the teacher to actively engage with the student’s underlying cognitive biases, going beyond surface-level identification of fallacies.

A major challenge in this task is that logical fallacies often hinge on contextual assumptions that are not explicitly stated (Glockner et al., 2025). For example, the sentence in Fig. 1 lacks definitive evidence on Person A’s susceptibility to friend’s influences, yet this context is critical in judging logical validity. To reason about these contexts, we utilize argument decomposition models such as the Toulmin model (Toulmin et al., 1984), which posits that a logical argument consists of a claim (the main assertion – Person A is not a suitable candidate ), the grounds (the supporting evidence – Person A’s friend’s involvement in scandals ), and the warrant (the underlying assumption linking the grounds to the claim – a friend’s scandals directly reflect the candidate’s incompetence ). This decomposition allows the teacher in Fig. 1 to focus on asking the learner for more evidence for the warrant. Another major challenge in misinformation edu-cation lies in the learner’s cognitive biases, which are often reinforced by reasoning structures and deep-rooted assumptions (Correia, 2011), as shown in their first response equating close relationships with direct influence (Fig. 1). Thus, laypeople’s fallacious beliefs are often resistant to shallow, one-shot identification and explanation (Helwe et al., 2024). In this regard, single-turn feedback is of-ten insufficient, and a multi-turn dialogic approach (Boghossian, 2003) is needed instead to help the learner engage in critical reflection of reasoning steps. This approach involves argument exchanges, where the teacher builds the discussion with prob-ing questions and targeted challenges to gradually reveal the logical flaw of the learner’s reasoning. We investigate LLMs’ performance in educat-ing laypeople about logical fallacies in this set-ting. Specifically, we choose Elec2Deb20 (Gof-fredo et al., 2023), a dataset on U.S. presidential debates (more details in § B). 

3.1 LLM’s Issues in Logical Fallacy Tutoring 

To our knowledge, no prior work has systemati-cally discussed evaluation metrics for LLM tutors in logical fallacy tutoring. As a first step towards filling this gap, we conduct a pilot study (§ A.3) to identify weaknesses of current LLMs in this task us-ing student-teacher dialogues simulated by GPT-4o. Furthermore, we draw on interdisciplinary insights into combating logical fallacies with reviews of relevant literature in education and logical reason-ing. Based on findings from both the pilot study and the literature review, we propose the following evaluation dimensions. Detailed examples and ex-planations of each issue are presented in Table 25. 

Divergence. Our pilot study found that 35% of di-alogues contain tutor responses unrelated to discus-sion of the logical fallacy (e.g. proposing solutions to the political issue in the sentence). However, topic relevance is crucial in education for facilitat-ing meaningful discussions (Tracy, 1984). We thus consider Divergence as a key evaluation dimension. 

Stance Change. Previous studies have shown that LLMs are prone to be persuaded by fallacious rea-soning, where LLMs succumb to persuasion by changing their stance (Xu et al., 2024; Payandeh et al., 2024). Thus, it is important for the teacher to hold their position when debating, as this might hin-der the student’s development of critical thinking skills (Withey and Zhang, 2016). 

Repetition. Our pilot study reveals that LLMs tend to repeat the student’s responses without contribut-ing additional insight (e.g. further questions to the responses) - this occurs in 60% of all dialogues. Such repetitions are harmful as they may increase the chance of the LLM tutor generating repetitive responses (Xu et al., 2022), leading to student con-fusion and poorer quality in teaching. 

Lack of Refutation. Prior work indicates that LLMs demonstrate sycophancy and lack the skills for “sustained” debates, including posing counter-arguments (Zhang et al., 2024). This issue was also present in the pilot study, where 53% of di-alogues lacked explicit challenges to the student. It is crucial for the teacher to actively refute the student’s illogical argument when necessary, which helps clarify misconceptions (Kowalski and Taylor, 2009) on the logical structure of the fallacies. 

Lack of Evidence Inquiry. Liu et al. (2024) ob-serve that LLMs lack “thought-provoking” teach-ing paradigms (e.g. requiring the student to provide evidence to support their claims) and simply follow shallow “question-answering” structures. In logical fallacy tutoring, the teacher must ask the student for evidence that supports their claims, since this step encourages critical thinking from the Socratic teaching perspective (Yang et al., 2005). 

Strategy Fixation. Our pilot study shows in 90% of dialogues, LLM teacher relies on a single strat-egy by suggesting the student to consider broader context and balanced perspectives for the logical validity of the sentence. This strategy ignores texts with multiple faulty logical structures, which leads to less reflective student feedback (Krupp et al., 2023), limiting their scope of thinking. 

Unexplained LF Terms. We discovered that in 43% of generated dialogues, the LLM teacher actively names logical fallacy types e.g. “ad hominem”, without properly explaining them. This could be avoided, as it may confuse the students if they find the terminology unfamiliar (Jiao et al., 2023), thus hindering effective learning. 

Passive Guidance. Previous work (Pal Chowdhury et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024) reports that LLMs of-ten fail to assert pedagogical control in education dialogues. Guidance is important in logical fal-lacy tutoring, as it can progressively direct the stu-dent to focus on their argument’s weaknesses (Nip-pold, 2023). However, passive guidance, where the teacher primarily follows the student without providing clear direction, can negatively affect stu-dents’ learning outcomes (Terentev et al., 2024). 

3.2 Identifying Issues with LLM-as-a-judge 

Based on insights from § 3.1, we evaluate LLM tutors by measuring the rate at which they avoid these issues. We measure each issue at the dialogue level using the criteria shown in Table 23, which they either pass or fail. We then collect results from all dialogues and compute the percentages at which the tutor passes the evaluation of each metric. When considering LLM candidates as judges (Gu et al., 2025), we prefer those with built-in reasoning capabilities due to their superior perfor-mance in complex reasoning tasks (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). Balancing performance with cost, we use QwQ-32B (Team, 2025; Yang et al., 2024) from the Qwen family for our LLM-as-judge evaluations. We verify the reliability of QwQ-32B as a judge by comparing its annotations against those from hu-man experts. We randomly sampled 100 logically fallacious text from Elec2Deb20 and generated 100 dialogues of 10 rounds with the setting in § A.3. We then let QwQ-32B evaluate all dialogues using prompts containing metrics in § A.5. For human-annotated golden labels, we recruited three human annotators who are fluent in English and have prior experience with linguistic annota-tion tasks. We first explained the metrics to each annotator using examples from table 25. The an-notators then evaluated dialogues with the same prompts as QwQ-32B. After that, they resolved conflicting labels, reaching an agreement level of over 97% (Miles et al., 2020) for all metrics. For each metric, we randomly sampled 30 golden dialogues with 15 positive labels and 15 neg-ative labels. We then computed F1-scores (Table 5) for QwQ-32B against golden labels, which average over 73% for all metrics. We further validate QwQ-32B’s performance by manually reviewing human annotations, having observed that the task is diffi-cult for human annotators, involving long text (700 words) with complex arguments. We performed a direct inspection of the annotator’s labels and measured the accuracy of QwQ-32B’s annotation, shown in Table 6. We found that the accuracy of QwQ-32B was greater than or equal to 80% for all metrics, which ensures its reliability as the judge. 

4 LFTutor 

As stated in § 3, logical fallacy education faces two main challenges: 1. Logical fallacies hinge on contextual assumptions, which requires argument decomposition to pinpoint; 2. Fallacy believers often harbor cognitive biases and are resistant to one-shot identification and explanation. To address them, we propose LFTutor , a dialogue tutoring agent that (i) locates logical flaws in the student’s responses by implicitly decomposing them into claims, grounds and warrants, and (ii) interactively adapts to the student’s intents over multiple turns. At each turn, LFTutor analyzes the student’s re-sponses using the Toulmin model to identify the text component containing logical flaws. It then applies a strategy tailored to that component to guide the student towards recognizing flaws. This technique, called intent-based pedagogical steering (Puech et al., 2024), structures the tutor’s output by detecting the student’s intent (implied purposes behind their response, e.g., asking for clarification), and then executing tutoring strategies aligned with the detected intent. We integrate this design into a four-step framework (Fig. 2): (1) Disagreement Check, (2) Intent Detection, (3) Intent-based Strat-egy Selection, and (4) Verified Strategy Execution. Detailed pseudocode is shown in Algorithm 1. 

Step 1: Disagreement Check . To structure the tutor-student dialogue and help LFTutor focus on specific parts of the text they disagree on, we in-troduce disagreement bank , a long-term memory module (Zhong et al., 2024) that stores a list of dis-agreement points between the two parties. Given the student’s response from the last turn, the text with logical fallacies, and the disagreement bank,

If the opposing claim is newly made, then LFTutor adds the complete argument to the disagreement bank, which it aims to address in this round, regardless of the contents from examples or assumptions. How-ever, if the claim has been discussed, then the exam-ples or assumptions must be different from those in the bank. Otherwise, LFTutor will skip the next steps and request the student to provide new argu-ments that support their position. 

Step 2: Intent Detection . Given the student’s argument, LFTutor analyzes it (§ F.2) for poten-tial intents: (I1) student requests clarification on the logical flaw of the text; (I2) student’s argu-ment lacks evidence supporting their claim; (I3) student’s argument lacks assumptions linking ev-idence to their claim; (I4) student’s argument is complete, but has clear logical flaws. These intents align naturally with the Toulmin model: I1 guides the student to formulate a clear claim in their re-sponse, I2 targets the student’s missing grounds, I3 highlights the student’s missing warrants, and I4 identifies weaknesses in the logical chain as a whole. All detected intents (one or more) are stored for LFTutor ’s reference in the next step. 

Step 3: Intent-based Strategy Selection . Given the detected intents, LFTutor focuses on address-ing the student’s reasoning flaws by selecting an ap-propriate education strategy related to their intent. We draw strategies from two main pedagogical the-ories: (1) Socratic questioning (Ed and Ed, 2002), which aims for dialogic, open-ended discussions on logical fallacies by probing the student’s reason-ing, and (2) critical argumentation (Walton, 2008), which allows LFTutor to target weaknesses of the student’s argument and provide sound refutations, nudging them to reflect on argument quality. We include four strategies corresponding to the afore-mentioned student intents: Responding, Evidence, Assumption, and Refutation. Prompts and example responses are shown in § F.3 and Table 20. 

(i) Responding: When the student requests expla-nations on the logical flaws in their argument or the logical fallacies, LFTutor clarifies and explains the logical flaw from those statements. 

(ii) Evidence: When the response lacks sufficient evidence, LFTutor asks the student for evidence supporting the claim. This is similar to Socratic questions on evidence shown in (Yang et al., 2005). 

(iii) Assumption: When the student’s response makes unclear assumptions, LFTutor challenges the student’s hidden or unsupported assumptions in the argument. This is akin to Socratic questions on assumptions discussed in (Yang et al., 2005). 

(iv) Refutation: When the student’s argument con-tain clear logical flaws, LFTutor provides logical refutations to the student’s argument, using meth-ods adopted from critical argumentation (Sinott-Armstrong and Frogelin, 2015). 

LFTutor selects strategies in the order listed above (i → iv), matching the priority of components in the Toulmin model (Hitch-cock, 2017): claims form an argument’s foun-dation (→Responding), followed by grounds (→Evidence), then warrants (→Assumption), and finally, rebuttal to a complete argument (→Refutation). Clarification of logical flaw(s) (Re-sponding) introduces a counter-claim, leading the student to reconsider and refine their position. After establishing the claim, LFTutor presses the student to provide relevant proof (Evidence). Assumption comes once claim and grounds are both present, and LFTutor challenges the student to form con-nections between them. Finally, LFTutor applies Refutation when it obtains a complete argument, urging the student to examine their argument’s log-ical validity. By structuring strategies according to this hierarchy, LFTutor guides the student through argument construction in a stepwise fashion by sys-tematically exposing component-level flaws. 

Step 4: Verified Strategy Execution . In the fi-nal step, LFTutor applies the chosen strategy to respond to the student (§ F.4). Then, another LLM verifies if the response adheres to the chosen strat-egy (§ F.5). If this verification fails, the LLM in LFTutor is asked to rephrase its response to meet the requirements. This step guarantees that 

LFTutor ’s response respects the strategy while re-maining consistent with the argument structure. 

5 Automatic Evaluations 

We begin by evaluating LFTutor using a set of automatic evaluations. We first describe the base-line LLM tutors that LFTutor will be compared with (§ 5.1) and the student design used in our evaluations (§ 5.2). We then describe the detailed experimental settings (§ 5.3) and the results (§ 5.4). 

5.1 Baseline LLM Tutors 

We designed two baselines for the tutor LLM for comparison. The first (B ASE ) uses a role-playing prompt, where the tutor is instructed to act as an educator whose goal is to convince a potentially bi-ased student that the given text contains logical fal-lacies (§ E.1). The second (B ASE W / P ROBLEMS )improves upon B ASE by incorporating descriptions of the issues in logical fallacy education (§ 3.1) in addition to role-playing instructions, and the tutor is asked to avoid those issues (§ E.2). This serves as a heuristic-enhanced baseline that attempts to mitigate the tutor’s pitfalls. 

5.2 Student Design 

For our automated experiments, we make use of simulated students, which is a common approach in recent works (Macina et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). We prompt GPT-4o to roleplay as a student who believes in a logically fallacious statement. How-ever, we wish to account for the fact that some of the issues discussed in § 3.1 might be too rare to be triggered by a normal student LLM, but may cause issues if triggered in a real conversation. Therefore, we also create adversarial student personas, which are specifically trying to trigger each issue. 

The normal student debates with the tutor by de-fending their position with evidence and explana-tions, believing that the text containing logical fal-lacies is logically valid (§ G.2). To create a “robust” student, we prompt the LLM to utilize various de-bating techniques (Table 19) in critical argumenta-tion (Walton, 2008; Sinott-Armstrong and Frogelin, 2015). 

The adversarial student intends to trigger the tu-tor’s issues discussed in § 3.1. We use role-playing techniques (Zhou et al., 2024) to design students that drive the tutor towards behaviors matching the issues’ descriptions (§ G.3). For “Divergence”, for example, we ask the LLM to play a student who often talks about information irrelevant to the dis-cussion of the logical fallacy. This way, we evaluate the robustness of tutors under worst case scenarios. We exclude adversarial students for Lack of Refuta-tion and Lack of Evidence Inquiry, as pilot studies show that these issues are primarily attributed to the lack of instructions in tutor’s prompts.

6 Human Evaluations 

Finally, to understand how LFTutor might work in a more real-world setting, we conduct pilot hu-man evaluations comparing LFTutor and B ASE .We choose B ASE as an evaluation baseline since it resembles behaviors of real-world LLMs with min-imal knowledge of issues in logical fallacy educa-tion, instead of B ASE W / PROBLEMS that performs slightly better in automatic evaluation but fails to reflect LLMs’ real-world exposure to logical falla-cies. The evaluation procedure is shown in § 6.1, with the results discussed in § 6.2 and § 6.3. 

6.1 Evaluation Procedure 

We selected two texts from Elec2Deb20 that partici-pants voted logically valid (§ I.1) for the evaluation. To align with previous work’s evaluation settings for interactive systems (Jiang et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2023), we recruited 20 participants fluent in written and spoken English to engage in debates with the tutors, with the order of tutors and sen-tences randomly chosen. During the study, every participant interacted with each tutor for 5 to 10 rounds each. They eval-uated the tutor after each session (Fig. 8) with met-rics (Table 23) on a Likert scale (Likert, 1932) from 1 to 5, where 1 indicated observation of the issue at every turn, and 5 indicated no observation of such issue. The participant also completed a post-study form (Fig. 9, 10), where they compared the tutors 

LFTutor and B ASE . In addition to existing metrics, we included “Helpfulness” to measure the subjec-tive learning helpfulness of the tutor for the partici-pant, which they rated on the same Likert scale. A score of 1 indicated least helpful for logical fallacy understanding, and 5 indicated extremely helpful. To determine whether the differences of human ratings between LFTutor and B ASE are statistically significant, we conducted quantitative analysis for all scores. We first ran Shapiro-Wilk Test (Shapiro and Wilk, 1965) to test the normality of each met-ric’s score distribution. Observing that they dis-tribute normally, we ran one-sided t-tests (Student, 1908) to compare the scores between LFTutor and BASE chatbot, with results shown in Table 3. Finally, we also performed a summative qual-itative analysis of the participants’ responses, by examining their chat histories and post-study forms, and then identifying key phrases from their descrip-tions of LFTutor and B ASE and extracting recur-ring themes that came up in the dialogues. 

6.2 Quantitative Analysis on Ratings 

LFTutor achieves statistically significant better scores than B ASE on eight out of nine metrics. 

The improvement is significant ( p < 0.05 ) for Strat-egy Fixation, Unexplained LF Terms, and Helpful-ness, and highly significant ( p < 0.01 ) for Diver-gence, Stance Change, Lack of Refutation, Lack of Evidence Inquiry, and Passive Guidance. This dif-ference is likely due to LFTutor ’s explicit emphasis towards detailed probing questions and refutations to users’ responses, which allows it to focus on tutoring logical fallacy and remain strong in its po-sition. These characteristics are directly reflected in LFTutor ’s responses but not in B ASE , which leaves a strong impression for the participants. 

LFTutor shows limited improvement in Repe-tition compared with B ASE . Repetition is less prominent in B ASE for dialogues with participants compared to LLM-simulated students, due to par-ticipants’ usage of more varied debating strategies and Student LLMs’ lack of strategy variations. This tendency decreases the likelihood of repetitive re-sponses from B ASE in human evaluation.

LFTutor , depicting its usage of strategies that prompted deeper thinking. This leads to their preferences on LFTutor when rating on Guidance, Helpfulness, Lack of Refutation, Lack of Evidence Inquiry, and Strategy Fixation. 7 Ablations 

To investigate the contribution of different design choices in LFTutor towards evaluation improve-ments, we conducted an ablation study under four settings: (1) removing step 1: Disagreement Check, 

(2) removing strategy verification and correction, 

(3) reversing the priority of pedagogical strategies in step 3, from (Responding → Evidence → As-sumption → Refutation) to (Refutation → Assump-tion → Evidence → Responding), and (4) allowing multiple pedagogical strategies per turn by ignor-ing Intent-based Strategy Selection. Due to bud-get limitations, we conducted the study using the normal student setting discussed in § 5.3 with 100 sentences randomly selected from Elec2Deb20. Re-sults under these settings are shown in Table 4. 

Removing Step 1: Disagreement Check led to reduced performance in all metrics except Rep-etition . The increase of score in Repetition can be attributed to fewer reminders for the student to propose new examples or assumptions, where 

LFTutor must reiterate the student’s claims and grounds. Passive Guidance suffers the most among all metrics, since LFTutor is unable to keep track of topics in the dialogue and mainly analyzes and tackles the student’s immediate responses as the discussion continues. 

Removing Step 4: Verified Strategy Execution significantly reduces LFTutor ’s performance in most metrics . The decrease of scores is signifi-cant in Divergence, Lack of Evidence Inquiry, and Stance Change. This shows that strategy verifica-tion is crucial in reducing LFTutor ’s hallucinations towards irrelevant responses, while ensuring it re-mains faithful to the designated pedagogical goals. 

Reversing Strategy Order harms performance the most in Unexplained LF Terms and Passive Guidance, with minor changes of performance in other metrics . Setting Refutation as the top strategy led to unwanted usage of LF terms, since refutation by pointing out the LF in the opponent’s words is common (shown in Table 1) for baseline LLMs. Passive Guidance is also negatively affected since LFTutor now primarily treats the student’s re-sponses as complete arguments and aims to directly defeat them, rather than attempting to uncover the student’s supporting evidence and assumptions. 

Allowing Multiple Strategies per Turn resulted in decreased performance across all metrics , of which Divergence, Stance Change, Strategy Fixa-tion, and Unexplained LF Terms suffered the most declines. When LFTutor is not explicitly prompted to follow certain strategies, it tends to adhere to the forms of the typical responses from the given strat-egy, yet it has no inherent knowledge on which strategy are appropriate against different types of student responses. This loss of focus caused signif-icant performance declines in many metrics. 

8 Conclusion 

We explored the novel task of educating laypeople to identify logical fallacies in everyday discourse through multi-turn dialogues with LLM-based tu-tors. We introduced eight evaluation metrics to assess tutor responses and developed LFTutor , an LLM tutor system with intent-based pedagogical strategies that helps learners recognize logical fal-lacies. Our extensive automatic and human evalua-tion showed that LFTutor is an effective and ped-agogically grounded solution for misinformation education, paving a way for designing more AI tu-tors that foster critical thinking through structured reflection, reasoning and interactive engagement. Limitations 

Limited Performance Gain on Guidance. Al-though LFTutor managed to improve upon the BASE framework on all criteria during automatic evaluation, it did not achieve high scores ( ≥ 75% )in Passive Guidance. We attribute this to the lack of structured, detailed, and progressive dialogue goals (e.g. first tackle the evidence, then tackle the warrant, then narrow down the discussion to certain aspects of the warrant.), which needs to be closely integrated with tutoring strategies to create responses that are robust to passive influence. Fu-ture works could consider introducing a list of high-level education goals and procedures for LFTutor 

to follow, such that it combines those goals and tu-toring strategies to produce responses that actively control the flow of the dialogues. 

Scope of Evaluation Metrics In this paper, we investigated recurring problems with LLM tutors in dialog-based tutoring of logical fallacies and proposed an automatic evaluation framework with a curated set of eight evaluation metrics motivated by prior work and empirical relevance. While the proposed evaluation framework covers several key dimensions for measuring the quality of logical fallacy educational dialogues, such dialogues are inherently multi-faceted. Additional aspects, such as emotional support, personalization, and ease of understanding are not captured by our current metrics and they represent promising directions for future work.

B Datasets Used 

B.1 Dataset for Main Experiments 

For dialogue generation, we use Elec2Deb20 (Gof-fredo et al., 2023), which contains scripts from presidential candidates in television debates from 1960 to 2020. Elec2Deb20 includes over 1800 log-ical fallacy examples in six categories: Appeal to Authority, Ad Hominem, Appeal to Emotion, Slip-pery Slope, False Cause, and Slogan. We believe that this dataset is representative of sentences with LF discussed on the internet, since the presiden-tial debates cover many actively debated topics in the United States, including healthcare, climate change, clean energy, etc. Compared with logical fallacy datasets such as LOGIC (Jin et al., 2022) or LFUD (Li et al., 2024), whose examples are either LLM-generated or taken from educational websites, Elec2Deb20’s sentences are more grounded in the real world as they are directly taken from political debates with robust human annotation. For clarity and ease of understanding for LLMs, we employed the full context of sentences from Elec2Deb20. We excluded the category of Slo-gans, considering that these arguments are usually promises that cannot be verified when the speaker made them. We also excluded the subcategory "flag waving" as justifications based on patriotism is highly subjective and hard to be discussed using objective evidence. Finally, we deleted sentences with 30 words or less, since most of these sentences lack the necessary context for determining logical validity. The preprocessed dataset contains 1317 samples, with the detailed number per LF category shown in Table 17. In addition to the experiments mentioned in the main text, we included results for separate logical fallacy categories in § D. 

B.2 Additional Dataset 

In addition to the logical fallacy examples in Elec2Deb20, we tested Ad Populum, False Dilemma, and Hasty Generalization from CO-COLoFa (Yeh et al., 2024), which includes over 7,000 logical fallacies from various news com-ments. We randomly selected 50 examples per chosen category, and we test BASE, BASE W/PROBLEMS , and LFTutor using the normal student setting discussed in § A.4. Results and discussions are shown in § D. 

C Experiments with other LLMs 

To further evaluate the performance of LFTutor 

on a wider range of LLMs, we conducted exper-iments with B ASE and LFTutor on LLaMA-3.1-405B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Gemini-2.5-pro (Comanici et al., 2025). We used the experiment setting for normal students discussed in § 5.3, and we generated dialogues using 200 sentences ran-domly selected from Elec2Deb20. Results for LLaMA-3.1-405B are shown in Ta-ble 7. LFTutor improves average performance by about 17.3% compared with B ASE , with increases in Stance Change, Lack of Refutation, Lack of Evidence Inquiry, Strategy Fixation, and Unex-plained LF Terms. LFTutor suffers performance loss in Divergence and Passive Guidance, with slight decrease of scores in Repetition. We observe that LLaMA-3.1-405B is weaker in instruction-following compared with GPT-4o, resulting in worse overall performance. Often, LLaMA failed to follow the education strategies given by the prompts, resulting in lack of detailed explanations of logical flaws and lack of effective Socratic Ques-tions. I.1 Participant Selection 

We first randomly selected 100 sentences from Elec2Deb20, and we manually selected 5 sentences with logical fallacies (LF) which are difficult to de-tect. We sent out the pre-study form including these sentences on social media for people to fill out, and we requested people to judge if the given sentences contain logical fallacies, while providing the reasons for their judgments. After gathering results from 59 potential participants, we picked two sentences with the highest number of people who selected "no fallacy", and we selected 20 par-ticipants who think these sentences are logically valid. All participants are fluent in written and spo-ken English, and they all agree to participate in the human evaluation by signing the consent form, whose content is shown in § J.7.

J Ethics and Application of LFTutor 

J.1 Potential Risks 

The main potential risk for users of LFTutor is being misguided by LLMs, due to LLMs’ lack of access to the context of some given sentences. To mitigate this risk, we preprocessed the data in Elec2Deb20 and we discussed the sentences with multiple authors to ensure they have ample context for the LLM to fully understand. We recommend users of LFTutor to provide enough context for sentences with potential logical fallacy, so that it reduces the probability of LLMs generating inac-curate information.

J.4 Intended Use for LFTutor as a Research Artifact 

This paper proposes LFTutor , a tutoring system based on LLM for helping people realize logical fallacies in sentences. 

Intended use: LFTutor is designed for academic research and development of educational tools. It is useful for teaching people to think critically about the logical validity of sentences through Socratic questions and critical argumentation. 

Restrictions: Interactions with LFTutor involve logical fallacies, which is a domain where infor-mation provided may not be easily verified. Thus, usage of LFTutor should only be restricted on es-tablished examples with logical fallacy for research purposes. Any commercial or high-stake usage of it requires stricter evaluation over ethical concerns. 

Ethical Consideration: Although LFTutor con-tains potential for real-world applications of logical fallacy education, we strongly advise the usage of 

LFTutor be restricted to established examples with logical fallacy, as those examples do not leave room for alternative interpretations. Using LFTutor on sentences with uncertain logical validity may result in spread of misinformation.

The teacher often passively follows the student’s lead in the dialogues, and the teacher is easily controlled by the student’s flow of topics. 

Repetition 60% 

Wang et al. (2024a) described teacher’s tendency to repeat answers in question-answering dialogues. The teacher repeats in two ways: sometimes they repeat their reason for believing that the given statement has logical fallacies. In other times, they simply repeat the student’s words that praises the sentence’s logical validity, especially after being convinced by the student. 

Short-Circuiting of Answers 

NA Puech et al. (2024) noted that LLMs lack the planning of sophisticated pedagogical interac-tions, but instead reveals answers too quickly to students. The teacher sometimes tries to point out logical fallacies hidden in the student’s claims, but this is allowed as the goal of LF Education is not simply teaching student an-swers. 

Lack of Learning Objectives 

NA Scarlatos et al. (2025) noted that LLMs lack clear learning objectives for maximizing stu-dent’s learning outcome in educational dia-logues. The teacher’s goal is to persuade the student that the sen-tence has logical fallacies, which the teacher seems to diverge from whenever they try to talk about other topics.

The teacher exhibits Divergence if the dialogue contains responses where it tries to discuss topics unrelated to the logical validity of the given sentence. 

Stance Change 

The teacher exhibits Stance Change if the dialogue contains responses where it explicitly shows agreement to the student that the sentence is logically valid, e.g. "I agree..", "you are right..", and the teacher’s initial position that the sentence contains LF is affected by the student. 

Repetition 

The teacher exhibits Repetition if the dialogue contains responses where it simply repeats or paraphrases the student’s words without further expla-nations or expansions into related topics. 

Lack of Refutation 

The teacher lacks Refutation if the dialogue does not contain any response where it challenge the student’s argument with counterarguments, coun-terexamples, or questions of assumptions. 

Lack of Evidence Inquiry 

The teacher lacks Evidence Inquiry if the dialogue does not contain any response where it to question the student on the sources of evidence supporting their claims. 

Strategy Fixation 

The teacher exhibits Strategy Fixation if the dialogue contains responses where it emphasizes general strategies such as considering broader context or balanced perspectives, without linking those strategies to the exact problem with the validity of the given sentence. 

Unexplained LF Terms 

The teacher shows Unexplained LF Terms if the dialogue contains re-sponses where mentions terms of logical fallacy e.g. strawman argument, ad hominem, without explaining these terms’ definitions clearly or relating them to the logical validity of the given sentence. 

Passive Guidance 

The teacher shows Passive Guidance if for the entire dialogue, it primarily follows the student’s lead of discussion without providing clear direction on how to analyze the logical validity of the given sentence.
