Abstract 

We show every multi-group learner in the transductive setting may incur a multiplicative penalty in its error rate on some group relative to the error rate achievable in the single-group setting, and the penalty can increasing linearly with the number of groups, up to roughly the square-root of the sample size. This stands in stark contrast to optimal multi-group learners in an analogous (group-realizable) statistical setting, where the penalty is always at most logarithmic in the sample size and independent of the number of groups. 

# 1 Introduction 

The multi-group extensions of statistical and online learning frameworks address the need to consider performance of predictors on subpopulations (a.k.a. groups or subgroups ) (Blum and Lykouris, 2020; Rothblum and Yona, 2021; Tosh and Hsu, 2022). In these settings, multi-group learners can achieve nearly the same performance guarantees (e.g., excess risk, regret) for all groups (after suitable rescaling based on the group size) as compared to what is achievable on individual groups. These performance guarantees hold even if (1) group-specific optimal predictors disagree where the groups intersect, and (2) the number of groups is very large or even infinite. The statistical cost is relatively mild and typically amortized by the per-group excess risk or regret whenever the “complexity” of the family of groups is dominated by that of the performance benchmark class. For example, in the multi-group agnostic learning scenario (Rothblum and Yona, 2021; Tosh and Hsu, 2022), a multi-group learner can produce a predictor f such that, for all groups S ∈ S,risk of f on S ≤ min

Indeed, the difficulty of multi-group learning appears to primarily be algorithmic: for example, the classical principle of Empirical Risk Minimization over the benchmark class is not always sufficient for multi-group learning; instead successful multi-group learners typically use ensemble methods or other forms of “improper learning”. In this paper, we initiate the study of the multi-group extension of another standard learning setting— transductive learning (Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1974; Haussler et al., 1994)—and we find that, there, all multi-group learners must incur a high statistical cost compared to what is achievable in the usual (single-group) scenario. # 2 Background on statistical and transductive learning 

We introduce transductive learning in both the (standard) single-group and the multi-group settings. For context, we also introduce single- and multi-group statistical learning, and discuss the achievable excess error rates in these settings as established in prior works. Additional related works are discussed in Appendix A.

# 3 Transductive learning and orientations of one-inclusion graphs 

This section sets up the graph-theoretic framework of Haussler et al. (1994) for (both single-group and multi-group) transductive learning in which prediction strategies are orientations of a certain 4induced subgraph of the n-dimensional Boolean hypercube. An orientation ϕ : E → V of an undirected graph G = ( V, E ) is a function that maps each edge 

e to an incident vertex ϕ(e) ∈ e. Graphically, ϕ produces a directed edge ( u, v ) when ϕ({u, v }) = u.The out-degree of a vertex v ∈ V in a subgraph H of G (with edges E(H)) oriented by ϕ is outdeg H,ϕ (v) := |ϕ−1(v) ∩ E(H)| = |{ e ∈ E(H) | ϕ(e) = v}| .

The n-dimensional Boolean hypercube Qn is the undirected graph with vertex set {0, 1}n and edge set containing all pairs of vertices {u, v } that differ in exactly one position. Given n points 

U = ( x1, . . . , x n) ∈ X n from a domain X and a concept class C ⊆ { 0, 1}X defined over this domain, the one-inclusion graph GC| U of C with respect to U is the induced subgraph of Qn corresponding to the vertex set V (GC| U ) := {(c(x1), . . . , c (xn)) | c ∈ C} of “behaviors” of C on U ; let E(GC| U ) denote the edges in this subgraph. Although we have regarded S as a family of subsets of the domain X , it is clear that since 

U ∈ X n is fixed, we can identify each S ∈ S with a subset of [ n]. So henceforth, we overload the symbol S to represent the subset of indices i′ ∈ [n] with xi′ ∈ S, and thus also regard S as a subset of 2 [n]. In fact, any induced subgraph of Qn can be regarded as a one-inclusion graph over the domain X := [ n] for a concept class specified by the vertices of the induced subgraph. 

Prediction strategies as orientations of a one-inclusion graph. We now explain the sense in which a transductive learner’s prediction strategy corresponds to orientations of GC| U (whether in the single-group or multi-group setting). After U = ( x1, . . . , x n) ∈ X n and C are fixed, the one-inclusion graph GC| U is determined, and the transductive learning scenario proceeds as follows. 1. Nature chooses a vertex y = ( y1, . . . , y n) ∈ V (GC| U ). 2. The test point index i ∈ [n] is chosen uniformly at random. 3. The learner receives only the labels ( yj )j̸ =i.4. The learner makes a (possibly randomized) prediction ˆ yi of the label yi.The labels ( yj )j∈[n] correspond to some vertex in GC| U , but the learner only has ( yj )j̸ =i; these labels will be consistent with either one or two vertices in GC| U .

• If there is only one consistent vertex in GC| U , then the label yi is already known to the learner. 

• If there are two consistent vertices u and v (differing only in the i-th position, ui̸ = vi, so 

{u, v } is an edge in GC| U ), then the prediction of the learner corresponds to a choice of one of these vertices, i.e., an orientation of the edge {u, v }. By convention, if the learner predicts the label ui (say), then we say the learner’s orientation ϕ of GC| U has ϕ({u, v }) = v.Every choice of the target concept by Nature corresponds to some vertex in GC| U , and every choice of i ∈ [n] (the index of the test point) specifies a potential edge incident with Nature’s chosen vertex. Thus, a prediction strategy of the learner corresponds to an orientation of GC| U . And vice versa: an orientation ϕ of GC| U defines a prediction strategy for every choice of i ∈ [n]. A learner that makes randomized predictions is specified by pairs of non-negative numbers 

p{u,v },u and p{u,v },v summing to one for every edge {u, v } ∈ E(GC| U ): with probability p{u,v },v , the learner predicts the label consistent with vertex u; with remaining probability 1 − p{u,v },v = p{u,v },u ,it predicts the label consistent with vertex v. # 6 Discussion 

The main result of this paper shows that the difficulty of multi-group transductive learning relative to standard (single-group) transductive learning can be much greater than that for statistical learning. We note that the analogous price in statistical learning against the oblivious adversary is at most log (n), but we do not know of a comparable lower bound. Although the simultaneous version of the multi-group statistical error rate is not really analogous to the multi-group transductive error rate, its analogous price is at most O(1 + max S∈S log |S|/d C| S ), but again it is not known if the log |S| (or even the VC dimension of S) is needed in the upper bound. So, the price may be even smaller in these statistical settings, making the contrast with the transductive setting potentially starker. We are not aware of multi-group regret lower bounds in online settings, although some upper bounds (based on randomized algorithms) have been given by Blum and Lykouris (2020). Closing the gaps in the statistical and online settings are interesting open problems. Finally, it would also be interesting to explore multi-group extensions of agnostic transductive learning (Long, 1998; Asilis et al., 2024).

# A Additional related work 

Statistical multi-group learning was initially studied by Rothblum and Yona (2021) and later by Tosh and Hsu (2022), building on the prior development in the online setting by Blum and Lykouris (2020) (with later algorithmic developments by Acharya et al. (2024) and Deng et al. (2024)). Tosh and Hsu (2022) introduced the group-realizability assumption discussed in Section 2.2, which was also studied by Ardeshir et al. (2026). The relationship between multi-group learning and other learning frameworks such as multicalibration (H´ ebert-Johnson et al., 2018), omniprediction (Gopalan et al., 2022), and multiaccuracy (Kim et al., 2019) has been explored by Haghtalab et al. (2023) and Balakrishnan et al. (2026). Sample complexity of multicalibration and omniprediction has also been studied in recent works (Gibbs and Tibshirani, 2025; Collina et al., 2026). Statistical multi-group active learning was studied by Rittler and Chaudhuri (2023), where the goal is to improve the label complexity through adaptivity. The focus of our paper is multi-group transductive learning, which has not been previously studied. Haussler et al. (1994) introduced and analyzed a learning algorithm based on the one-inclusion graph for the (single-group) transductive learning setting, and showed that it also yields a learning algorithm for various statistical settings (although its error rate may not have optimal dependence on the “confidence parameter” (Aden-Ali et al., 2023a)). This algorithm has been extended for other learning settings, such as multi-class learning (Rubinstein et al., 2009; Asilis et al., 2024), real-valued prediction (Bartlett and Long, 1998), and agnostic learning (Long, 1998; Asilis et al., 2024) to name a few. The upper bound in our paper continues this line of work by providing an extension to multi-group learning. The presence of large stars in the one-inclusion graph has been identified as a barrier for various statistical learning settings (Hanneke and Yang, 2015; Hanneke, 2016b; Aden-Ali et al., 2023a). A single large star in the one-inclusion graph is not necessarily a barrier in (multi-group) transductive learning, since stars do not have high edge density (ratio of number of edges to number of vertices). The lower bound in our paper constructs a one-inclusion graph with high overall edge density in which every vertex is the center of some large star in a subgraph defined by a subset of dimensions.
