# Theory and internal structure of ADER-DG method for partial differential equations 

## Popov I.S. ∗

Highly accurate stability boundary values for the ADER-DG method with the LST-DG predictor are obtained for arbitrary degrees 

N of basis polynomials. It is found that, in the linear case, instability is violated precisely when one of the matrix eigenvalues reaches λ = −1, regardless of the phase θ of the numerical solution. A rigorous mathematical framework for the stability of the ADER-DG method is developed. The stability condition is significantly simplified, reducing it to the problem of calculating the roots of polynomials in the Courant number CFL, from the initially complex problem of finding the maximum spectral radius of a matrix dependent on the continuous phase θ ∈ [0 , 2π]. The maximum of the Courant numbers CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG method are calculated. These results are new and very convenient for practical use. A comparison of the obtained results with existing results reveals differences that may be significant for the selection of calculation parameters using the ADER-DG method, especially for high degrees N . It is shown that widely used existing estimates CFL max (N ) ∝ 1/(2 N + 1) are overestimated. An interesting qualitative asymptotic CFL max (N ) ∝ (N + 1) 2 is obtained. A rigorous direct proof of the approximation of the ADER-DG method is presented. Approximation orders p = N + 1 for arbitrary degrees N are rigorously derived. A set of numerical experiments is carried out to apply the ADER-DG method to solving both a linear advection equation and an Euler system of equations. The results obtained in these calculations confirm the theoretical results well. In particular, an excess of the Courant number over the CFL max (N ) by even 1% in the linear case immediately leads to significant instability of the numerical solution. The obtained quantitative estimates of the boundary Courant number in the nonlinear case are somewhat underestimated – by no more than 5%, which is due to the diffusivity and stability of the approximate Riemann solver. Empirical convergence orders are obtained, which are in good agreement with the theoretical results p = N + 1 for the orders of approximation. 

Highlights: 

• A new deep stability theory for the ADER-DG method with an LST-DG predictor of arbitrarily high order is constructed. 

• Simple polynomial equations for the maxi-mum Courant numbers for the ADER-DG method of arbitrarily high order are derived. 

• The maximum Courant numbers for the ADER-DG method are calculated for degrees 1–12 of basis polynomials. 

• The stability limits of the ADER-DG method of arbitrarily high order are shown upon reaching the maximum Courant num-ber for linear and nonlinear systems of equa-tions. 

• A direct approximation theory for the ADER-DG method with an LST-DG predic-tor of arbitrarily high order is constructed.

## 1 Introduction 

Finite-element ADER-DG numerical methods have an arbitrarily high order and are among the most accurate numerical methods for solving systems of partial differential equations (PDE systems) [107]. Titarev and Toro [100, 102] developed the ADER paradigm for use in finite-volume methods, and the main innovation was the use of a solution to the generalized Riemann problem based on calculating the evolution of a local space-time solution on the interfaces of finite-volume cells using the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya procedure, based on expanding the solution into high-order Taylor series in coordinates and time (see also [76]). The ADER paradigm was significantly developed in subsequent works [101, 103, 104] by Titarev and Toro. Dumbser et al [40, 65] modified the ADER paradigm for finite-volume methods based on the use of a discrete space-time solution obtained in a separate stage of the local space-time DG-predictor (LST-DG predictor), and showed higher solution accuracy than the original version, especially for stiff problems. Finite-element ADER-DG methods are fundamentally linear methods for solving PDE systems, so they are subject to the well-known Godunov theorem and such methods require limiters to preserve the monotonicity of the numerical solution. Currently, finite-element ADER-DG methods are based on the development of the Multi-dimensional Optimal Order Detection (MOOD) paradigm [27, 35, 36, 74], which resulted in the development [48, 113] of finite-element ADER-DG methods with a posteriori correction of the solution in subcells by a finite-volume limiter, which can be the finite-volume ADER-WENO method [43, 47]. This approach made it possible to preserve the monotonicity of the numerical solution and the subgrid resolution characteristic of DG methods [48]. However, the further development of purely finite-volume methods of the ADER family, carried out in the works [12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 19], is not directly related to the ADER-DG method with a posteriori correction of the solution. The current modern state and formulations of finite-element ADER-DG numerical methods with the LST-DG predictor are presented in [3, 21, 41, 44, 56, 57, 91, 105, 107]. It is necessary to note the historical aspect that the DG methods were created in the work [90] for numerical solution of the neutron transport equations. Cockburn, Shu et al in a series of works [28, 29, 30, 31, 32] created an accurate and thoroughly developed mathematical basis of DG methods, which stimulated their further development and use for solving a wide class of problems. The ADER-DG numerical methods with the LST-DG predictor are widely used in solving problems of classical ideal and dissipative hydrodynamics and magnetohydrodynamics [20, 37, 53, 114], relativistic hydrodynamics and magnetohydrodynamics [38, 45, 52, 58], which are characterized by

In recent years, significant interest [42, 46, 79] has been associated with the development and use of numerical ADER-DG methods for solving Einstein equations of general relativity in the formulations of first-order hyperbolic systems [5, 8, 9, 10, 60, 92, 109]. The ADER-DG numerical methods are also used to solve the equations of motion of deformable media [21, 41, 99], seismic wave propagation [7, 41, 91, 110], the equations of shallow water theory [39, 54, 89], simulating blood flow [78], simulating compressible barotropic two-fluid flows [93] and nonlinear dispersive systems [22]. A special place is occupied by problems associated with the study of the reacting flows, which are characterized by abnormally high stiffness, for which the finite element ADER-DG methods and finite volume ADER-WENO methods were developed and studied in the works [40, 65, 82, 84]. Application of finite element ADER-DG method for solving a wide range of other problems are also presented in [41, 91]. Finite-element ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor are developed for structured Cartesian meshes [42, 46, 48, 52, 113], using the adaptive mesh refinement paradigm [43, 47, 53, 58, 114], unstructured triangular and tetrahedral meshes [3, 37, 41, 44, 45, 89] and moving meshes [16] (see also [107]). A detailed discussion of the features of the mathematical and algorithmic formulations of numerical methods and their software implementation is presented in works [21, 25, 26, 41, 85, 91, 96, 97]. The implementation of the ADER-DG numerical method on a GPU is presented in [24]. Dumbser et al developed well-balanced ADER-DG method for the Euler equations in general relativity problems [46] and for the multilayer shallow water equations [54]. Zhao et al developed path-conservative discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method to solve shallow water equations [115]. In [59] Gaburro et al developed entropy preserving ADER-DG schemes (see also [71]). Based on the ADER paradigm, a SPH numerical method was created [6], which demonstrated a very high quality of modeling hydrodynamic and magnetohydrodynamic flows with discontinuities, compared to classical SPH methods. The numerical methods of the ADER family are significantly interconnected with the deferred correction (DeC) numerical methods, which showed Han Veiga et al [64]. DeC methods have a long history and their application goes back to [33], and are effectively used to solve partial differential equations [1, 2] and ordinary differential systems of equations [49, 49, 73, 77]. DeC methods, like the methods of the ADER family, allow one to obtain an arbitrarily high order and are characterized by a high accuracy of the numerical solution. The current state of research on DeC methods [64] shows that they compete with methods of the ADER family. The relationship between the ADER paradigm and the DeC paradigm are discussed in [64, 75]. An important feature of the ADER-DG method, like other DG methods, is a very strict limitation on the time step and on the permissible maximum Courant number CFL max , especially for high degrees N of the basis polynomials used in the DG representation of the solution (in particular, Huynh developed [66, 67, 68] DG methods with flux reconstruction to weaken this limitation; see also the discussion in [72, 81, 94, 108, 111]). ADER-DG methods are characterized by very small values of the Courant number CFL max , which is used in particular for subcell limiting of the solution with finite-volume limiters, which do not have such a strict limitation on the time step and can reliably compute solutions even for smaller coordinate grid steps [48, 53, 58, 113, 114]. There are several different estimates of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG method with the LST-DG predictor. Many works (for example [47, 48, 52, 82, 84, 113]) employ the widely used estimate CFL max (N ) ∝ 1/(2 N + 1), which, in particular, forms the basis for a certain rule for choosing the number of subcells Ns = 2 N + 1 on each cell for a posteriori subgrid constraint of a solution by a finite-volume limiter [47, 48, 113], for which the Courant number is usually expected to be approximately one. In [38] the estimates of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG method with the LST-DG predictor were first calculated for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 4of basis polynomials. In a subsequent work [58] estimates of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG method with the LST-DG predictor were calculated for basis polynomials for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 6 of basis polynomials, which were in good agreement with the previous estimates [38] in the intersection region N ⩽ 4. A new study [11] has recently been published in which estimates of the maximum Courant number have been calculated for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 9 of basis polynomials, and here differences with existing results [38, 58] have begun to arise, which are described in detail in [11]. It should be noted that estimates [11, 38, 58] for large degrees N ≫ 1 of the basis polynomials differ significantly from the widely used estimate CFL max (N ) ∝ 1/(2 N + 1), which is significantly, sometimes by more than twofold, overestimated. Therefore, using estimates CFL max (N ) ∝ 1/(2 N + 1) requires introducing an additional empirical factor C( N ) ⩽ 1, with C( N ) ≪ 1 for large degrees N of the basis polynomials. The calculations in [11, 38, 58] were based on the Von Neumann stability criterion (spectral stability criterion) and were technically implemented by calculating the maximum spectral radius of the amplification matrix (the matrix of the evolution operator for a single time step ∆ t) for a continuous set of phase θ ∈ [0 , 2π]. Clearly, such calculations require an estimate of the robustness of the eigenvalues and an extremely detailed discretization of the continuous set of phase θ ∈ [0 , 2π]. Moreover, the evolution operator matrix itself for a single time step, when directly calculated, even in the one-dimensional case, requires multiplying ( N + 1) × (N + 1) 2 and ( N + 1) 2 × (N + 1) matrices to obtain a ( N + 1) × (N + 1) matrix, which also affects the final calculation error for large degrees N of the basis polynomials, when using standard machine arithmetic to represent real numbers with single- or double-precision floating-point numbers. In this work, an attempt is made to construct the consistent and rigorous stability theory for a classical ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor based on the Von Neumann stability criterion. This theory is completely free from the problem of enumerating a continuous set of phase θ ∈ [0 , 2π]. The theory is built on only one empirically substantiated assumption: the numerical method becomes unstable when one of the eigenvalues λk of the matrix of the evolution operator for a single time step ∆ t reaches λ = −1, regardless of the phase for which this occurs. The resulting equations, the solution of which determines the maximum Courant numbers, are simple finite polynomial equations. These results are new and very convenient for practical use: a detailed Table 3 of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) for degrees 

N = 1 , . . . , 12 of basis polynomials is presented; closed-form polynomial equations for the maximum values of the Courant number CFL max (N )for an arbitrary degree N of basis polynomials are derived. A rigorous, consistent, and constructive approximation theory for the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is also constructed, based on direct derivation rather than on integral and variational estimates. The results of numerical experiments are presented, which are in good agreement with the developed theory and demonstrate the limitations associated with incorrect estimates of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ). The numerical experiments are carried out for a linear advection equations and a nonlinear system of equations — the Euler system of equations. The article is divided into four main Sections. Section 2 “General description of the numerical method” presents a detailed description of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor for the linear advection equation, for which a direct rigorous approximation theory and stability theory are constructed. This Section introduces all the basic concepts and notations, as well as the main properties of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor, which are used in the subsequent Sections for research. Section 3 “Approximation analysis” presents the direct rigorous approximation theory of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. Subsection 3.1 “Discrete space-time solution” is devoted to the derivation of the order and approximation conditions for the local discrete spatiotemporal solution obtained at the LST-DG predictor stage. Subsection 3.2 “Corrected solution at time steps” is devoted to the derivation of the approximation order for the adjusted solution determined at time steps. This Section also introduces and rigorously proves properties of the matrices that arise during a detailed analysis of the numerical method and are used in the next, main Section of the article. Section 4 “Stability analysis” is the key Section of this article and is divided into four Subsections. Subsection 4.1 “Qualitative considerations” is devoted to the derivation and justification of a new empirical estimate for the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor, which has an asymptotic behavior CFL max (N ) ∝ (N + 1) 2 for sufficiently large degrees N ≫ 1 of the basis polynomials. Subsection 4.2 “Statement of the problem of quantitative considerations” is devoted to a detailed derivation of the matrices required for a detailed stability analysis of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor, as well as the derivation and proof of the relations required for further research. The constant-preserving property of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is also rigorously proven. Subsection 4.3 “Empirical considerations” is devoted to the justification of the empirical assumption that stability is violated when one of the (or several) eigenvalues λk of the matrix of the evolution operator for a single time step ∆ t reaches the value of λ = −1. Subsection 4.4 “Rigorous quantitative considerations” is devoted to a detailed derivation of equations whose solutions determine the maximum Courant numbers and are simple finite polynomial equations. This Subsection presents the main results, including the obtained values of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N )for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 12 of the basis polynomials and polynomial equations for the values of the maximum Courant number CFL max (N ) for an arbitrary degree N . Section 5 “Applications” contains problem statements and results of numerical experiments that confirm previously 2developed theoretical results. Subsection 5.1 “Linear advection equation” is devoted to numerical experiments for a linear advection equation. Subsection 5.2 “Nonlinear Euler equations” is devoted to numerical experiments for the system of Euler equations, which was chosen as a good example of a nonlinear problem for which ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor are widely applicable.

## 4 Stability analysis 

This Section is devoted to the stability analysis of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. Subsection 4.1 “Qualitative considerations” presents a qualitative analysis of the ADER-DG numerical method’s stability and obtains an asymptotic estimate of the boundary Courant number CFL max (N ) for high degree N ≫ 1 of basis polynomials. Subsection 4.2 “Statement of the problem of quantitative considerations” presents a detailed derivation of the relations necessary for a rigorous stability analysis of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor within the framework of the Von Neumann criterion (spectral stability criterion). Subsection 4.3 “Empirical considerations” presents a comprehensive set of empirical results from analyzing the spectrum of the evolution operator matrix for a single time step. Based on this analysis, several assumptions are made and substantiated regarding the localization of the eigenvalue boundary upon reaching which the stability of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is lost. Subsection 4.4 “Rigorous quantitative considerations”, based on the obtained empirical results, significantly simplifies the general stability problem of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. This results in a set of polynomials whose roots determine the boundary Courant number CFL max (N ). 10 4.1 Qualitative considerations

The obtained results showed that it is better to analyze the stability boundary CFL max (104) as follows: first, transform the original problem ρ[R(CFL , θ )] ⩽ 1 in order to exclude the phase θ included in the analyzed expression in the form exp( ±iθ ), and then try to simplify this expression as much as possible under the assumption of searching for the boundary value of the Courant number CFL max for which one of the eigenvalues λk of the matrix (for any phase θ) reaches the value λ = −1. One interesting detail revealed by analyzing the dependencies presented in Figures 3 and 4 should be noted. Although the stability 

ρ[R(CFL , θ )] ⩽ 1 of the numerical method ADER-DG with LST-DG predictor is lost when one of the eigenvalues reaches λ = −1, for sufficiently large polynomial degrees N , even at lower Courant numbers CFL ≪ CFL max (104), some eigenvalues λk of the matrix R(CFL , θ )(83) of the evolution operator R with absolute values |λk | greater than one remain in the vicinity of point λ = +1, approximately in the region of the eigenvalue phase arg λk ∈ [−π/ 4, +π/ 4]. However, they exceed unity only by ∼ 10 −5–10 −9, and the Courant numbers CFL for which the condition is achieved in this region are unusually small — 1 .5–4 .0 times smaller than the boundary value CFL max . This phenomenon is not an error in the eigenvalue calculations (error ∼ 10 −994 –10 −997 ). The values |λmax | presented in Figures 3 and 4 are derived with four decimal places, so this phenomenon is not observed in this representation. Many calculations performed numerically in all previous years (see [16, 21, 41, 42, 44, 52, 82, 84, 85, 91, 114]) show that such a strong underestimation of the stability boundary CFL max is unnecessary. Otherwise, the studies would use “effective Courant numbers” (74) C ∼ 0.05–0 .10, which would be very significant but has never been observed in calculations. In “real-world” calculations, especially for quasilinear systems of equations, approximate Riemann solvers are used, charac-terized by a small but nonzero diffusivity that overcomes grows of the functions λnk with eigenvalues λk greater than one by absolute value on ∼ 10 −5–10 −9: |λnk | = | exp( n ln λk )| ∼ exp(10 −9n)–exp(10 −5n). This effect can be classified as practically unobservable which could only complicate the stability analysis of the numerical method ADER-DG with LST-DG predictor. Mathematically, this phenomenon can be explained as follows. Despite the fact that the LST-DG predictor (13) is implicit ((23) and (24)), in the case of a linear advection equation (1), due to its linearity and the nilpotency D N +1 = 0 of the differentiation matrix D, the final solution 18 ˆq of the system of algebraic equations of the LST-DG predictor represents a classical Taylor series expansion (40) of the solution up to and including the N -degree term in τ (3). This is mathematically completely equivalent to the explicit method, whose “stability function” represents a classical Taylor series expansion of the evolution operator, and the approximation order (43) is determined by the truncation error of the series (43). In the case of the implicit method [80, 88], this “stability function” would be a fractional polynomial function like the Pad´ e approximant. Explicit methods are characterized by precisely such peculiarities in the structure of the boundary of the stability region [23, 34, 61, 62].

However, in computational physics, it is clear that the computa-tional experiment is everything, so this final Section 5 “Applications” presents the results of applying the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor to solving both the original linear advec-tion equation (1) and the nonlinear problem for the Euler system as a characteristic example of a quasilinear hyperbolic system of equations. Direct checks of the obtained approximation orders p = N + 1 and the obtained boundary values of the Courant numbers CFL max (N )defining the stability boundary are performed. Although empirical convergence orders have been calculated numerous times in previous studies of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predic-tor (see [11, 16, 21, 38, 41, 42, 44, 45, 52, 58, 82, 84, 85, 91, 114]), in this work, the calculations are performed for a high accurately defined stability boundary CFL max (N ). All calculations are performed using a computational framework developed for these purposes, a detailed description of which is presented in [82, 84, 85]. For the calculations, only the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is used, and the possibility of a posteriori limitation using a subcell finite-volume limiter is disabled. Subsection 5.1 “Linear advection equation” presents a set of numer-ical experiments for a linear one-dimensional advection equation (1). Analysis of the obtained results showed that the boundary values of the Courant number calculated CFL max (N ) in the previous Section 4 “Stability analysis” are determined with sufficient accuracy and correctness, and that going beyond the stability boundary CFL > CFL max (N )determined by them immediately leads to instability of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. Moreover, in the stability region up to the stability boundary, the empirical convergence orders correspond well to the approximation orders p = N + 1 of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor calculated in Section 3 “Approximation analysis”. Subsection 5.2 “Nonlinear Euler equations” presents a set of numerical experiments for a system of quasilinear Euler equations. Analysis of the obtained results showed that the results 27 obtained for a linear one-dimensional advection equation can, to a certain extent, also be applied to a quasilinear system of Euler equations. However, the use of dissipative Riemann solvers leads to a slight expansion of the stability region of the numerical method.

## 5.2 Nonlinear Euler equations 

The results presented in the previous Subsection 5.1 “Linear advection equation” pertain only to the accuracy and stability of ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor for linear problems (1). Clearly, the results of linear analysis based on the study of the linear advection equation (1) can be extended to the more general nonlinear case of systems of quasilinear hyperbolic equations, since they can be represented as a system of advection equations for Riemann invariants (though, of course, a quasilinear system of equations). However, certain differences in the results from the linear case can be expected in nonlinear case. This is primarily due to the fact that for quasilinear systems, it is common to use approximate Riemann solvers [106] in calculations, which can exhibit significant numerical dissipation, which can alter the boundaries of the stability region CFL max (N ) of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. ## 6 Conclusion 

In conclusion, it should be noted that this study yielded very accurate values for the stability boundary of the Courant numbers CFL max of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor within the framework of the Von Neumann stability criterion (spectral stability criterion) for arbitrary degrees N of basis polynomials {φ} (6). It was found that, in the linear case, instability occurs precisely when one of the matrix R(CFL , θ ) (83) of the evolution operator R eigenvalues λk reaches λ = −1, regardless of the phase θ (77) of the numerical solution. It is important to note that the overall instability of the numerical solution is associated with the eigenvalue λ = −1, which begins to significantly exceed unity, while the phase determines θ = k∆x the harmonic wave number k for which instability occurs. Based on the obtained and substantiated empirical reasoning, it was constructed a rigorous mathematical theory (generally, with some limitations, free from the choice of specific basis polynomials {φ} (6)) and significantly simplify the stability condition ρ[R(CFL , θ )] ⩽ 1, reducing it from the complex problem of finding the maximum spectral radius ρ[R(CFL , θ )] of the matrix R(CFL , θ ), dependent on the continuous phase 

θ ∈ [0 , 2π], to the problem of calculating the roots of ( N + 1)-degree polynomials F1(CFL) = 0 and F2(CFL) = 0 (158) in the Courant number 33 CFL. This is technically very simple and can be performed with arbitrarily high accuracy, even without significant computational resources. It was found that, despite instability within the expected boundaries of the Courant number when one of the matrix R(CFL , θ ) (83) eigenvalues 

λk reaches λ = −1, small unstable eigenvalues with phase arg λk ∈ [−π/ 4, +π/ 4] still arise. However, their absolute values |λk | exceeding one are very small, and the corresponding eigenvalues λk have only a positive real part ℜλk > 0, which does not lead to the development of unstable oscillations. The boundary values of the Courant numbers CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor were calculated for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 12 of the basis polynomials. A comparison of the obtained results with existing literature data [11, 38, 58] revealed differences in CFL max for certain degrees N of the basis polynomials, especially for large degrees N ∼ 9, that could be significant for the selection of calculation parameters using the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor. It was shown that widely used existing estimates of the boundary value of the Courant number CFL max (N ) are somewhat overestimated, necessitating the introduction of an “effective Courant number” C (74), which will be significantly less than unity. The choice of this effective Courant number C is related precisely to the discrepancy between this common estimate and the correct one CFL max (N ). An interesting qualitative asymptotic CFL max (N ) ∝ (N + 1) 2

(73) dependence of the boundary value of the Courant number CFL max (N ) for the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is obtained. This allows for computational cost estimates when using very high-degree basis polynomials and is in relatively good agreement with the quantitative results CFL max (N ) for N = 1 , . . . , 12 obtained in this work. A rigorous direct proof of the approximation of the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor is presented. It is shown that, despite the implicit nature of the LST-DG predictor in the linear case, the local discrete space-time solution is in fact calculated using an explicit scheme with a numerical evolution operator representing the first N + 1 terms of the Taylor series for the solution. Approximation orders p = N + 1 for arbitrary degrees N of basis polynomials are rigorously derived. A set of numerical experiments was carried out to apply the ADER-DG numerical method with the LST-DG predictor to solving a linear advection equation (1) to verify the obtained theoretical results. Results were obtained for degrees N = 1 , . . . , 12 of the basis polynomials. The results obtained in these calculations clearly confirm the theoretical results. In particular, even a 1% excess of the Courant number CFL above the boundary value CFL max (N ) immediately leads to significant instability of the numerical solution. Empirical convergence orders p were obtained, which are in good agreement with the theoretical results p = N + 1 for the approximation orders. To determine the applicability of the obtained results to “real-world problems”, a set of numerical experiments was carried out for the Euler system of equations (169) as a good special case of a nonlinear problem. It was shown that the obtained quantitative estimates of the boundary value of the Courant number CFL max (N ) are also applicable in this nonlinear case; however, they are somewhat underestimated — by no more than 5%. This is due to the use of the HLLE approximate Riemann solver, which is characterized by strong diffusivity and high stability, so it suppresses unstable harmonics until their increment becomes too large. In general, these features of the transfer of stability analysis results from the case of linear problems (1) to nonlinear problem (169) are expected. In the nonlinear case (169), empirical convergence orders p were also obtained, which are in good agreement with the theoretical results p = N + 1 for the orders of approximation.
