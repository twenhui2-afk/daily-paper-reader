# Tree-cut decompositions for displaying undominated edge-ends 

Max Pitz Lucas Real ∗

June 19, 2026 

Abstract 

We prove that every graph admits a linked, componental, rooted tree-cut decomposition of finite adhesion that displays all undominated edge-ends. As a first application, we deduce that this tree-cut decomposition also displays the edge-degrees of all undominated edge-ends. For locally finite graphs — where every end is an undominated edge-end — this yields a linked tree-cut decomposition of finite adhesion into 

finite parts that displays all ends and their edge-degrees. As a second application, this latter tree-cut decomposition yields short, unified deduc-tions of Thomassen’s theorem on boundary-linked finite partitions, and of Bruhn and Stein’s characterisation of Eulerian locally finite graphs in terms of even ends. 

# 1 Introduction 

Tree-cut decompositions, introduced by Wollan [16], are the edge-connectivity analogue of the classical tree-decompositions of Robertson and Seymour [13]. For many applications, however, one wishes the adhesion sets of such a decomposition to faithfully reflect the connectivity of the underlying graph; for tree-decompositions of finite graphs, this is captured by Thomas’s notion of a linked tree-decomposition [14]. Two extensions of Thomas’s theorem have since been pursued: from finite to infinite graphs with tree-decompositions into finite parts by Albrechtsen, Jacobs, Knappe and Pitz [2], and from vertex separators to edge separators (to linked tree-cut decompositions for finite graphs) by Giannopoulou, Kwon, Raymond and Thilikos [7]. The goal of this paper is to combine these two threads, establishing linked tree-cut decompositions for arbitrary infinite graphs.

1.2 Displaying edge-degrees As our first application of Theorem 1.1, the witnessing tree-cut decomposition displays the edge-degrees of all undominated edge-ends, resolving the edge-analogue of a question of Halin from 1977 [10] asking, for a locally finite graph, for a tree-decomposition that simultaneously displays all ends and their combined degrees. Recall that the edge-degree of an edge-end ω ∈ ΩE (G) is defined as 

deg( ω) = sup {|R| : R ⊆ ω is a family of edge-disjoint rays } ∈ N ∪ {∞} .

Theorem 1.2. Every graph G admits a tree-cut decomposition (T, V) of finite adhesion that displays all undominated edge-ends together with their edge-degrees: for each displayed ω with corresponding ray Rω = t0t1t2 . . . in T starting at the root, deg( ω) = lim inf n |δ(Ctn )|.

For locally finite graphs, every end is an undominated edge-end, and we deduce the following strengthened version: 

Theorem 1.3. Every locally finite connected graph G admits a linked tree-cut decomposition of finite adhesion into finite parts, with the decomposition tree itself locally finite, that displays all ends of G and their edge-degrees. 

The reduction from Theorem 1.1 to Theorem 1.2 is short: applying the linkedness property between minimal adhesion sets on the ray Rω forces the edge-degree to match the lim inf along 

Rω. The additional finite-bags conclusion of Theorem 1.3 then follows from Kőnig’s result that every infinite locally finite graph contains a ray. Both reductions are carried out in Section 3.

1.4 Eulerian locally finite graphs: Bruhn–Stein’s characterisation Following Bruhn and Stein [4], an edge-end ω ∈ ΩE (G) of a locally finite graph G is even if there is some finite set of vertices S∗ ⊆ V (G) such that, for every finite S ⊆ V (G) with S ⊇ S∗, the maximum size of an edge-disjoint family R ⊆ ω starting at vertices of S is even; otherwise ω is an odd 

end. Following the topological approach of Diestel and Kühn in [6], an Eulerian locally finite graph is one whose Freudenthal compactification admits a continuous image of the unit circle traversing each edge exactly once; equivalently, every finite cut has even size, recovering the familiar finite-graph criterion. # 2 Preliminaries 

2.1 Edge-connectivity and edge-ends The edge-ends of a fixed infinite graph G were first discussed by Hahn, Laviolette and Širaň in [8] after naturally modifying the well-known definition of (vertex-)ends from Halin in [9]. In both cases, these objects comprise equiva-lence classes of one-way infinite paths of G after identifying those that are either infinitely vertex-connected or infinitely edge-connected, depending on the chosen structure. As well as its formalization, basic properties regarding the classical (vertex-)end space Ω( G) of G can be found in the textbook [5, Chapter 8]. In order to first compile basic terminology on edge-connectivity, however, recall the notations 

E(A, B ) := {xy ∈ E(G) : x ∈ A, y ∈ B}, E(C) := E(C, C ) and δ(C) := E(C, V (G) \ C) for given sets of vertices A, B, C ⊆ V (G). In particular, δ(C) is often referred to as the cut or 

boundary of C, while the subgraph induced by C and this set of edges may be written as C.Then, N (C) := C \ C shall be used to denote the set of endpoints in V (G) \ C of the edges from 

δ(C). Furthermore, C ⊆ V (G) is called a region of G if G[C] is connected and its boundary 

δ(C) is finite. On the other hand, two subgraphs H and H′ of G are said to be separated by 4a finite set of edges F ⊆ E(G) if there is no path in G \ F containing both a vertex of H and one of H′.Now, aiming to properly revisit the literature on edge-ends, recall that a ray in G is any one-way infinite path. When presenting it in terms of its vertex set as R := v0v1v2 . . . , we also refer to v0 and v0v1 as its starting vertex and starting edge respectively. In addition, we call the infinite connected subgraphs of R its tails , which then have the form vnvn+1 vn+2 . . . 

for some n ∈ N. Considering that, a second ray S of G is said to be edge-equivalent to R

if no tail of S can be separated from a tail of R by a finite set of edges. This now defines an equivalence relation over the family of all rays in G, whose quotient shall then be written as 

ΩE (G) := {[R]E : R is a ray of G}. Following the original concept from [8], the edge-end of the ray R corresponds to its edge-equivalence class [R]E . In particular, for every finite set of edges F ⊆ E(G), the connected component of G\F containing a tail of R is uniquely determined upon this edge-end and, hence, can be denoted simply by CE (F, [R]E ). If v / ∈ CE (F, [R]E ), we say that such a vertex v ∈ V (G) is separated from [R]E by F .In general, we may use lower-case Greek letters such as “ ω” and “ ε” for referring to the elements of ΩE (G), besides also saying that some ω ∈ ΩE (G) lives in a subgraph C of G if all its representatives have tails in C. When C is actually a region, this is equivalently written as 

CE (δ(C), ω ) = C. Moreover, following a definition introduced by Thomassen in [15] (although there for locally-finite graphs), we say that C is boundary-linked to ω if there is a family 

R ⊆ ω of |δ(C)| many edge-disjoint paths in C starting at the edges from δ(C). In particular, each such edge must belong to precisely one ray of R.A vertex v ∈ V (G) edge-dominates a ray R of a graph G if it cannot be separated from any tail of R by a finite set of edges. Naturally, ω := [ R]E is hence called an edge-dominated (or simply dominated ) edge-end of G, while v is referred to as one of its edge-dominating vertices .

# A Counterexample regarding the strongly-linked property 

Within finite graph theory, the existence of linked tree-cut decompositions attaining optimal width is the core of the mentioned work due to Giannopoulou, Kwon, Raymond and Thilikos in [7]. However, again reflecting the treatment due to Thomas in [14] for finite tree-width, the corresponding index trees in their discussions are not required to be rooted and, hence, suggest an even more general statement to the linkedness condition. More precisely, we say that a tree-cut decomposition (T, V) for a graph G is strongly-linked if, for every pair of edges 

e, e ′ ∈ E(T ), there are min {| Ff | : f ∈ E(eT e ′)} edge-disjoint paths in G connecting the edges from the adhesion set Fe to those of Fe′ , where eT e ′ denotes the unique sizewise minimum path in T containing e and e′. Just as the paper [2] by Albrechtsen, Jacobs, Knappe and Pitz inspires Theorem 1.1, their counterexamples in [1] with regard to strongly-linked tree-decompositions suggest that our main result also cannot be stated while considering this “unrooted version” of linkedness. Indeed, the aim of this appendix is to construct a locally finite graph as in the following result: 19 Theorem A.1. There is a locally-finite graph G such that no tree-cut decomposition displaying all its ends is strongly-linked.
