# Assessment of the quantitative impact of occlusal positioning splints on temporomandibular joint conditions 

## Agnieszka Anna Tomaka, Krzysztof Domino, Dariusz Pojda, and Michał Tarnawski Institute of Theoretical and Applied Informatics, Polish Academy of Sciences, Bałtycka 5, 44-100 Gliwice, Poland 

Abstract 

A computational method for quantitative analysis of temporomandibu-lar joint (TMJ) configuration using occlusal positioning splints is proposed and demonstrated. The method models a positioning splint as a physi-cal realization of a predefined rigid transformation of the mandible, de-rived from multimodal data, including CBCT, facial motion acquisition, and dental scans integrated within a common coordinate system. Splints corresponding to selected mandibular positions are designed and fabri-cated, and their positioning accuracy is evaluated using repeated scans of plaster models. Discrepancies are represented as error transformations and analyzed statistically in the space of rigid motions. The estimated transformations are propagated to segmented TMJ structures, enabling simulation-based evaluation of joint space changes. Transformation-based error analysis and surface distance metrics are used to quantify differences between planned and achieved configurations. The method enables indi-rect assessment of TMJ configuration using a single anatomical model and transformation data, reducing the need for repeated imaging across mul-tiple mandibular positions. This study is intended as a methodological demonstration, supported by a clear step-by-step graphical presentation, and does not aim to provide clinical validation. 

Keywords: occlusal positioning splints; accuracy of occlusal posi-tioning splint; temporomandibular joint assessment; transformation-based splint positioning; statistical error assessment 

# 1 Introduction 

In orthodontics, temporomandibular joint disorders (TJD) and occlusal changes— whether arising from malocclusions or as a consequence of treatment with vari-ous orthodontic appliances—have traditionally been regarded as separate issues. 1

> arXiv:2604.25322v1 [cs.CV] 28 Apr 2026

However, direct visualization of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) remains limited due to imaging constraints. Conventional radiographic methods and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) are invasive and unsuitable for dy-namic assessment. While motion capture systems provide displacement data, they do not reveal internal joint structures, and MRI remains limited in captur-ing functional spatial changes. Consequently, quantitative evaluation of TMJ function is still not routinely performed. At the same time, the use of various orthodontic appliances is not without influence on the TMJ. While contemporary software tools are capable of predict-ing post-treatment occlusion following the use of aligners, reliable prediction of changes in the position of the mandibular condyle within the temporomandibu-lar joint remains challenging. Assessment of the quantitative impact of occlusal positioning splints on tem-poromandibular joint conditions is therefore an important task in supporting appliance design. It enables prediction and evaluation of changes in joint config-uration after splint insertion, contributing to improved diagnostic interpretation, treatment planning, and objective documentation of the design process. In the approach considered in this work, the occlusal surface is incorporated into the splint based on patient-specific data, rather than being adjusted man-ually during clinical procedures. This distinguishes the proposed methodology from conventional approaches, in which the occlusion is refined directly on the patient. Instead, the occlusal relationship is transferred and repositioned within a computational framework, allowing the splint to be interpreted as a physical realization of a predefined transformation of the mandible. The aim of this study is not to perform a diagnostic evaluation of actual temporomandibular joint changes induced by occlusal positioning splints, but rather to develop and demonstrate a method for their quantitative assessment. In particular, we propose an approach in which mandibular transformations derived from multimodal data are used both to design positioning splints and to simulate their effect on joint structures, enabling indirect evaluation of TMJ configuration without the need for repeated imaging in multiple mandibular positions. This study is intended as a proof-of-concept demonstration of the proposed method and does not aim to provide clinical validation or diagnostic conclusions. 

# 2 Related Work 

The assessment of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) conditions and mandibular positioning has been studied from multiple perspectives, including landmark-based measurements, volumetric analysis, and motion-integrated approaches. In parallel, occlusal positioning splints have been investigated as clinical tools influencing mandibular configuration. This section reviews existing methods for TMJ assessment, approaches that integrate motion and imaging data, and techniques for designing positioning 2splints, with particular emphasis on their limitations in relation to transformation-based analysis and quantitative evaluation. 

## 2.1 Methods for measuring mandibular position relative to the glenoid fossae 

Assessment of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) conditions, understood as the measurement of the relationship between the glenoid fossa and the mandibular condyle, can be performed using various methods depending on the available imaging data. In radiographic assessments, TMJ evaluation is performed based on the vis-ible contours of the condyle and the glenoid fossa [1, 2]. The principle of these methods is to define reference points on the mandibular condyle and determine their minimal distances to the articular fossa. Subsequently, these methods were adapted for the analysis of CBCT images on single sagittal sections and further extended to analyses in the coronal and axial planes [3, 4]. Furthermore, the introduction of condylar analysis in relation to the mid-sagittal plane made it possible to conduct numerous studies on symmetry [5, 6, 7]. Based on CBCT, detailed morphometric analyses of the temporomandibular joint have been conducted, including the definition of landmarks, measurement of distances and inclinations of the individual walls of the articular fossa and the condyle, along with appropriate population statistics [8]. Comparable stud-ies have been conducted on the Chinese population [9], including evaluation of condylar volume for normal occlusion, referring to [10], which indicates that reduced volume is an indicator of abnormalities associated with the articular disc. Using the described measurement methods and repeated acquisitions, a se-ries of studies have been conducted to evaluate changes in subsequent examina-tions. Beyond asymmetry analysis, studies have also examined changes resulting from surgical procedures intended to compensate for asymmetry [11], changes in TMJ conditions resulting from different mandibular positioning [12, 13], changes resulting from occlusal splint placement [2, 14], and finally changes after splint therapy to evaluate therapeutic outcomes [15, 16, 17]. Some approaches evalu-ate TMJ changes during mandibular motion recorded with Zebris [18] or other motion acquisition devices [19], integrating these recordings with static mor-phometric volume images to simulate motion between the stable and moving components [20]. While the use of landmarks facilitates the definition of measurement pa-rameters, the complex anatomy of the mandibular condyle and the articular fossa makes it challenging to define landmarks that correspond either between patients or across different mandibular positions. Additionally, landmark po-sitions may vary depending on slice selection and anatomical interpretation, leading to inconsistencies in reference definitions. Due to these limitations, and because CBCT provides volumetric imaging, volumetric analysis methods have been developed [21, 22], in which glenoid fossa volume and condylar volume are analyzed. Dynamic volumetric analysis 3involves creating distance maps for each mandibular position and tracking their changes during motion [23]. An alternative approach describes TMJ changes in terms of transforma-tion analysis. A rigid-body transformation is determined, both from the initial configuration and as an instantaneous change, represented using a screw rep-resentation. This framework includes the finite helical axis (FHA), describing motion between two configurations, and the instantaneous helical axis (IHA), describing motion at a given instant. [24] proposed averaging these axes to de-fine a mean helical axis (MHA). The axis of this transformation is analyzed to determine the direction about which mandibular rotation occurs. Recent stud-ies focus on TMJ diagnostics based on motion analysis [25], incorporating both joint space analysis and transformation-based approaches for the classification of temporomandibular disorders (TMD). 

## 2.2 Positioning occlusal splints derived from intraoral scan-ners 

In our previous studies [26, 27], positioning splints were fabricated based on ha-bitual occlusion and the displacement of the mandible to a therapeutic position, as digitally recorded by an intraoral scanner. This approach ensured that the data needed to construct the splint—specifically, the form of the dental arches and their habitual and therapeutic relationships—were obtained using the same type of imaging. Superimposing the maxillary and mandibular models from both acquisitions in a common coordinate system enabled determination of the transformation of the mandible from the habitual occlusal position to the therapeutic position. Following 3D printing of the resulting splints, intraoral scans of the dental arches were carried out with the positioning splint attached. Modern intraoral scanners generally provide high accuracy, often with true-ness and precision values in the range of a few tens of micrometers. Given this accuracy, positioning precision of the splint on the order of 0.2 mm was achieved. A different situation occurs when dental arch morphology and mandibular mo-tion data are acquired using separate imaging techniques. 

## 2.3 Research gaps 

This study does not aim to provide a diagnostic evaluation of actual temporo-mandibular joint changes, but rather focuses on developing and demonstrating a method for their quantitative assessment based on archival data and model-based analysis. Accordingly, the study is based on archival data; therefore, plaster models are used, as they can be rescanned without the need for patient involvement and ensure stability of occlusal conditions over time, in contrast to intraoral scans, which are subject to variability. In this research direction, the following research gaps can be identified: 

• There is a lack of systematic research addressing the measurement of the 4effect of occlusal splints on the position of the mandibular condyle within the temporomandibular joint. The literature contains only a limited num-ber of studies, mostly isolated reports. 

• There is currently limited research on standardized methods for the three-dimensional analysis of temporomandibular joint conditions. The poten-tial of three-dimensional visualization of joint structures remains underuti-lized and is difficult to translate into meaningful assessment parameters. 

• An approach is being developed that treats the splint as a physical real-ization of a predefined transformation. However, there are no studies that allow evaluation of occlusal positioning splints in relation to their design or comparison of the achieved changes, both in occlusal areas and in the TMJ, with those originally intended. 

• The statistical assessment of measurement errors remains limited, and a systematic approach to this issue is lacking. This applies both to errors measured as distance deviations and to errors defined in terms of trans-formation matrices. 

• There is a lack of discussion of the factors that may influence the accuracy of occlusal splints and, therefore, the differences between intended and achieved TMJ and occlusal relationships. We aim to address these research gaps through a systematic study of tem-poromandibular joint conditions based on positioning splints, scanning proce-dures, and plaster models. In detail, the study presents a method for designing occlusal positioning splints as a function of predefined mandibular transformations derived from selected jaw positions recorded during movement. For each of these positions, a transformation matrix is determined to map the mandible from the reference position (maximum intercuspation) to the target position. The occlusal splint is then defined as a function of this transformation and fabricated using 3D printing. Repeated scans of plaster models with the splints in place are subsequently used to evaluate the transformations introduced by each splint. This is achieved through statistical analysis of the error transformation, defined as the difference between the planned and measured mandibular positions. Both the target transformation and the estimated error transformations are used in simulations enabling qualitative and quantitative assessment of changes in the position of the condyle within the temporomandibular joint. These sim-ulations allow the use of various methods for assessing condylar positions and their changes within the joint, within a unified computational framework in-tended as a methodological demonstration rather than a clinical study. We conclude that, despite the variety of existing approaches, a consistent framework linking mandibular transformations, occlusal splint design, and quan-titative assessment of TMJ configuration is still lacking. In particular, the 5relationship between intended mandibular positioning and the configuration ac-tually achieved by positioning splints remains insufficiently explored. To address these limitations, the following section presents the proposed method, which combines transformation-based splint design, statistical analy-sis of positioning error, and simulation-based evaluation of temporomandibular joint configuration within a unified computational approach. 

# 3 Method 

To investigate how positioning occlusal splints and their accuracy influence changes in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) region, we propose a unified computational approach that combines multimodal data integration, transformation-based splint design, and statistical analysis of rigid transformations. The method builds upon previously developed approaches to mandibular mo-tion acquisition using a mandibular bow and a facial scanner [28], multimodal image segmentation and registration [29], and splint design based on transfor-mation models [26]. These components are extended using a transformation tree framework [30, 31] and complemented by statistical analysis methods defined on the Lie group SE (3) .The proposed method enables: (i) representation of mandibular motion as a sequence of rigid transformations; (ii) design of positioning splints as physical realizations of predefined transformations; (iii) estimation of transformation er-rors based on repeated measurements; and (iv) simulation-based evaluation of TMJ configuration changes. 

## 3.1 Multimodal data 

Technical developments make it possible to enhance the analysis of the stom-atognathic system by combining images acquired using different imaging modal-ities. Dental morphology can be represented using virtual dental models ob-tained from intraoral scans or laser scans of dental casts. Facial geometry can be captured using 2D and 3D surface imaging. Radiographic techniques provide information about internal anatomical structures, while cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) enables three-dimensional representation of skeletal and dental structures. The creation of a digital patient model involves image acquisition, segmenta-tion, three-dimensional reconstruction, and multimodal data integration. Seg-mentation is used to isolate anatomical structures of interest, allowing consistent representation of bones, teeth, and soft tissues, and enabling the extraction of specific regions such as the temporomandibular joint (glenoid fossa and condyle), dentition, and facial surfaces. ## 3.2 Transformation tree: integration of multimodal image data 

A key step in multimodal integration is image registration, which enables spa-tial alignment of images acquired from different modalities within a common coordinate system. The adopted approach follows the transformation tree framework introduced in [30, 31], in which rigid transformations relate data acquired in different coor-dinate systems. Registration is performed using a surface-based approach with the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm [32, 33]. Three coordinate systems are considered: CBCT ( C), 3dMD ( F ), and Kon-ica Minolta ( K). Each group contains original images as well as segmented regions used for registration. The corresponding datasets are illustrated in Fig-ure 1. Registration between modalities is performed using anatomically correspond-ing regions: dentition for CBCT–Konica Minolta alignment, facial surfaces for 7CBCT–3dMD alignment, and facial arches for linking the 3dMD and Konica Minolta coordinate systems.

## 3.3 Transformation-based splint design 

Positioning occlusal splints are constructed based on predefined mandibular transformations. The occlusal surface is transferred according to the transfor-mation mapping the mandible from a reference position to a target position. In this formulation, the splint can be interpreted as a physical realization of a transformation acting on a rigid body. This enables direct correspondence be-tween the designed mandibular position and the resulting occlusal configuration [27] (Figure 3). 

## 3.4 Measurement of the accuracy of occlusal positioning splints 

Rigid body transformations derived from motion analysis describe mandibular displacement, including both bone and teeth. Mandibular tooth positions ob-tained from motion capture were used as patient-specific therapeutic positions for occlusal splint design (Figure 4), ensuring physiologically attainable align-ment. The splints were fabricated using 3D printing. Accuracy was evaluated by scanning plaster models with the splints in situ (Figure 4B). The resulting scans (Figure 4C) were registered to a common coordinate system using the surface of the maxillary plaster model. Registration of the mandible in the therapeutic position to the acquired data represents a measure of the error introduced by the occlusal splint. Scanning was repeated for seven positioning splints, enabling statistical analysis. To prevent improper fitting of the mandibular teeth to the scanned occlusal splint surface (Figure 4D) from affecting the measurement statistics, the tolerance range was limited to 2 mm.

## 3.7 TMJ simulation model 

Assuming rigid-body motion, mandibular transformations are propagated to the temporomandibular joint structures segmented from CBCT data. This enables simulation of condylar positions corresponding to both planned and measured mandibular configurations. The resulting configurations are evaluated using distance-based metrics de-scribing the relationship between the condyle and the glenoid fossa. 

# 4 Experiments 

The purpose of the experiments is not to provide a clinically representative analysis of TMJ behavior, but to demonstrate the applicability of the proposed method to real multimodal data and to illustrate the types of measurements and variability that can be obtained. 11 The experiments were conducted on archival multimodal data from a single patient and include splint design, repeated measurement of realized positioning, and simulation-based assessment of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) configura-tion. The proposed method was evaluated using positioning occlusal splints de-signed for selected mandibular positions derived from the recorded motion se-quence. The analyzed positions included anterior incisor contact, maximum protrusion, maximum lateral rotation of the mandible to the right and left with maintained anterior tooth contact, maximum mouth opening, and maximum opening combined with simultaneous rotation to the right and left. For each selected position, all 3D imaging data were registered within a com-mon coordinate system. The corresponding mandibular transformation relative to the natural bite position (maximum intercuspation) was then determined, and a positioning splint realizing this transformation was designed and fabri-cated using 3D printing. Subsequently, plaster models with the splint in place were scanned using a Konica Minolta scanner mounted on a rotary table. Scanning was performed after repeated manual positioning of the maxillary plaster model with the ap-plied splint and the mandibular model. The acquired scans were registered to the upper plaster model, and the transformation of the mandibular model in the therapeutic position relative to the scans was estimated for each acquisition. Segmentation was performed semi-automatically based on expert knowledge. Registration relied on existing tools, including ICP-based procedures and Rapid-Form mechanisms, rather than on a newly implemented registration algorithm. The transformation tree representation enabled consistent integration of partial registrations into a unified framework. Eight positioning splints were analyzed. For each splint, four independent scanning cycles were performed, resulting in a total of 32 samples.

## 5.2 Statistical structure of transformation error 

The PCA analysis (Table 2) and the covariance ellipsoid (Figures 5 and 6) indicate strong anisotropy of the translational error. The first principal compo-nent explains approximately 68% of the variance, showing that the variability is concentrated along a single dominant direction.

This result is visualized in Figure 6 as projections onto the PCA planes. In the space of rotation vectors, the distribution is more compact and homoge-neous, indicating that rotational variability remains limited. The histograms show that the translational error is right-skewed, with a small number of larger deviations, while the rotational error remains concen-trated around small angles. These observations indicate that most samples exhibit relatively small devi-ations, with occasional larger translational errors. 14 5.3 Distance-based analysis of measured configurations 

An additional analysis was performed based on distances between the surfaces in the planned and measured configurations. For each sample, the mean signed distance and the standard deviation of the distance distribution were computed. Global statistics were estimated using weighted aggregation. For the analyzed dataset, the following values were obtained: 

μ ≈ 0.091 mm , σ ≈ 0.571 mm The mean distance remains close to zero, indicating the absence of a signifi-cant global offset, while the standard deviation reflects local geometric discrep-ancies. The histogram of mean distances (Figure 7) shows a right-skewed distri-bution, with most values below zero and a small number of larger positive deviations. The grouped error-bar plot highlights differences between splints. The mean values indicate a small systematic bias, while the standard deviation varies between approximately 0.2 mm and 0.5 mm, indicating differences in local surface agreement. Notably, a low mean error does not necessarily correspond to low variability, indicating that global positional offset and local surface agreement should be treated as distinct components of error. 

## 5.4 Simulation-based TMJ analysis 

Using the estimated transformations, the positions of the mandibular condyles within the temporomandibular joints were simulated based on CBCT-derived anatomical models. Distances between the condylar head and the articular surface were evaluated for the planned and measured configurations. The resulting distance maps are shown in Figure 8. The method used to visualize maps of the shortest distances between points on the glenoid fossa and the condyles is a conceptual extension of that presented in [23]. As shown in [19], changes in distance may be more informative than absolute values. While the authors illustrated this for a single point, in the present study the distance changes are mapped over the entire glenoid fossa. The visualization was limited to the distance range from 0 to 10 mm to focus on clinically relevant regions. The difference between planned and measured configurations was used to quantify the error of condylar position reproduction. The grouped error-bar plot (Figure 9) summarizes these errors across splints. The results indicate the presence of some (but limited) systematic bias, with differences observed between the left and right joints, as well as variability in local surface agreement. These findings demonstrate that discrepancies between intended and real-ized mandibular transformations propagate to measurable differences in TMJ configuration. 15 Figure 6: Statistical characterization of transformation error. ## 5.5 Qualitative interpretation of TMJ changes 

Figure 8 presents distance maps for the prescribed transformations, the mea-sured transformations, and their differences. Regions of reduced joint space are observed as shifts in the distance maps, indicating displacement of the condyle relative to the glenoid fossa. The differ-ence maps highlight areas where joint space decreases when comparing measured configurations with planned ones. These changes are spatially non-uniform, indicating that transformation discrepancies propagate differently across joint regions. The analyzed splints correspond to different types of mandibular motion, including protrusion, lateral rotation, and mouth opening. Differences between these configurations are reflected in the spatial patterns of the distance maps. The solid hor-izontal line indicates the ideal zero-error reference. Measurements 3t, 4t, and 5t were acquired in the natural orientation, whereas 6t corresponds to the inverted (“upside-down”) configuration. 19 6 Discussion 

The presented study evaluates the accuracy of occlusal splints designed for se-lected stages of mandibular motion and, through simulation, enables assessment of the resulting configuration within the temporomandibular joint (TMJ). The ability to predict the position of the mandibular condyles within the joint after splint placement may be of particular importance in the design pro-cess. It provides a basis for documentation and comparison of planned and achieved configurations and, in future, may serve as an input parameter in splint design, allowing joint conditions to be explicitly incorporated into the planning process. 

Scope and generalizability of the results The presented study is based on a single archival patient case and a limited number of repeated measurements. Therefore, the statistical analyses should be interpreted as descriptive of the studied acquisition and processing pipeline rather than as population-level esti-mates. In particular, the PCA-based analysis and covariance representations are used here to characterize dominant directions and the structure of variability, rather than to infer generalizable statistical properties. The proposed method, encompassing multimodal data integration, transformation-based splint design, measurement, and simulation, is subject to several limi-tations. First, the accuracy of the estimated TMJ configuration depends on the precision of determining the spatial relationship between the maxillary and mandibular dentition. Any error in the registration of the dental arches propa-gates directly to the estimated condylar position. Second, the approach assumes rigid-body motion of the mandible, neglecting potential deformation of biological tissues. While this assumption is commonly adopted in similar studies, it may introduce discrepancies between the simulated and actual joint conditions. The conducted analyses indicate that the stability of occlusion plays a critical role in the transformation-based design of splints. Even slight sliding of the mandible on the splint surface may lead to measurable displacement of the condyles within the joint. 

Systematic offset in mandibular positioning A consistent translational offset was observed in the analyzed transformations, predominantly along a sin-gle spatial direction. Within the present study, this effect cannot be attributed to a single factor. Possible sources include residual bias in multimodal registra-tion, systematic characteristics of splint geometry or positioning, inaccuracies in defining the reference occlusal position, and limitations of the measurement setup based on plaster models and rotary-table scanning. The fact that this tendency is observed across multiple splints suggests the presence of a system-atic component in the acquisition–design–measurement pipeline rather than a purely random one (the measurement of accuracy requires a geometrical model of the splint, which, to our knowledge, has been examined only by the authors). 20 The accuracy obtained for splints designed using plaster models is lower than that typically reported for splints based on intraoral scans. This is partly due to the reduced accuracy of plaster model scanning, but also to limitations in determining the reference occlusal position and in capturing the full set of patient-specific data during motion acquisition. In our previous work based on intraoral scans, positioning accuracy on the order of 0.2 mm was achieved [26, 27], which is substantially lower than the deviations observed in the present study. This difference reflects the cumulative effect of additional processing steps and data transformations in the current workflow. The measurement procedure itself also contributes to the observed variabil-ity. In the current approach, the error is estimated as the distance between scans of plaster models in the therapeutic position and measurement scans obtained with the splint in place. The procedure involves aligning the maxillary model and evaluating discrepancies in the mandibular region. A more precise approach would involve segmentation of the measurement scans into separate components corresponding to the maxilla, mandible, and splint, allowing independent registration and error estimation. Such a procedure could be implemented using automated or semi-automated methods, although it would require additional assumptions regarding the selection of correspondence regions. In the present study, the distance-based analysis was restricted to a prede-fined tolerance range to avoid incorrect correspondences between mandibular teeth and splint surfaces. As such correspondences were observed for deviations exceeding approximately 3 mm, the range used for error calculation was limited to 2 mm. While this approach improves robustness, it introduces an additional heuristic parameter that may influence the results. 

Influence of reference configuration The proposed method relies on the definition of a reference occlusal position (maximum intercuspation). If this position is not stable or is subject to variability, part of the observed transfor-mation error may reflect instability of the reference configuration itself rather than inaccuracies in splint realization. This factor should be taken into account when interpreting the results. 

Experimental setup and repeatability The number of repeated scans per splint was limited to four. These repetitions were intended to characterize mea-surement repeatability within the experimental setup rather than to provide statistically robust estimates of variability. While a larger number of repe-titions would improve statistical reliability, the current design is sufficient to distinguish between systematic and random components of the observed error. Finally, the experimental setup itself may affect the measured accuracy. In particular, larger splints were more difficult to position stably on the rotary table during scanning, which may contribute to increased variability in some cases. 21 It should be emphasized that the presented results are not intended to char-acterize patient-specific TMJ behavior, but rather to demonstrate how the pro-posed framework can be used to quantify and interpret such changes. 

# 7 Conclusions 

Within the analyzed experimental setup, the results indicate that mandibular positioning accuracy using the investigated splints exhibits a two-component structure, consisting of a systematic offset and anisotropic variability. The observed effects should be interpreted within the context of the ana-lyzed acquisition—design—measurement pipeline and not as generalizable clin-ical characteristics. A consistent deviation from the planned position is observed within the an-alyzed dataset, predominantly along a single spatial direction. This systematic component may be related to splint design, positioning conditions, or limitations of the transformation-based planning process. In addition, the variability of the results is anisotropic, with significantly larger dispersion in the translational component than in the rotational one. This indicates that mandibular orientation is reproduced relatively consistently, whereas spatial positioning remains the primary source of uncertainty. The distance-based analysis further demonstrates that global positional ac-curacy does not necessarily correspond to local surface agreement. Even when the mean error is small, noticeable local discrepancies may occur, indicating that global offset and local geometric consistency should be considered as dis-tinct aspects of the error. The proposed method enables not only the analysis of condylar distance within the joint, but also the evaluation of positioning error propagated through the entire splint design and measurement process. This provides a more com-prehensive assessment of splint performance than approaches based solely on geometric or landmark-based metrics. The results indicate that the main limitations of the investigated splints are related to translational inaccuracies and local surface discrepancies. Fu-ture work should investigate whether these effects are associated with specific splint designs, material properties, measurement conditions, or limitations of the acquisition and registration procedures. Additionally, anatomical factors, such as occlusal instability in individual patients, may further influence the observed accuracy and should be considered in future studies.
