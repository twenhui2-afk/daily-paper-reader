# On estimating operator norm distance, with optimal trace distance estimation when one state is pure

Abstract 

We investigate the computational complexity of estimating the operator norm distance 

T∞(ρ0, ρ 1), defined via the operator norm ∥A∥∞ := σmax (A), where σmax (A) is the largest singular value of A, given poly( n)-size state-preparation circuits of n-qubit quantum states 

ρ0 and ρ1. We provide efficient quantum estimators for the operator norm distance whose complexity is independent of the rank (and thus the dimension) of the states: (1) When one state is pure, we establish an optimal quantum estimator using Θ(1 /ϵ )

queries to the state-preparation circuits. Consequently, for constant additive error, say ϵ = 1 /5, our estimator runs in poly( n) time. Since the operator norm distance 

T∞(|ψ⟩⟨ ψ|, ρ ) is exactly half of the trace distance T( |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|, ρ ), our result gives a rank-independent query complexity for estimating T∞(|ψ⟩⟨ ψ|, ρ ) and T( |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|, ρ ), whereas the approaches due to van Apeldoorn, Cornelissen, Gilyén, and Nannicini (SODA 2023) and Wang and Zhang (TIT 2024) have query complexity scaling at least linearly with 

rank( ρ), which can be exp( n) in general. In addition, our query complexity matches the optimal bound when both states are pure by Wang (TIT 2024). (2) For general quantum states, we also provide a quantum estimator using ˜O(1 /ϵ 3/2)

queries to the state-preparation circuits, which shows that the corresponding promise problem is BQP -complete and improves the QMA upper bound sketched by Liu and Wang (ESA 2025). Together with an Ω(1 /ϵ ) quantum query complexity lower bound, this leaves only square-root room for improvement. The key intuition behind our estimators is that, when one state is pure, the pure state |ψ⟩

has overlap at least 1/2 with the top unit eigenvector of |ψ⟩⟨ ψ| − ρ, reflecting a structural feature specific to the operator norm distance.

Testing the closeness between quantum states is a central topic in quantum property test-ing [MdW16], which studies efficient quantum algorithms for testing properties of quantum objects and extends classical distribution testing (see e.g., [Can20]) to the non-commutative setting. This problem is also closely connected to the verification of quantum devices, such as 

Q0 and Q1, which prepare the corresponding quantum states ρ0 and ρ1. In (tolerant) quantum state testing, one seeks to design efficient quantum algorithms that determine whether ρ0 is 

> 2

/5-far from or 1/5-close to ρ1 with respect to a chosen closeness measure, typically the trace distance, which is induced by the ℓ1 norm. Importantly, the trace distance is one of the most widely used measures of closeness because it plays a central role in several research areas: quantum hypothesis testing and state dis-crimination [Hel67, Hol73], information-theoretically secure quantum cryptography [BOHL +05, RK05, PR22], classical and statistical zero-knowledge [SV03, Wat02, Wat09], and certification and benchmarking [GLN05, BOW19, EHW +20]. Beyond the ℓ1 norm distance, namely the total variation distance and the trace distance, (tolerant) closeness testing between probability distributions or quantum states with respect to the ℓα norm distance, for instance, 

Tα(ρ0, ρ 1) := 12 ∥ρ0 − ρ1∥α, where ∥A∥α := tr( |A|α)1/α ,

has also been studied in [Wag15, LW25]. Interestingly, for all real α ≥ 1 for which Tα(ρ0, ρ 1) is a distance metric, a dichotomy emerges: 

• In the ℓ1 norm case, the sample complexity, namely the number of samples drawn from the distributions, scales polynomially with the support size of those distributions [VV17, CDVV14, JHW18]. A similar phenomenon holds for quantum states: the number of state copies required scales polynomially with the dimension or rank of the states [BOW19]. 

• For all real α > 1, the sample complexity becomes independent of the support size [Wag15]. Analogously, rank-independent query and sample complexity upper bounds for estimating the quantum ℓα distance, defined via the Schatten norm, were also obtained in [LW25]. However, the case α = ∞ remains mysterious. On the classical side, the support-size-independent (and thus dimension-independent) sample complexity upper bounds in [Wag15] already include this endpoint case. On the quantum side, however, the operator-norm-distance case is still listed as an open problem in [LW25]: What are the query and sample complexity upper bounds for estimating the oper-ator norm distance T∞(ρ0, ρ 1)? Can one obtain such bounds in a rank-independent 

manner, similar to the classical setting, or is rank dependence unavoidable ?Why is it important to study the operator norm distance? While the trace distance ( α = 1 )serves as the most important case in the quantum ℓα distance hierarchy, defined via the Schatten norm, the operator norm distance ( α = ∞), namely 

T∞(ρ0, ρ 1) := 12 ∥ρ0 − ρ1∥∞,

is the most relaxed notion of closeness precisely because it is the endpoint of the hierarchy, and thus may be viewed as another particularly important case. The operator norm distance captures the largest spectral deviation between two states, and therefore arises as a closeness measure in quantum state tomography [CKW +16, XK16, vACGN23], as well as an intermedi-ate analytic tool that can sometimes be converted into trace-distance guarantees in [GKKT20]. This spectral viewpoint is also closely connected to compressed-sensing and low-rank recovery approaches, which allow high-dimensional states to be reconstructed from far fewer measure-ments [Liu11, Gro11]. Beyond measuring the closeness between states, related operator-norm 1notions also play an important role in quantum process and unitary tomography [HKOT23] and in quantifying the precision of Hamiltonian simulation [BCK15, HHKL23]. In this work, we provide an affirmative answer to these questions on estimating T∞(ρ0, ρ 1).We next present our main results and discuss their significance. 

1.1 Main results 

We start by stating our first main result, where T( ρ0, ρ 1) := T α=1 (ρ0, ρ 1) = 12 tr |ρ0 − ρ1|:

Theorem 1.1 (Optimal quantum estimator for the trace and operator norm distances when one state is pure, informal) . Given quantum query access to the state-preparation circuits of two n-qubit quantum states, denoted by ρ and |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|, without prior knowledge of which input circuit prepares the pure state, the quantum query complexity for estimating T( ρ, |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|) or 

T∞(ρ, |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|) to within additive error ϵ is Θ(1 /ϵ ).

Here, the lower bounds in Theorem 1.1 follow directly from the corresponding quantum query lower bounds for estimating T( |ψ0⟩⟨ ψ0|, |ψ1⟩⟨ ψ1|) and T∞(|ψ0⟩⟨ ψ0|, |ψ1⟩⟨ ψ1|) to within additive error ϵ in the case where both states are pure, established in [Wan24, Theorem V.2] and [LW25, Theorem 22(1)], respectively. 

Theorem 1.2 (Quantum estimator for the operator norm distance, informal) . Given quantum query access to the state-preparation circuits of n-qubit quantum states ρ0 and ρ1, there exists an explicit quantum algorithm that estimates T∞(ρ0, ρ 1) to within additive error ϵ with query complexity ˜O(1 /ϵ 3/2), where ˜O(·) suppresses poly-logarithmic factors.

1.2 Proof techniques 

We begin by explaining the intuition behind our quantum estimators for the operator norm distance. One natural approach is based on phase estimation and proceeds as follows: (1) Apply an appropriate version of phase estimation, 2 constructed based on a unitary dilation of ∆ := ( ρ0 − ρ1)/2,3 to a state supported on the top eigenspace of ∆, which corresponds to the largest eigenvalue λmax (∆) .(2) Repeat the same construction for −∆, and then take the maximum of the two resulting estimates, thereby obtaining an estimate of σmax (∆) = max {λmax (∆) , λ max (−∆) }.

Consequently, any eigenvector corresponding to a positive eigenvalue must have substantial overlap with |ψ⟩, and in particular ∆ has exactly one positive eigenvalue when ∆̸ = 0 . From naïve phase estimation to maximum phase estimation. To resolve the latter issue, one might first consider the naïve approach of combining Kitaev’s standard phase esti-mation [Kit95] with Hamiltonian simulation, such as that in [BCK15]: namely, one simulates 4eit ∆ and recovers an extremal eigenvalue of ∆ from the corresponding eigenphase. While this approach is conceptually natural, it is not well suited to our setting. Indeed, estimating an eigenvalue to within additive error ϵ in this way requires resolving phases to precision O(ϵ), and thus using simulation time t = Θ(1 /ϵ ). Implementing such a long-time evolution then incurs an additional factor proportional to t, yielding only a ˜O(1 /ϵ 2)-type quantum query upper bound, which is quadratically worse than the optimal bound in Theorem 1.1. The right choice, instead, is to work with a unitary dilation of ∆ and then apply maximum phase estimation [MdW26] (see also Lemma 3.3) after qubitization [LC19] (see Lemma 3.2 for the specific version we need). Under the resulting monotone correspondence between eigenvalues and eigenphases, the largest eigenvalue of ∆ is converted into the largest eigenphase of the associated unitary. Hence, the problem becomes that of estimating a maximum phase from a state having nontrivial overlap with the top eigenspace. Consequently, in the single-pure-state setting, the warm-start lemma (Lemma 3.1) provides exactly such a state with constant overlap, and this is precisely what enables the maximum-phase-estimation primitive to recover λmax (∆) , and hence both T∞(ρ, |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|) and T( ρ, |ψ⟩⟨ ψ|),within additive error ϵ using O(1 /ϵ ) queries, as stated in Theorem 1.1.

1.3 Discussion and open problems 

A natural question is whether our warm-start lemma (see Lemma 3.1) can be extended to a more general setting. Here, we provide a simple example showing that such a warm start already 5fails for a pair of rank-two states, thereby revealing an immediate obstruction: 

ρ(η)0 := (1 − η)|0⟩⟨ 0| + η|1⟩⟨ 1| and ρ(η)1 := (1 − η)|0⟩⟨ 0| + η|2⟩⟨ 2|.

A direct calculation shows that T∞

> (

ρ(η)0 , ρ (η)1 

> )

= η/ 2 and T(ρ(η)0 , ρ (η)1 

> )

= η. Moreover, the extremal positive and negative eigenvalues of ρ(η)0 − ρ(η)1 are η and −η, respectively, with the corresponding eigenvectors |1⟩ and |2⟩. Consequently, the overlaps between these eigenvectors and the input states are ⟨1|ρ(η)0 |1⟩ = η and ⟨2|ρ(η)1 |2⟩ = η. Since η can be chosen arbitrarily small, there is no constant c > 0 such that every pair of rank-two states admits an extremal eigenvector of ρ0 − ρ1 with overlap at least c with one of the input states, or even with a linear combination of them. This motivates our first open problem: (i) Can one characterize the largest family of mixed states for which one can still prepare a state that has constant overlap with the top eigenspace of ρ0 − ρ1?A second direction concerns optimality. Our current bounds in Theorem 1.2 for the general setting still leave a square-root gap, leading to the following question: (ii) Can the quantum query and sample complexity of estimating the operator norm distance be made optimal? More generally, can analogous optimal results be achieved for estimating the quantum ℓα distance for real α > 1 [LW25]? 

1.4 Related works 

Quantum state tomography has been studied with respect to trace distance, fidelity (Bures distance), Frobenius distance (Hilbert–Schmidt distance), and operator norm distance in the literature [HHJ +17, OW16, CHL +23, vACGN23, PSW26, SSW25, PSTW25]. The closeness testing of quantum states, also known as the quantum state certification, was studied with respect to trace distance, fidelity, ℓ2 distance, and ℓ3 distance in [BOW19, GL20]. The tolerant closeness testing, which is equivalent to closeness estimation, was studied for trace distance, fidelity, and ℓα distance in [CCC19, WZC +23, GP22, WGL +24, WZ24, LLW26].

2.1 Closeness measures for quantum states 

We begin by defining the trace distance: 

Definition 2.1 (Trace distance) . Let ρ0 and ρ1 be two quantum states on the same Hilbert space. The trace distance between ρ0 and ρ1 is defined by 

T( ρ0, ρ 1) := 12 ∥ρ0 − ρ1∥1 = 12 tr( |ρ0 − ρ1|).

Next, we define the operator norm distance, a quantity that has also been used in [GKKT20, vACGN23], where the corresponding definition is only implicit, while an explicit formal defini-tion is given as a special case of [LW25, Definition 2.3]: 6Definition 2.2 (Operator norm distance) . Let ρ0 and ρ1 be two quantum states on the same Hilbert space. The operator norm distance between ρ0 and ρ1 is defined by 

T∞(ρ0, ρ 1) := 12 ∥ρ0 − ρ1∥∞ = 12 max {λmax (ρ0 − ρ1), λ max (ρ1 − ρ0)}.

Here, λmax (A) denotes the largest eigenvalue of the matrix A.

Notably, the operator norm distance arises as the endpoint case ( α = ∞) of the quantum 

ℓα distance [LW25], which generalizes both the trace distance ( α = 1 ) and the Hilbert-Schmidt distance ( α = 2 ) via the Schatten norm. In addition, both the trace distance and the operator norm distance are distance metrics, and hence satisfy the triangle inequality, as stated in [LW25, Lemma 2.4] (see also [AS17, Proposition 1.16]). Moreover, the inequalities that relate the operator norm distance to the trace distance were established in [LW25], as stated in Lemma 2.3. Interestingly, when one of the states is pure, the trace distance is exactly twice the operator norm distance. 

Lemma 2.3 (T∞ vs. T, adapted from [LW25, Theorem 4.2(2)]) . Let ρ0 and ρ1 be quantum states on the same Hilbert space. Then, the following bounds hold: 

2 · T∞(ρ0, ρ 1) ≤ T( ρ0, ρ 1) ≤ 2 min {rank( ρ0), rank( ρ1)} · T∞(ρ0, ρ 1).

Furthermore, when ρ0 = |ψ⟩⟨ ψ| and ρ1 = ρ, the inequality becomes an identity:

Definition 2.4 (Quantum State Distinguishability Problem with Schatten α-norm, QSD α,adapted from [LW25, Definition 17]) . # 4 Efficient quantum algorithms for estimating operator norm distance 

We now turn to the general setting, in which neither input state is assumed to be pure. The same overall strategy still applies, but the constant-overlap warm start from Section 3 is no longer available and therefore must be replaced by a weaker overlap guarantee. In particular, by combining the overlap lemma in Section 4.1 with the qubitization framework from Section 3.2, we obtain general quantum query and sample algorithms for estimating the operator norm distance, which are presented respectively in Sections 4.2 and 4.3.
