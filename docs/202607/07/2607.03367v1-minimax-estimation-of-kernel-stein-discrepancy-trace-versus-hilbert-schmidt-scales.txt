## ABSTRACT 

Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD) compares a sample to a fixed target distribution known only through its score, and is widely used for goodness-of-fit testing, sample quality assessment, and approximate inference. We study the estimation of KSD( P0, P ) from n independent observations and identify the sharp spectral constant governing the minimax risk: it is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the Stein covariance operator C⋆, giving the minimax scale p∥C⋆∥HS /n . This scale is attained by the positive-part square-root U-statistic, whereas the standard plug-in V-statistic remains at the trace scale ptr( C⋆)/n and is therefore suboptimal by the fourth root of the effective rank of C⋆; for a Gaussian target with a fixed-bandwidth Gaussian kernel this factor is exponential in the dimension. 

## 1 Introduction 

Comparing a sample to a target distribution is a basic task in statistics and machine learning. In many applications the target density is known only up to a normalizing constant. This happens, for example, for Bayesian posteriors, energy-based models, and distributions produced by approximate MCMC methods. The kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) is useful in this setting because it depends on the target P0 through the score ∇ log p0, and not through the normalizing constant. Stein’s method was introduced by Stein [1] and then developed as a general method for distributional approximation; see, for example, Stein [2] and Chen et al. [3] . Its use for sample quality assessment is more recent. Gorham and Mackey [4] proposed a computable Stein discrepancy for comparing exact, biased, and deterministic sample sequences. The kernel version was introduced soon after by Liu et al. [5] and Chwialkowski et al. [6] , who combined Stein identities with reproducing kernels and used the resulting KSD for goodness-of-fit testing. Gorham and Mackey [7] studied when KSD controls convergence to the target and showed that the choice of kernel is important for this property. For a recent overview of Stein’s method in probabilistic inference and learning, see Liu et al. [8]. Several subsequent works developed KSD and related Stein discrepancies in different directions. On the algorithmic side, Liu and Wang [9] used Stein identities in Stein variational gradient descent, and recent work has established finite-particle convergence rates in KSD and Wasserstein metrics [ 10 ]. To reduce the quadratic cost of KSD, Huggins and Mackey [11] introduced random feature Stein discrepancies, and Kalinke et al. [12] developed a Nyström approximation. On the testing side, Jitkrittum et al. [13] proposed a linear-time kernel Stein test, and Kanagawa et al. [14] extended Stein testing to latent-variable models. Stein discrepancies have also been used as optimization objectives, for example in Stein points [ 15 ] and minimum Stein discrepancy estimation [ 16 ]. Since KSD is often used as a goodness-of-fit statistic [17 , 18 ], minimax testing theory is also relevant. In this direction, Hagrass et al. [17] study minimax goodness-of-fit testing with KSD at a fixed target P0. Their analysis shows that ordinary unregularized KSD tests can be minimax suboptimal for detecting alternatives separated in χ2(P, P 0), and that spectral regularization is needed to attain the optimal testing boundary. The closest comparison inside kernel methods is the minimax theory for kernel mean embeddings and the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). For a reproducing kernel k, the kernel mean embedding of P is μP = EX∼P k(X, ·),and MMD( P, Q ) = ∥μP − μQ∥H [ 19 , 20 ]. Tolstikhin et al. [21] studied estimation of the embedding μP itself. For

where ξP0 is the Stein feature 1. The main difference from the usual MMD setting is that the target P0 is fixed and the Stein feature depends on its score, hence it is typically unbounded. The result closest to ours is Cribeiro-Ramallo et al. [23] . They establish a minimax lower bound of order n−1/2 for KSD estimation, both for Langevin–Stein KSD on Rd and for KSDs on general domains. Their minimax analysis is worst-case over both the target P0 and the sampling distribution P . Their result settles the sample-size rate and, together with existing upper bounds, implies rate optimality of the usual V-statistic. In this paper the target distribution is fixed, and the worst case is taken only over the sampling distribution P . Our analysis identifies the sharp spectral scale of estimation, which is not visible from the optimal dependence on the sample size n alone. In particular, we show that the V-statistic is suboptimal in this spectral sense, whereas the debiased U-statistic is minimax optimal. For example, for a Gaussian target with a Gaussian kernel, the gap between the two scales grows exponentially with the dimension. The relation to quadratic functional estimation is also worth mentioning. A classical example is the problem of estimating R f 2, or more generally quadratic functionals of an unknown density; see, for example, Birgé and Massart [24] and Giné and Nickl [25] , together with the references therein. In that problem the density has to be reconstructed from the observations. If one uses a Rosenblatt–Parzen estimator, the bandwidth has to balance smoothing bias and variance. This is why the optimal rate depends on the regularity of the unknown density, with the usual elbow phenomenon at high regularity [ 24 ]. The KSD problem is different in this respect. Once the target score and the base kernel are fixed, the Stein kernel KP0 is known. Thus KSD 2(P0, P ) = EX,Y ∼P KP0 (X, Y ) is the expectation of a known two-sample kernel. Consequently, there is no density-smoothing bias for the unknown sampling distribution P ,and the rates are always parametric.

We are not merely interested in convergence rates, but also in identifying the sharp spectral scale that governs the minimax scale. Hence the minimax scale is achieved without estimating the full Stein mean embedding. We also quantify the behavior of the usual plug-in estimator. ## 2 Setup 

Let P0 be the target distribution on X ⊆ Rd. We assume that P0 has a continuously differentiable, everywhere positive density p0, and we write 

s0(x) = ∇ log p0(x)

for its score. The key feature of the KSD is that it depends on the target only through this score. Hence the normalizing constant of p0 is never needed. Let k ∈ C1,1(X × X ) be a positive definite kernel with RKHS Hk. We use the vector-valued Hilbert space H := Hdk.

The Langevin–Stein feature map associated with P0 is the H-valued function 

ξP0 (x) := s0(x)k(x, ·) + ∇xk(x, ·). (1) This feature map is the basic object of the paper: the KSD will be the norm of the average of these features. The inner product of two Stein features defines the Stein kernel 

KP0 (x, y ) := ⟨ξP0 (x), ξ P0 (y)⟩H .

Expanding this inner product gives the familiar closed form 

KP0 (x, y ) = s0(x)⊤s0(y) k(x, y ) + s0(x)⊤∇y k(x, y )+ s0(y)⊤∇xk(x, y ) + ∇x ·∇ y k(x, y ). (2) The expression above is useful computationally, because it allows the KSD to be evaluated using only the score of the target and derivatives of the base kernel. For a sampling distribution P , define the Stein mean embedding by 

μP := EP ξP0 (X),

whenever the Bochner integral is well defined. The non-normalized kernel Stein discrepancy is then 

KSD( P0, P ) := ∥μP ∥H.

Thus the statistical problem studied here is not to estimate the whole Hilbert-space vector μP , but only its norm. We impose the following basic integrability and Stein-identity assumption. 

Assumption 1. Stein’s identity holds at the target: 

μP0 = EP0 ξP0 (X) = 0 .

Moreover,

EP KP0 (X, X ) < ∞.

## 4 Conclusion 

We proved that the minimax rate of estimating the kernel Stein discrepancy at its target is of order O

p∥C⋆∥HS /n 



attained by the debiased square-root U-statistic but not by the plug-in V-statistic, whose risk is ≍ ptr( C⋆)/n and exceeds the optimum by reff (C⋆)1/4. For Gaussian targets with a fixed-bandwidth Gaussian kernel this factor is exponential in the dimension d.The mechanism is the degeneracy of the Stein kernel at P0 forced by Stein’s identity: the variance of Un collapses from 

O(n−1) to O(n−2), and the square root turns this variance collapse into the Hilbert–Schmidt risk scale. The plug-in cannot see this collapse, because its squared form retains the diagonal terms KP0 (Xi, X i) that keep it at the trace scale. The scalar functional KSD( P0, P ) is thus easier to estimate than the embedding μP , a saving the plug-in does not exploit; for KSD diagnostics in MCMC or variational inference we recommend the U-statistic form, especially in high dimensions. Our analysis assumes that the target score s0 = ∇ log p0 is known. If the score is estimated, as in score matching or learned energy-based models, the KSD estimator incurs an additional error not covered by the present bounds, and identifying the sharp scale of this error is a natural next question.
