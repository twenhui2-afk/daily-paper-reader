Abstract 

We consider the problem of finding the root vertex of a random uniform attachment tree, when the union of the unlabeled tree and an Erdős-Rényi random graph G(n, p ) is observed. We prove that, as long as p = o(log n/n ), for any ε > 0, one can construct a confidence set of vertices of size K(ε) that depends only on ε and not on n, such that it contains the root with probability at least 1 − ε. This affirms a conjecture of Crane and Xu [17]. Our approach ranks vertices by their Jordan centrality in the largest component of the subgraph spanned by high-degree vertices. We show that the same approach works in other noise models as well. 

# 1 Introduction 

An active area in combinatorial statistics is network archaeology , concerned with large networks that evolve in time. Upon observing the current configuration of the network, one wishes to infer its past properties. For example, one may wish to study the origin of a rumor spreading in social networks, the spread of computer viruses in computer networks, or the spread of a disease upon observing the network of currently infected individuals. Large networks that change dynamically over time are often modelled using simple random dy-namics, which, despite being relatively simple, can provide a quite accurate macroscopic description of real networks. The existing literature on network archaeology—see Haigh [23], Shah and Zaman [30], Shah and Zaman [29], Bubeck et al. [12], Lugosi and Pereira [28], Devroye and Reddad [21], Addario-Berry et al. [2], Curien et al. [19], Bubeck et al. [11], Bubeck et al. [13], Jog and Loh [24], Jog and Loh [25], Khim and Loh [26], Banerjee and Bhamidi [6], Banerjee and Huang [7], Crane 1arXiv:2607.18201v1 [math.PR] 20 Jul 2026 and Xu [17], Contat et al. [16], Addario-Berry et al. [3], Addario-Berry et al. [4], Briend et al. [10], Coll Josifov et al. [15], Bäumler et al. [14]–, mostly focuses on the simplest possible kind of networks, that is, trees. In various models of growing random trees, it is well understood to what extent one can identify the tree’s origin (i.e., the root) by observing a large unlabeled tree. These models in-clude uniform attachment, linear preferential attachment, and diffusion over regular trees. Perhaps surprisingly, in all these models, the size of the tree does not play a role. In other words, there exist root-finding algorithms that can select a small number of nodes such that the root vertex is among them with high probability, regardless of how large the tree is. A largely unexplored topic in network archaeology is robustness. Mis-specified models, noisy observations, and censored data all pose highly nontrivial challenges both from methodological and mathematical points of view. In a canonical model proposed by Crane and Xu [18], one aims at finding the root in random recursive trees based on a noisy attachment model in which the tree is observed with random edges added to it. More precisely, the statistician observes the union of a random recursive tree and an Erdős-Rényi random graph. In preferential attachment trees, where the root vertex typically has a very high degree, root-finding methods that are based on vertex degrees have near-optimal inference in the noiseless case (Banerjee and Huang [7], Contat et al. [16]). Hence, these methods are highly noise-tolerant, since sparse Erdős-Rényi random graphs do not have high-degree vertices. On the other hand, the case of the uniform attachment tree is significantly more challenging, since the root is not among the vertices with the highest degree (Devroye and Lu [20], Addario-Berry and Eslava [1], Eslava [22]). Even though Crane and Xu [18] determine the optimal root-vertex estimator, it is not known if bounded-size confidence sets exist for the root vertex, even if the noise is a sparse Erdős-Rényi random graph with constant average degree. Crane and Xu [18] prove that if the noisy edge probability is less than log n/n , then there exists a confidence set of size at most nγ for some γ < 0.8. At the same time, Crane and Xu conjecture that if the noisy edge probability is o(log n/n ), then the optimal confidence set has size Op(1) . The main result of this paper is precisely a proof of this conjecture. In particular, for every ε ∈ (0 , 1) , we construct a confidence set K of size |K| = O(log(1 /ε )/ε ) that contains the root vertex with probability at least 

1 − ε, independently of the size of the observed graph. The problem of root finding with Erdős-Rényi noise is illustrated by Figure 1. Our construction is based on “filtering out” the noise edges belonging to the Erdős-Rényi graph by considering the subgraph spanned by vertices whose degree exceeds a carefully chosen threshold. We show that, with high probability, the largest component of the resulting graph is a tree that contains the root vertex. By listing the |K| most central vertices of this tree (according to their 

Jordan centrality ), we obtain the desired confidence set. We also show that our framework can handle noise models different from Erdős-Rényi graphs. As an example, we consider random matching noise, when the uniform attachment tree is observed with a random perfect matching added to the vertices; see Figure 2 for an illustration. 2(a) Union of a urrt and G(50 , 0.05) . In this section, we state the key definitions and our main results. 

Definition 1.1. A uniform random recursive tree ( urrt ) on the vertex set [n] is a connected acyclic graph constructed recursively as follows. (i) Start with the graph T1, where T1 is the singleton graph on {1} with an isolated vertex 1.(ii) For each i ≥ 1, given Ti, to construct Ti+1 , introduce the vertex i + 1 , which randomly samples a parent from Ti and connects to it via an edge. In particular, for each i ≥ 1, Ti is a tree on the vertex set [i].Our main interest in this paper is to infer the root vertex 1. In particular, we consider the problem of finding the root when the unlabeled tree Tn is observed in the presence of additional random edges. In order to address this problem, we first consider suitably pruned sub-forests Fn

of Tn, and assume that the combinatorial structure of the forest Fn is observed without the vertex labels . The first question we address is the following: Observing the pruned sub-forest, given an 

ε > 0, can one construct a confidence set K whose size does not depend on n but only on ε, such that the root vertex lies in K with probability at least 1 − ε?The study of the case Fn = Tn was initiated by Bubeck, Devroye, and Lugosi [12], who proved that the answer is yes and gave explicit upper bounds on the size of the confidence set K(ε).Addario-Berry, Fontaine, Khanfir, Langevin, and Têtu [3] show that the size of K(ϵ) can be as small as eC√log(1 /ϵ ) for a constant C > 0.Let us now define the precise types of forests Fn we consider. Throughout the paper, for any graph G and a vertex v in it, dG(v) denotes the degree of v in G.

Definition 1.2 (α-forests) . Let α ∈ (0 , 1) . Define the α-forest Fn(α) obtained by removing every edge from Tn which has at least one end vertex u with dTn (u) ≤ (1 − α) log n.

Remark 1.5 (Size of K). Our proofs show that can take K(ε) to have size at most C log(1 /ϵ )/ε for a constant C > 0. Indeed, our analysis shows that the bound for the confidence set obtained by taking 5the K(ϵ) most central vertices (according to Jordan centrality) in a urrt derived by Bubeck et al. [12] is inherited to the current setting. Note that a more careful analysis of Jordan centrality in a 

urrt yields the improved bound C/ϵ , see [15, Theorem 9]. We believe that a similar improvement is possible, but we do not pursue this direction, in order to keep the arguments manageable. Similarly, we believe that by ordering vertices according to rumor centrality instead of Jordan centrality may give confidence sets of size eC√log(1 /ϵ ) as in [3], but we leave this for future research. This result opens doors towards root finding from noisy observations of the urrt Tn. To state these results, we begin with a definition. 

Definition 1.6 (Noisy recursive trees) . For any graph G = ( V, E ) on the vertex set V = [ n], define the graph Tn(G) as the union of Tn and G. That is, Tn(G) has vertex set [n], with an edge between two vertices present if and only if the corresponding edge is either present in Tn or in G.We think of the edges coming from G as noise , hiding information about the structure of Tn

from the statistician. The canonical example, considered by Crane and Xu [17], is when G is an Erdős-Rényi random graph. 

Theorem 1.7. Fix ε > 0. Consider Tn(G), where G = G(n, λ/n ) is an Erdős-Rényi random graph independent of Tn, with edge connection probability λ/n , where λ = λn may depend on n. If λn =

o(log n), then upon observing the graph Tn(G) without its vertex labels, one can construct a confidence set K = K(ε) of vertices, whose size does not depend on n, such that lim inf n→∞ P (1 ∈ K) ≥ 1 − ε.

The condition λn = o(log n) is sufficient for root finding, but need not be necessary. We leave the question of the exact location for the transition from the possible to the impossible regime as an interesting problem for future research. To illustrate the generality of our techniques, we show that root finding is also possible when G

is a random perfect matching :

Theorem 1.8. Fix ε > 0, and assume that n is even. Let G be a uniformly sampled perfect matching on the complete graph on [n], independent of Tn. Then, observing the unlabeled graph Tn(G), it is possible to construct a confidence set K(ε), such that P (1 ∈ K(ε)) ≥ 1 − ε.

Remark 1.9. The sizes of K = K(ε) in both Theorems 1.8 and 1.7 can be taken to be at most 

C log(1 /ε )/ε , as in Remark 1.5. This is because, as our proof shows, the problem with noise can be broken down into an instance of Theorem 1.4, so that, with high probability, the same set K can be found despite the presence of the noise. The techniques that we develop to prove Theorems 1.4, 1.7, and 1.8 work under some reasonably general conditions on the noise graph G. In Section 6 we discuss the applicability of our techniques. The outline of the proof of Theorem 1.4 is as follows. If all the degrees in the urrt Tn were equal to their expected values, then the forest Fn(α) would consist of the vertices {1, 2, . . . , n α}

In this idealized scenario, Fn(α) is simply a urrt on nα vertices, and standard techniques deliver 6the confidence set K, e.g., by ranking vertices by their Jordan centrality [12]. However, random fluctuations of the vertex degrees significantly change the structure of Fn(α). Nevertheless, we show that Fn(α) contains an initial chunk of the urrt , up to the first nγ vertices for some γ > 0.Additionally, we show that the connected component containing this chunk (and therefore the root) is the largest component in Fn(α). The main remaining challenge is to show that the largest component ‘behaves’ like a urrt (of its own size) in a certain sense, so that ranking vertices by their Jordan centrality can be applied to it to obtain the confidence set K. This behavior is established using exchangeability arguments. The proof of Theorem 1.7 follows a similar structure, although establishing that the largest component is a urrt -like object is more challenging.

# 6 A general discussion on robust root finding 

In this paper, we developed a robust method of root finding that works in urrt s with additional noisy edges. We focused on two specific examples of Tn(G) = Tn ∪ G, with G being the random perfect matching (Theorem 1.8) and G = G(n, λ n/n ) (Theorem 1.7). The technique can be adapted for more general choices of G, as long as G satisfies certain properties. In order to guarantee that the approach works, it suffices the check the following steps: 

## 6.1 Sandwiching 

Consider any G, with max v∈[n] dG(v) = o(log n). The subgraph Hn(α) spanned by edges with both end-vertices having degree at least (1 − α) log n in Tn(G) satisfies the "sandwitching" properties of Lemma 4.4 and Corollary 4.5. This means that Hn(α) is a forest, whose largest component CH,α (1) 

contains the root, with high probability. Moreover, it contains the urrt T⌊nγ−ε⌋ up to the first 

bnγ−εc steps. In the proof of Theorem 1.7, the sandwiching step is where the condition λn = o(log n) is used. Indeed, when λn = Ω(log n), the maximum degree of the noise graph starts competing with the maximum degree of the urrt , and the sandwiching step is not valid anymore. 

## 6.2 Exchangeability 

By the sandwiching step, we can construct the component CH,α (1) by grafting to each vertex 

v ∈ [bnγ−εc] of T⌊nγ−ε⌋, the subtree tn(v) := CH,α (1)( v; E⌊nγ−ε⌋). The key question is whether the collection (tn(1) , . . . , t n(bnγ−εc)) is exchangeable. In that case, the arguments of Propositions 4.16 and 4.18 go through, and root finding is possible. We have worked with two somewhat closely related methods to verify this exchangeability. In the proof of Theorem 1.4, we established exchangeability by writing (tn(v), v ) as ψ(1 −α) log n−1off (Tn(v; E⌊nγ−ε⌋), v )

and using that such functionals are exchangeable, due to Proposition 2.11. For Theorem 1.7, this exchangeability is established using Proposition 2.15, which says that ‘samples from exchangeable measures are exchangeable’. If exchangeability can be established by a model-dependent argument, then our techniques can be used for proving that root-finding is possible. 53 6.3 Example: random regular graphs 

Another example where our techniques can be applied is when G is a random dn-regular graph with 

dn = o(log n). (The special case with dn = 1 reduces to random perfect matchings discussed above.) The condition on dn ensures that the sandwiching step (Lemma 4.4 and Corollary 4.5) go through when we construct the high-degree subgraph Hn(α). A calculation analogous to Lemma 4.8 shows that all vertices with degree at least (1 − α) log n in Tn have neighborhoods that are independent size-dn random subsets of [n]. Consequently, for any L ∈ { 0, 1, . . . , n }, if we define XL := |S \ [L]|,where S is a size-dn random subset of [n], we can construct tn(v) in a similar way as in Proposition 4.13: (i) To each vertex in Tn(v; E⌊nγ−ε⌋), add XdTn (v) extra leaves to construct the random tree (T′ 

> v
