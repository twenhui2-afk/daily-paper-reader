ON CANONICAL ROOTS OF FRACTIONAL IDEALS     

> D. M. H. VAN GENT
> Abstract. We give an algorithm to compute in polynomial time the roots of a fractional ideal of an order R. We take care not to assume Ris Dedekind, since the maximal order of a number field is generally inaccessible in polynomial time. Consequently, the output of such an algorithm is no longer uniquely defined. For it to be a satisfying algorithm we additionally require it be functorial, i.e., isomorphisms on the inputs should induce isomorphisms on the outputs. To adhere to these two constraints, we generalize results from Dade–Taussky–Zassenhaus, and Ge and Buchmann– Eisenbrand.

1. Introduction 

Let K be a number field. By factoring the polynomial Xn −a over K, we may efficiently test if a ∈ K

has an n-th root in K and if so compute it [12]. It is a rule of thumb that any sufficiently multiplicative statement on the elements of K∗ might be better stated for the invertible ideals of the maximal order of 

K instead. Accordingly, Belabas–Simon [1] gave a polynomial-time algorithm that, given the maximal order of a number field and an invertible ideal a of this order, computes the roots of a. The maximal order however is generally inaccessible in polynomial time; the difficulty of computing it is similar to that of prime factorization (cf. Theorem 1.3 in [5]). This algorithmic inaccessibility can be mitigated by a design paradigm used in for example [4, 5, 9]. Instead of formulating an algorithm for the maximal order, one allows an arbitrary order R. Then, if a step of the algorithm fails because R does not resemble the maximal order well enough, the algorithm should instead return a strictly larger order of the same number field, with which we can replace R. Essentially, R is an approximation of the maximal order. In this paper we present such a polynomial-time root-taking algorithm for fractional ideals of orders. Unlike the algorithm of Belabas–Simon, ours does not require additional ramification data. We encounter two ways an order R can fail to resemble the maximal order O. Firstly, it can be that an ideal a of R has no (non-trivial) roots, but that it has roots in O (Example 7.7). Secondly, even if a root exists in R, it need not be unique or even characteristic, i.e., fixed by the automorphisms that fix a (Example 7.8). Uniqueness is an essential obstruction; if we could efficiently compute an order 

R where for each n each fractional ideal has a unique n-th root, then we could efficiently compute O

(Proposition 7.10). Instead, we impose that our algorithms compute a functor (Section 3). We will prove our results for a broader class of rings than in the PhD thesis of the author [16], on which the present paper is based: for us, an order will be a commutative ring R which is free of finite rank as Z-module, which consequently may contain zero-divisors, and a fractional ideal of R is a finitely generated R-submodule a ⊆ QR such that Qa = QR. To this end, we will similarly generalize results of Dade–Taussky–Zassenhaus [7], and Ge and Buchmann–Eisenbrand [4, 9]. Although we opt to present full proofs for completeness, the contributions of the present paper to these generalizations should be considered minor. Our main result is as follows.

The algorithm of [1] and the algorithm of Theorem 1.1 can both be summarized as partially factoring a depending on the Z/(a ∩ Z)-algebra structure of R/ a until the maximal root of a can be read off from this factorization. In fact, for the inputs in which both algorithms apply, their behavior is essentially the same. We use Theorem 1.3 (cf. For the factorization of a we require a generalization of the factor refinement algorithm of Ge [9] for fractional ideals of orders in number rings. Buchmann–Eisenbrand [4] show that the algorithm of Ge can be made functorial using results from Dade–Taussky–Zassenhaus [7] on blowups. We will generalize these results to a broader class of rings. We briefly describe how we choose to represent our mathematical objects algorithmically. Ideally, whether an algorithm runs in polynomial time is independent of this choice, as long as the choice is reasonable. Hence the coming sections are written in a sufficiently abstract way that is agnostic to the choice of representation, following the style of [13]. We encode a finitely generated free Abelian group by an integer n, its rank, and a linear map between such groups as a matrix in the natural way. A subgroup is represented by (the image of) an injective linear map. Using the LLL-algorithm [14, Section 5] we may replace the representation of a full-rank subgroup by an equivalent one, i.e., one with the same image, such that the lengths of the coefficients of the matrix representing this map are bounded by a polynomial in the length of the index of this subgroup. Consequently, iterative computations avoid coefficient blowup as long as we properly bound the index. Recall that an order is a commutative ring that as an Abelian group is finitely generated and free. We encode an order as a finitely generated free Abelian group together with some M ∈ Zn×n×n

A fractional ideal of an order R is up to integer scaling a finitely generated subgroup of R and will be encoded as such. For fractional ideals a and b of R we may efficiently compute a + b, ab and 

a : b. Consequently, for an additional fixed constant k ∈ Z≥1 we may compute ak in polynomial time. However, should k be variable, then k must be encoded in unary if we desire a polynomial-time algorithm, as would be the case for integer exponentiation. A concept that we will make use of in this paper is that of a functorial algorithm, or equivalently an algorithm that computes a functor. Suppose F : C → D is a functor and suppose we have chosen a representation of the objects and morphisms of both C and D. We say an algorithm computes F if the algorithm takes as input objects X, Y ∈ C and a morphism f : X → Y , and outputs F (X), F (Y )and F (f ). When dealing with category theory informally it is common to be vague about the action of a functor on the morphisms, as it is generally ‘obvious’ what it should be. The algorithm of Theorem 7.5 differs from that of Belabas–Simon [1] mainly in that we allow arbitrary orders, while [1] requires that the order be the maximal order of a number field. Even in the case of a maximal order of a number field, the algorithm of [1] requires additional ramification data of which it is unclear if it can be computed in polynomial time given just the maximal order. Thus our proof offers an improvement also in the specialized case. The practical performance of the two algorithms could be quite similar on the inputs where both apply, as there the algorithms behave essentially the same, factoring the ideal a depending on the structure of R/ a as Z/(a ∩ Z)-algebra.
