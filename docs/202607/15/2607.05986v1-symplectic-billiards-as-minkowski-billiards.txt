# Symplectic billiards as Minkowski billiards 

## Peter Albers ∗ Ana Chavez Caliz † Serge Tabachnikov ‡

Abstract 

We establish a connection between Minkowski billiards and sym-plectic billiards, two classes of dynamical systems that have been stud-ied largely independently. We show that the Minkowski billiard map can be described in symplectic terms via reduction from the canoni-cal symplectic structure on V × V ∗, and that symplectic billiards can be viewed as a “square root” of a symplectic version of Minkowski billiards. As an application, we recover several known results on symplectic billiards from the more general Minkowski setting, and extend some of them to higher dimensions and to periodic orbits of even period. In particular, we prove the existence of at least ( r − 1)( n − 1) 2 r-periodic symplectic billiard orbits in dimension 2 n.

# 1 Introduction 

The goal of this paper is to connect two “stories” that seem to have evolved in-dependently of each other. The first “story” concerns Finsler billiards, intro-duced a while ago in [19], more specifically their particular case, Minkowski billiards. This subject remained in a dormant stage until its connections with symplectic capacities and the Viterbo and Mahler conjectures were discov-ered and studied in [1, 6, 7]. See also [22, 27, 31]. We recall the definition and main properties of Minkowski billiards in Section 2.

## 2.1 Polar duality, Legendre transform, Minkowski met-ric 

We start with a reminder of some basic notions of convex geometry. Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space and V ∗ its dual space. Let 

Q ⊂ V be a strictly convex closed C1-smooth hypersurface that contains the origin in its interior, i.e. the region it bounds in V . To every vector q ∈ Q

one assigns the covector p ∈ V ∗ uniquely defined by the conditions Ker p = TqQ and q · p = 1 ,

where the dot denotes the pairing of vectors and covectors. The set of these covectors p comprises a closed hypersurface P ⊂ V ∗ that is also strictly convex and contains the origin in its interior. The hypersurface P is called the polar dual to Q, written as P = Q∗, and the map L : q 7 → p is called the Legendre transform . The hypersurface polar dual to P is Q, and the Legendre transform P → Q is the inverse of the Legendre transform Q → P .Of course, here we use the canonical identification V ∼= V ∗∗ .A (not necessarily symmetric) Minkowski metric in a vector space V

is defined by a strictly convex closed C1-smooth hypersurface I ⊂ V that contains the origin in its interior as follows. The norm |v| of a non-zero vector v ∈ V is, by definition, |v| := t where t satisfies vt ∈ I and t > 0. The hypersurface I is the unit sphere in this metric; it is called the indicatrix ,and its polar dual is called the figuratrix .

## 2.3 2-periodic orbits 

It is known that a strictly convex closed C1-smooth hypersurface in n-dimensional space has at least n diameters, that is, the chords orthogonal to the hypersurface at both endpoints (see [21], Theorem 4). These diame-ters are 2-periodic billiard trajectories. We extend this result to Minkowski billiards. 

Theorem 1 The Minkowski billiard associated with hypersurfaces Q ⊂ V

and P ⊂ V ∗, where dim V = n, has at least n two-periodic orbits. 

Proof. For convenience, identify V with Rn, so we use normals as Legendre transforms. A 2-periodic orbit is presented in Figure 5. For every point q ∈ Q, denote by q∗ the point where the normal N (q∗) is antiparallel to that at q, and let p∗ have a similar meaning for points p ∈ P .A 2-periodic orbit of the Minkowski billiard is a quadruple ( q, p, q ∗, p ∗) such that q∗ − q ∼ N (p) and p∗ − p ∼ N (q). 7Figure 5: A two-periodic orbit of the Minkowski billiard ( q, p, q ∗, p ∗). The line qq ∗ is parallel to the normals at the points p and p∗, and the line pp ∗ is parallel to the normals at the points q and q∗.Let ∆ = {(q, p, q 1, p 1) | q = q1 or p = p1} be the “diagonal.” Consider the function Φ( q, p, q 1, p 1) = ( q1 − q) · (p − p1)from Lemma 2.2 in the complement of ∆. The group Z2 × Z2 acts on the quadruples ( q, p, q 1, p 1) by swapping q with q1 and p with p1. The function Φ is odd with respect to each factor Z2 and is invariant under the diagonal action of Z2: ( q, p, q 1, p 1) 7 → (q1, p 1, q, p ). A critical point of Φ off ∆ is a quadruple ( q, p, q 1, p 1) such that q1 − q is collinear with N (p) and N (p1), and p1 − p is collinear with N (q) and N (q1). Hence q1 = q∗, p 1 = p∗, and a 2-periodic orbit of the Minkowski billiard corresponds to a Z2 × Z2-orbit of this critical point. These critical values of Φ are separated from zero. Let M ⊂ Q × P × Q × P be the set of quadruples where Φ is positive. In particular, q1̸ = q and p1̸ = p in M . We claim that M is homotopically equivalent to Sn−1.Let 

N = {(u, v ) | u, v ∈ Rn, |u| = |v| = 1 , u · v > 0}.

Then N is homotopically equivalent to Sn−1: for every unit vector u, the set of unit vectors v with u · v > 0 is a hemisphere, a contractible set. Next, we have a projection M → N that assigns to ( q, p, q 1, p 1) the normalized to unit 8vectors q1 − q and p − p1. The fibers of this projection are contractible as well, which proves the claim. Let ε > 0 be small enough that there are no critical points of Φ in M

with the critical values greater than or equal to ε. Let Mε be the set where Φ ≥ ε. Then Mε is a manifold with boundary, and the gradient of Φ has the inward direction along the boundary. Therefore we can apply the Morse– Lusternik–Schnirelmann theory to Mε. Since the diagonal action of Z2 is free on Mε and Φ is Z2-invariant, the number of critical Z2-orbits is not less than the category of M/ Z2, which is homotopically equivalent to RP n−1. The Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of this space is n, and the result follows.

## 2.4 Symplectic structure 

The space V × V ∗ has a canonical symplectic structure Ω = dq ∧ dp , and this makes it possible to describe Minkowski billiard maps as symplectic transformations. In the recent literature on the relation between Minkowski billiards and the Viterbo and Mahler conjectures, the dynamics of Minkowski billiards are described in terms of the Reeb flow on the boundary of the Lagrangian product of two convex bodies; see [1, 6, 7, 9, 20, 22, 26, 27, 28]. We present below a somewhat different treatment, similar to the more familiar case of Birkhoff billiards. The left vertical projection is the quotient of the space Q × V ∗ by the characteristics, and by the lemma above, the quotient space is the cotangent bundle T ∗Q with its canonical symplectic structure, obtained from Ω by symplectic reduction, that is, the restriction to a hypersurface Q × V ∗ and factorizing by the kernel of this restriction. Now consider the map Q × P to T ∗Q, the composition of the inclusion 

Q × P ⊂ Q × V ∗ and the projection Q × V ∗ → T ∗Q. The image consists of points ( q, p ) where |p| = 1 in the Minkowski norm defined by P . Every point in the image has two preimages in Q × P , and an involution arises that interchanges these two preimages. By construction, this involution preserves the restriction of Ω to Q × P , which is symplectic. Likewise, one has another involution of Q × P , similarly associated with the map Q × P to T ∗P . ## 3.3 Some results on symplectic billiards revisited 

In this section we show how some results on symplectic billiards, obtained in [5], follow from our current setting. We also extend some of these results to higher dimensions and greater periods. 

3.3.1 Four-periodic symplectic billiard orbits 

It is proved in [5], Theorem 12, that the number of 4-periodic symplectic billiard orbits in 2 n-dimensional symplectic space is not less than 2 n. In view 13 of the correspondence between symplectic billiards and Minkowski billiards, this result follows from Theorem 1 above. 

3.3.2 Ellipsoids 

Consider a Minkowski billiard system associated with a pair of hypersurfaces (Q, P ), where P is an ellipsoid centered at the origin. As we mentioned earlier, in this case one is dealing with the usual billiard in Q with the Euclidean metric defined by P . If Q is also an ellipsoid, this billiard system is completely integrable, see, e.g., [30]. Now consider the symplectic billiard in an ellipsoid. Then we are in the setting described in the preceding paragraph, and Theorem 3 implies that if ( . . . q, p, q 1, p 1, q 2, p 2, . . . ) is a trajectory of the symplectic billiard, then (. . . q, q 1, q 2, . . . ) is a trajectory of the respective Minkowski billiard. In par-ticular, the symplectic billiard map in an ellipsoid is completely integrable. This is another result of [5], see Theorem 10 therein. 

3.3.3 Radon curves and Minkowski bodies of constant width 

The Birkhoff billiard in a curve of constant width possesses an invariant curve consisting of 2-periodic points, the diameters of the curve, i.e., the chords that are orthogonal to the curve at both endpoints. Likewise for bodies of constant width in higher dimensions. Consider the Minkowski billiard defined by hypersurfaces Q ⊂ V and 

P ⊂ V ∗, where dim V = n. Let ¯Q := Q ⊕ (−Q), ¯P := P ⊕ (−P )be the symmetrizations of Q and P , respectively (where ⊕ denotes the Minkowski sum). 

Lemma 3.1 If ¯P = ¯Q∗ then the Minkowski billiard has an (n−1) -parameter family of 2-periodic orbits: such an orbit originates at every point q ∈ Q and exists for every direction. 

Proof. Referring to Figure 5 and the notations of the proof of Theorem 1, one needs to do the following: given q ∈ Q, define p by N (p) ∼ q∗ − q, and check that p∗ − p ∼ N (q). 14 First, observe that if the above property holds for ¯Q and ¯P then it holds for Q and P as well. Indeed, q∗ −q is the point of ¯Q with the same (co)normal as at point q∗ of Q, and likewise for P . Thus we may assume that Q and P

are centrally symmetric to start with. With this assumption, N (p) ∼ q∗ − q means that p is the Legendre trans-form of q∗ = −q and, similarly, p∗ − p ∼ N (q) means that Q is the Legendre transform of p∗ = −p. These two conditions are equivalent because the Legendre transform is an involution. 2

For example, if P is a circle, then we are dealing with Birkhoff billiards. If Q is a curve of constant width, then ¯Q is also a circle, and ¯Q∗ = ¯P . More generally, one can take, as Q and P , two curves of constant width. It was observed in [5, Section 2.4.2] that the symplectic billiard map with respect to a Radon curve possesses an invariant curve consisting of 4-periodic orbits. Radon curves are self-dual, that is, they satisfy the condition of Lemma 3.1, therefore this lemma implies the said observation concerning Radon curves in [5]. Note also that a recent paper [14] contains a related result: for a symplec-tically self-polar convex body, the outer billiard has an invariant hypersurface consisting of centrally symmetric 4-periodic orbits. The relation to symplec-tic billiard is that the midpoints of an outer billiard 4-periodic orbit form a 4-periodic orbit of the symplectic billiard inside the same body. 

3.3.4 Periodic orbits 

Concerning periodic orbits of symplectic billiards in 2 n-dimensional space, the following two results are proved in [5]: 

1) For every r ≥ 2, the symplectic billiard map has an r-periodic trajectory; 2) The number of 3- and 4-periodic symplectic billiard trajectories is not less than 2n.

(Here one is counting the orbits of the dihedral group Dr acting on r-periodic trajectories). However, Minkowski billiards are a particular case of Finsler billiards. Periodic orbits of the latter were studied in [16], and one can apply the results obtained therein to symplectic billiards. The main result of [16], Theorem 1.2, specialized to the Minkowski set-ting, is as follows. Consider the Minkowski billiard map in a strictly convex closed C1-smooth hypersurface in d ≥ 3-dimensional vector space. Let r ≥ 315 be a prime number. Denote by NM (d, r ) the number of r-periodic Minkowski billiard orbits, where two orbits that differ by a cyclic permutation of points are considered to be the same (i.e., here one counts the orbits modulo the action of the cyclic group Zr). Then NM (d, r ) ≥ (r − 1)( d − 2) + 1. We note that this result holds for every strictly convex closed C1-smooth hypersurface, that is, it is obtained via Lusternik–Schnirelmann theory; if the hypersurface is generic in the sense that the Morse theory applies, and d

is even, the result is stronger: NM (d, r ) ≥ (r − 1) d. We also note that these lower bounds apply to non-reversible Finsler billiards, such as the magnetic ones); one expects to have stronger results for reversible ones.
