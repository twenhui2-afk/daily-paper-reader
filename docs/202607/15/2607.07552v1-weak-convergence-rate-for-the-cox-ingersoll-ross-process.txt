# Weak convergence rate for the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross process 

Alexandros Pavlis 1 and Umut C ¸ etin 11Department of Statistics, London School of Economics and Political Science, 10 Houghton Street, London, WC2A 2AE, UK 

Abstract 

We study the weak convergence rate of a drift-implicit discretisation scheme for the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process in the regime where the process remains strictly positive. Specifi-cally, we consider scheme (4) of Alfonsi [8], which arises naturally from applying a drift-implicit Euler step to the SDE satisfied by the square root of the CIR process and admits a unique positive closed-form solution at each time step. Using a PDE approach combined with a continuous-time SDE representation of the discretised process, we prove that the weak convergence rate is O(1 /N ) under the Feller condition 2 α ≥ θ2 and mild polynomial growth conditions on the payoff function. The proof requires only elementary techniques and, in particular, avoids the semi-exact simulation machinery used in earlier work. The methodology is expected to extend to a broader class of diffusion processes.

# 1 Introduction 

The Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process is defined by the stochastic differential equation 

dX t = k(μ − Xt) dt + θpXt dW t, X0 = x, t ∈ R+, (1) where W = {Wt}t≥0 is a one-dimensional Brownian motion, k ≥ 0 is the speed of mean reversion, 

μ ≥ 0 is the long-run mean, and θ > 0 is the diffusion coefficient. We write α := kμ throughout. The process (1) was introduced by Cox, Ingersoll and Ross [1] as a model for short-term interest rates. By Feller’s test [2], if X0 > 0 then 

P(Xt > 0, t ≥ 0) = 1 (2) provided the Feller condition 2 α ≥ θ2 holds. The CIR process has since found widespread use beyond interest rate modelling, notably as the variance process in the Heston stochastic volatility model. At the core of most financial applications is the evaluation of E[f (XT )], where f is a payoff function and T > 0 is a fixed horizon. Although the transition density of X is known explicitly as a scaled non-central chi-squared distribution, the expectation E[f (XT )] rarely admits a closed form for general f , and one must resort to numerical approximation. Even though the non-central chi-squared structure of the increments permits exact simulation in principle [8], this is computationally demanding, and discretisation-based schemes 1arXiv:2607.07552v1 [math.PR] 8 Jul 2026 are generally preferred in practice. Literature on strong convergence Strong convergence of discretisation schemes for the CIR process has been studied extensively. Deelstra and Delbaen [6] and Bossy and Diop [7] established strong convergence results for variants of implicit schemes. Alfonsi [8] proved strong convergence of order 1 for schemes (3) and (4) under the Feller condition. Neuenkirch and Szpruch [10] and Dereich, Neuenkirch and Szpruch [9] subsequently proved first-order strong convergence for a related class of drift-implicit methods under optimal conditions. 

Literature on weak convergence Weak convergence is arguably the more relevant notion for derivative pricing, since one is interested in the accuracy of E[f ( ˆXT )] as an approximation to 

E[f (XT )]. For SDEs with globally Lipschitz coefficients, the classical work of Talay and Tubaro established a weak convergence rate of O(1 /N ) for the Euler-Maruyama scheme; see also Mil-stein [3]. For the CIR process, the non-Lipschitz nature of the square-root diffusion coefficient makes these classical results inapplicable. Alfonsi [8] studied the weak convergence of his schemes under a hypothesis (HW) on the discretisations, and showed that, conditional on (HW) holding, a weak convergence rate of O(1 /N ) follows. However, (HW) does not hold for the more natural discretisation scheme (6), while it is valid for (4). 2Contribution of the present paper The purpose of this article is to prove rigorously that the weak convergence rate of scheme (6), i.e. scheme (4) of [8], is O(1 /N ) under the Feller condition 2α ≥ θ2 and a mild polynomial growth assumption on f . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first rigorous proof of the optimal weak convergence rate for this scheme. We highlight three distinguishing features of our work relative to the existing literature. First, we work directly with scheme (6), which is arguably the simplest of Alfonsi’s implicit schemes to implement in practice. Second, the payoff regularity we require is mild: we need only f ∈ C (2) ((0 , ∞), R) with |f ′′ (x)| ≤ 

K(1+ xm) for some m ≥ 2, whereas [8] requires four continuous derivatives with polynomial growth. Third, the methodology itself is elementary and general.
