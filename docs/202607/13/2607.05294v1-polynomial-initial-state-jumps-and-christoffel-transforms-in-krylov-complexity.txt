# Polynomial Initial-State Jumps and Christoffel Transforms in Krylov Complexity 

Abhishek Chowdhury a Ajit Prasad Mahapatra a

School of Basic Sciences, Indian Institute of Technology Bhubaneswar, Argul, Odisha 752050, India

Abstract: State Krylov, or spread, complexity is a property of a pair (H, |K0⟩) rather than of the Hamiltonian alone. Thus, changing the initial state at fixed H generally changes the Lanczos coefficients and the ordered Krylov basis. We solve this relative initial-state problem for normalized polynomial filters, |ψQ⟩ = Q(H)|K0⟩/√NQ with NQ = ⟨K0|Q(H)†Q(H)|K0⟩.The filtered spectral measure is the positive polynomial modification |Q(E)|2dμ(E)/N Q, and orthogonality turns this measure change into a finite-band transfer from reference Fourier– orthogonal-polynomial moments to shifted Krylov amplitudes. We derive exact finite sums for individual amplitudes and projected Christoffel–Darboux kernels for cumulative probabilities and spread complexity. The formulae cover confluent roots, complex seed coefficients, support loss, and terminal quotients in finite dimensions. We evaluate the construction in three canonical Jacobi families, the Heisenberg–Weyl/Charlier oscillator, the compact SU (2) /Krawtchouk spin, and the constant-coefficient tight-binding/Chebyshev chain, with a Hermite central-limit scaling of Charlier as a continuous-spectrum check of this Christoffel jump machinery. Finite seed families are organized by a matrix-valued parent measure whose scalar compressions recover the individual shifted problems. The fixed-inner-product construction carries over to operator Krylov complexity after the replacement 

H 7 → L and |K0⟩ 7 → O; polynomial seeds then become nested-commutator descendants 

Q(L)O. The result is an exact relative calculus in which a solved cyclic problem generates a family of polynomially related initial-state dynamics without repeating Lanczos in the original Hilbert space. 

Keywords: Krylov complexity, spread complexity, Christoffel transforms, orthogonal polynomials, Jacobi matrices, Krylov subspaces

1 Introduction 

Krylov complexity provides a one-dimensional description of quantum evolution. Starting from a normalized state |K0⟩, a time-independent Hamiltonian generates the cyclic orbit 

{| K0⟩, H |K0⟩, H 2|K0⟩, . . . }. Lanczos orthogonalization maps this orbit into an ordered basis {| K0⟩, |K1⟩, . . . }, and the time-evolved wavefunction spreads along the corresponding Krylov chain. For spread complexity of states, this basis is intrinsic to the problem. It is selected by the pair (H, |K0⟩) and is privileged by the nested short-time minimization – 1 – underlying the spread-complexity construction [ 1 ]. The fundamental object of study is therefore not H alone, but the pair (H, |K0⟩).Changing the initial state while keeping the Hamiltonian fixed is, in the Lanczos sense, already a new Krylov problem. Although two complete orthonormal bases of a common Hilbert subspace are related by a unitary, the Krylov ordering is not an arbitrary basis choice as it is reconstructed from the orbit of the chosen seed under H. Thus, a linear change of the initial vector does not induce a linear transformation of the Lanczos coefficients or of the ordered Krylov basis. Even when the new seed lies in the reference Krylov span, its Lanczos chain must be reorthogonalized from that seed; in finite dimension, the new orbit may also terminate earlier. Recent literature has emphasized variations of initial conditions, Hamiltonians, and Hilbert-space dimension, introducing quantities such as Koherence to compare perturbed and reference Krylov bases [ 2 –6 ]. In this work, we isolate a broad algebraically controlled class by assuming the new seed is obtained from the reference seed via a polynomial spectral filter,

In solvable chains, this class encompasses number-state jumps in the Heisenberg–Weyl oscillator, weight-state jumps in finite-spin representations, and fixed-Hamiltonian localized-site jumps in constant-coefficient tight-binding models, among others. In a many-body system, a polynomial in the full Hamiltonian is a global spectral operation, not a local operator insertion. At a fixed low degree, it gives a controlled displacement within the first few reference Krylov levels, whereas a degree that grows with system size can generate an increasingly extended preparation. The formalism remains exact at higher degree, but the nearby-jump interpretation belongs to the fixed low-degree regime used below. This regime appears in several concrete ways. In solvable models, it includes excited oscillator states, finite-spin-weight states, and localized sites in a fixed tight-binding Hamil-tonian, among others. In a finite chaotic system, it gives a controlled family of low-degree spectral preparations for the same Hamiltonian, so that initial-state dependence can be sep-arated from changing the spectrum, the Hilbert-space dimension, or H itself. In numerical many-body work, the practical gain is that the same reference Krylov data can be reused to survey fixed low-degree seed deformations through finite connectors and projected kernels, rather than repeating the full Hilbert-space Krylov construction for each seed. The same spectral-versus-spatial distinction is useful for operator Krylov complexity. Once an operator inner product is fixed, Heisenberg evolution is a spread problem in Liouville space, with the Liouvillian L = [ H, ·] replacing H [ 8– 10 ]. In that setting, a polynomial seed – 2 – is the nested-commutator descendant Q(L)O. The scalar theory developed below applies directly to fixed-inner-product, self-adjoint Liouvillian problems. Changes of operator inner product or temperature are also measure changes; appendix H explains when they reduce to scalar Liouvillian-frequency filters, when the finite-band polynomial proposition applies, and when additional matrix-valued or bivariate spectral data are needed. Mixed preparations also fit this spectral framework once the relevant scalar or operator realization has been chosen. Without choosing a purification, a Gelfand–Naimark–Segal (GNS) representation, or a Liouville-space inner product, the canonical object is the scalar functional f 7 → Tr (ρf (H)) .Appendix G explains how finite mixed preparations in a reference Krylov window appear as scalar compressions of the matrix-valued parent measure of section 6, while appendix H treats density matrices as operator seeds when the physical Liouville evolution is intended. The preceding sections used only the data of a Jacobi problem and a choice of polynomial seed, without invoking any underlying group-theoretic structure. Group-theoretic Hamiltonians are valuable because they provide an explicit solution to the reference Jacobi problem, yielding the reference recurrence, the measure, and the moments In(t) in closed form. The shifted construction then transforms these reference data into shifted amplitudes. The three examples below realize the construction in an infinite discrete Charlier problem, a finite Krawtchouk problem, and a constant-coefficient Chebyshev problem. The Charlier subsection also has a Hermite central-limit endpoint, worked out in appendix D, which shows that the construction is not restricted to pure-point spectra. In these solvable examples, closed generating functions make the shifted amplitudes explicit; in generic many-body systems the input is instead the numerically determined reference moments.

7 Discussion and outlook 

State Krylov complexity is sensitive to the initial vector, even when the Hamiltonian is held fixed. This paper isolates a class of such changes for which the dependence on the seed is completely explicit. If the new seed is obtained by a normalized polynomial filter Q(H)|K0⟩,the only change in the scalar spectral problem is the positive Christoffel reweighting 

dμ 7 → WQdμ . Orthogonality then turns this reweighting into two exact transfer mechanisms: a finite-band connector for individual amplitudes and a finite-rank projected kernel for cumulative probabilities. Consequently, a solved reference Jacobi problem determines the shifted Krylov data for every polynomially related seed, subject only to the finite-depth or terminal-support qualifications described above. The examples above are meant as complementary tests of the same mechanism rather than as a catalogue. The Charlier and Hermite cases check infinite support and continuous scaling; the finite SU (2) chain exposes terminal closure and support loss; the tight-binding chain tests fixed-Hamiltonian localized seeds against the elementary sine-transform calcu-lation. In the lattice case, this distinction is especially useful where varying Nlat changes the Hamiltonian and Hilbert-space dimension, whereas varying |0⟩ → | k⟩ at fixed H(Nlat )tb

The result has a sharp spectral scope because Q(H) is a spectral filter. For a local many-body Hamiltonian, a fixed-degree Q prepares a state in finitely many reference Krylov – 32 – levels, but this property is distinct from spatial locality. A local insertion O| K0⟩ belongs to the construction precisely when it lies in the closure of K(H, |K0⟩); components orthogonal to this cyclic subspace require additional seed data. Numerical reference moments, when available, generate the polynomial seed family through the exact transfer and projector formulae. The propositions hold at every finite seed degree. Root, Gram, projector, terminal-lift determinant, and reduced-Jacobi realizations are equivalent evaluations of the transform, with the latter formulations giving stable implementations at high degrees. If Q vanishes on atoms of the reference measure, the corresponding positive-mass spectral components are removed. In finite support, this is precisely the dimension reduction in eq. (2.22) ; in infinite support, it can remove atoms but not a non-atomic continuum component. Within this polynomial theory, the resulting initial-state dependence has a precise spectral meaning. At fixed H, changing Q leaves the energy levels and their correlations unchanged and reweights only their contribution to the scalar seed measure by |Q(E)|2.Variations in spread complexity across polynomially related seeds can therefore arise from this exact reweighting, even though the underlying Hamiltonian data remain unchanged. This gives a complementary fixed-Hamiltonian comparison problem to the displacement-generated families studied in recent Krylov-complexity work [ 4]. Chaos remains encoded in the reference measure, Jacobi data, and Fourier–OP moments, while the Christoffel calculus governs the relative dependence on the polynomial seed. These data also have a geometric organization. The polynomial Q specifies a finite divisor of spectral zeros; repeated roots are nonreduced points, and the projected-kernel construction removes the corresponding jet functionals from the reference polynomial space. In finite cyclic dimension, the terminal polynomial replaces the affine spectral line by the quotient algebra C[E]/⟨Td⟩, so proper lifts, terminal reduction, and support loss are quotient-level forms of one divisor constraint. In the broader theory of spectral transformations, moment determinants are tau-functions and Darboux–Christoffel steps act naturally on Grassmannian data [ 34 , 36 –38 ]. We use this geometry only for organization, whereas the orthogonal-polynomial formulation is retained throughout because it yields the amplitudes, cumulative probabilities, and shifted Jacobi data directly. The same qualification applies to the integrability language used above. The finite-band and projected-kernel identities hold for any cyclic pair (H, |K0⟩), independently of whether the physical Hamiltonian is integrable. The integrable terminology refers instead to the auxiliary Jacobi and moment problem where polynomial multipliers act as Christoffel/Darboux transformations, and auxiliary deformations of the moment sequence organize the same determinant data as Toda or KP tau-functions [ 21 , 34 , 37 –39 ]. In classical families such as Charlier, Krawtchouk, and Chebyshev this auxiliary structure becomes explicitly solvable. In a generic chaotic many-body system, the same identities remain exact once the reference Jacobi data and Fourier–OP amplitudes are known, but the Toda/Darboux terminology should be read only as a spectral-algebraic organization of the Krylov data. It is not a statement of physical integrability, nor does it imply a closed scalar equation for KQ(t).The relative-filter cocycle of proposition 2.1 fixes the corresponding status of non-polynomial extensions. Reciprocal polynomial factors give Geronimus-type steps, while – 33 – rational and resolvent-dressed seeds belong to the same relative-filter calculus whenever the relevant support-compatibility conditions are satisfied. Thermal filters such as e−βH |K0⟩

generally produce infinite-band transformations rather than the finite-band connectors of the polynomial proposition. Likewise, large-degree limits of the projected kernels lead to Christoffel–Darboux asymptotics. Large-degree and scaling limits should therefore be understood as limits of the connector or projected-kernel data, not as additional closed-form principles for the full complexity. The speed-limit interpretation also has this relative character. The Mandelstam–Tamm bound and its modern variants constrain how fast a given quantum state moves in projective Hilbert space [ 12 , 13 ]. Recent Krylov-space speed-limit work instead bounds the growth of operator complexity using uncertainty relations for the complexity operator and the Liouvillian [ 14 , 15 ]. Here, the exact statement is a transfer statement at a fixed Hamiltonian. For every polynomially shifted seed, the survival amplitude, the energy variance entering the Mandelstam–Tamm speed, the projected-kernel currents, and the complexity-growth profile are finite Christoffel transforms of the reference amplitudes and connector data. The mixed and operator appendices complete this organization from two complementary directions. Mixed preparations in a finite seed window enter through scalar compressions of the matrix-valued parent measure, while physical density-matrix evolution is a Liouville-space problem. In the operator setting this Christoffel calculus applies to polynomial descendants Q(L)O once an inner product makes L self-adjoint; changing the inner product or temperature may require scalar frequency filters, or else matrix-valued or bivariate transition data. A natural next setting is explicitly time-dependent dynamics. Time-dependent Hamil-tonians, moving frames, and Floquet or Sambe-space generators have their own Krylov constructions [ 40 , 41 ]. Extending the Christoffel-transfer viewpoint to those settings would require tracking both the time-dependent Krylov frame and the transformed seed data. This would be the corresponding extension of the fixed-Hamiltonian polynomial theory developed here.

H Liouville-space operator Krylov extension 

We explain how the polynomial seed calculus developed in the main text also applies to operator Krylov complexity. With a fixed operator inner product, the Christoffel seed-transfer construction carries over directly to Liouville space, where operator growth is represented as spread in the auxiliary Hilbert space of operators. The generator is the Liouvillian, and a polynomial change of the initial operator is a polynomial spectral filter of the Liouvillian. The distinction from the state problem is interpretational rather than algebraic. The spectral variable may be a vacuum excitation energy, a thermal transition frequency, or an energy gap, depending on the chosen operator inner product. Changes of operator inner product are therefore also measure changes; the scalar polynomial cases – 54 – are precisely scalar polynomial Christoffel transforms of a previously solved Liouvillian measure. Two standard extensions require separate tools. First, many infinite-temperature operator Krylov chains have vanishing diagonal coefficients because the Liouvillian spectral measure is even under ω 7 → − ω. A polynomial seed with definite parity preserves this evenness, while a generic complex or parity-mixing seed can break it; the shifted operator Krylov chain is then a general Jacobi chain with nonzero diagonal entries. This parity statement is specific to two-sided frequency measures. Second, non-Hermitian generators, such as generic Lindbladians in open-system Krylov problems [ 49 , 50 ], are described by Arnoldi, bi-Lanczos, or biorthogonal polynomial technology unless detailed-balance assumptions render the generator self-adjoint in a suitable inner product.
