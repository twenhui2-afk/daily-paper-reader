# Petersson-Rigid Lattices in a Census of 100 Rank-Three Root Bases 

Weakly harmonic Maass forms from rank-three Lorentzian Kac–Moody Cartan matrices 

Eungang Cho 

Department of Mathematics, Chung-Ang University 

2026-09-02. Sequel to arXiv:2608.19706 (“paper I”). Reproduction archive: Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22257942 (archive v3.5). 

Abstract 

The first paper worked out one family of four lattices in full — |det | = 12, 24, 36 and 72 . This paper generalizes it. We enumerate the 44 symmetrizable rank-three hyperbolic Cartan matrices and their 56 depth-one edits, 100 root bases in all, and compute the obstruction space 𝑆 5/2 (𝜌 𝐿 ) of the 98 that lie within our weight-5/2 budget: 10 vacuous, 34 unobstructed, 54 obstructed [ C ] .The vacuous ten are the lattices Bruinier, Ehlen and Freitag call simple , read in signature (2, 3) . They classified them — there are 15 — and our ten realize five of the fifteen; of the other ten, five need more than three generators and cannot be the discriminant form of a rank-three lattice at all, three fail | det 𝐿| = 2𝑘 2, and two are simply not root bases. Within the rank-three hyperbolic world the simple lattices are exactly the Feingold–Frenkel neighbours of index 𝑘 ≤ 4 .The discriminant group 𝐿 ′/𝐿 carries a finite quadratic form, and the finite group of its isometries acts on the weight-3/2 cusp forms for 𝜌 𝐿 , the bottom antisymmetric rung of the weight tower. We survey the lattices on which that action is absolutely irreducible of dimension at least two, so that the Petersson pairing on that space is pinned down up to a single scalar. The condition alone cuts the census to three: 𝐿 4 of the first paper, and two new ones at |det | 40 and 88 . The three quaternion discriminants that occur are 6, 10 and 22 , the three for which the Shimura curve 𝑋 𝐷 has genus zero. Rigidity uses no quaternion input, so we record this as an observation about the census and not as a characterisation (§5.2). Both invariants of the first paper — the shadow norm ‖Ξ‖ 2 and the Petersson scalar 𝑡 — were single points there. Three rigid lattices instead of one are where their special faces come off: the irrational part of each generalizes, 

‖Ξ‖ 2 against 𝐿(𝑓, 1) where the first paper read 𝐿(𝑓, 2) , and 𝑡 against an elliptic curve’s imaginary period where it read Γ(1/3) . Both numerically, to between 26 and 58 digits (§§5–6).

# 1. Introduction 

1.1 The thesis 

The world in which the Borcherds correction succeeds has been mapped. Gritsenko and Nikulin classified the lattice Weyl vectors in the family 2𝑈 ⊕ ⟨−2𝑡⟩ , Allcock enumerated the reflective lattices of small rank, and the resulting picture — which Lorentzian lattices carry an automorphic product with the expected divisor — is by now a finite, published list. This paper counts the complement. It asks what happens when the correction fails, and finds that the failure is not a defect but an invariant: a weight-5/2 cusp form, canonically attached to the Cartan matrix, whose Hecke decomposition and Petersson norm are arithmetic data of the same kind that the successful cases suppress. The organising discovery is that failure is rare and concentrated . Of the eleven compact-hyperbolic rank-three Cartan matrices — the ones every proper node-deletion of which is of finite type — exactly one has a nonvanishing shadow. And of the 100 root bases, exactly four are Petersson-rigid — three lattices, since 𝐿 4 is counted through each of its two root bases (§4.7): the isometries of the discriminant form act absolutely irreducibly, in dimension at least two, on the weight-3/2 space one rung below the shadow. Those three sit on the three genus-zero Shimura curves — all three of them, and nothing else. Rigidity is a condition on that lower rung and not on the shadow itself, which at one of the three is not even pure (§5.1, §6.1).

# 2. Background 

This section is longer than a background section usually is. The reason is that the objects here — a Cartan matrix, an infinite product, a Maass form, a quaternion order, a curve of genus zero — are not usually seen in one place, and the paper’s content is precisely that they are the same object seen five ways. A reader who already knows all five may skip to §3. Petersson-rigid lattices 5

2.1 Rank three: where the search is finite 

A generalized Cartan matrix of hyperbolic type is one that is indefinite but only just: delete any node of its Dynkin diagram and what remains is of finite or affine type. The condition is severe. In rank 3 there are exactly 44 symmetrizable ones (§3.1); in rank 4 there are still finitely many; by rank 11 there are none at all. At rank 3 the root system is already infinite, with multiplicities growing exponentially and no closed formula. But the Weyl group acts on a Lorentzian lattice of signature (2, 1) , so the associated symmetric space is the upper half-plane, the denominator is a modular object in one variable, and — this is the point — a finite computation can decide things about it. The founding example is Feingold and Frenkel’s, from 1983: the lattice FF = 𝑈 ⊕ ⟨2⟩, 𝑈 = the hyperbolic plane ,

whose Kac–Moody algebra they connected to Siegel modular forms of genus 2. FF will reappear in §3.4: it is exactly the locus where the obstruction of this paper vanishes for trivial reasons.

2.5 Ternary lattices and quaternion orders 

A rank-three quadratic lattice carries a second life. The even Clifford algebra of a ternary quadratic form is a quaternion algebra over ℚ, and the correspondence is an equivalence between ternary quadratic lattices and quaternion orders — Brzezinski, Gross–Lucianovic. So every row of our census has a quater-nion discriminant 𝐷 , the product of the ramified primes. The correspondence also carries a size: the discriminant of a ternary form with even Gram 𝐺 is det (𝐺)/2 , and the even Clifford functor preserves it, so | det 𝐿|/2 is the reduced discriminant of the order, with 𝐷 dividing it [ S ] . 2.6 Known classifications: Gritsenko–Nikulin, Allcock 

Two classification results describe the region where the correction of §2.2 goes through, and this paper is written against them. 

Gritsenko–Nikulin studied the family 

𝑆 𝑡 = 2𝑈 ⊕ ⟨−2𝑡⟩, 𝑡 = 1, 2, 3, … 

and determined for which 𝑡 the associated denominator admits a lattice Weyl vector — that is, for which 

𝑡 the Borcherds correction exists. Small 𝑡 gives the classical quartet Δ5, Δ 2, Δ 1, Δ 1/2 (𝑡 ≤ 4 ), where the obstruction space is zero and there is nothing to obstruct. A further handful of 𝑡 are “miracles”: the obstruction space is nonzero yet the correction still exists. 

Allcock classified the reflective lattices in the relevant range, giving the other half of the map, and 

Bruinier, Ehlen and Freitag exhibit lattices carrying many Borcherds products at once. §3.4 uses their Table 8 substantively: it matches our ten vacuous root bases against their fifteen entries through Petersson-rigid lattices 8

the genus symbol, and their classification is what closes the sharpness statement above 𝑘 = 4 . That table is quoted, not re-derived [ Gate ] .Together these are a finite, published list of the successes. This paper does not compete with them: it takes their region as given and counts the complement. The census meets their region twice, and both meetings are results rather than restatements. The vacuous locus of Theorem A is exactly the Feingold–Frenkel neighbourhood of §2.1, and nine of its ten root bases — four of its five lattice classes — lie outside the classical quartet (§3.4). And the chain that is this paper’s subject sits at the determinants 2𝑡 for 𝑡 = 6, 12, 18, 36 , two of which are miracles. Rigidity never occurs on that family at all, for a reason of group theory recorded in §5.1. 

A limitation. We cannot reproduce, with our machinery, the assertion that 𝜆 = 0 at the Gritsenko– Nikulin miracles. The charts 𝑈 ⊕ ⟨2𝑡⟩ have no simple-root menu, so our reflective menu sweeps every reflective class while their prescription selects specific divisors. Our 𝜆 for a chart is not their 𝜆 .Nothing in this paper compares the two sides at the level of 𝜆 , and any statement of the form “ 𝜆 = 0 

on the split side” would be outside our evidence.

4.1 Why this chain 

Three reasons, in increasing order of interest. It is the only compact-hyperbolic chain with a failure (Theorem B). Of the four chains in the strongest-finiteness part of the census, this is the one where the Borcherds correction breaks, and it breaks at exactly one of its four members. It is entirely in the core . All four Gram matrices are genuine hyperbolic Cartan matrices, inside the canonically defined 44. The scope caveat of §3.1 — the one real weakness of this census — does not touch anything in this section. Whatever is proved here is proved about a closed set. And it is a filtration, not a list . Its four members have determinants 12, 24, 36, 72 ; their discriminant groups grow in a controlled way — the 3-part squares between 24 and 72 and the 2-part goes non-cyclic at every even step (§4.3), though they do not nest, since 𝐷(12) = ℤ/4 × ℤ/3 has an element of order 4

# 5. Petersson-rigid lattices 

5.1 Two conditions 

Let 𝐿 be a lattice in the census. The rigidity condition below is a property of the lattice alone and does not mention 𝜆 ; we therefore impose no obstruction hypothesis here, and §5.2 records what that buys. 

Definition (rigidity). 𝐿 is Petersson-rigid if the 𝑂(𝐷(𝐿), 𝑞) -representation on 𝑆 3/2 (𝜌 𝐿 ) — the 

bottom antisymmetric rung — is absolutely irreducible of dimension at least two. 

Definition (selection). 𝐿 is selected if the discriminant group 𝐷(𝐿) is non-cyclic, its automor-phism group 𝑂(𝐷(𝐿), 𝑞) is non-abelian, and dim 𝑆 𝑂 5/2 (𝜌 𝐿 ) = 1 .The two conditions live on opposite parities of the weight tower (§4.5). Selection is a condition on the symmetric rung 𝑆 5/2 , where the shadow lives and where dim 𝑆 𝑂 is the meaningful quantity. Rigidity is a condition on the antisymmetric rung 𝑆 3/2 , where 𝑆 𝑂 = 0 structurally and no shadow can exist. So the checklist is not two tests of one space; it is one test on each side of the parity. That also explains the shape of the two definitions — on the symmetric side we ask how small the invariant part is, on the antisymmetric side we ask how indecomposable the whole space is, because on that side there is no invariant part to measure. 

Both conditions are bottom-rung phenomena, and for the same reason. Each parity is a whole tower — 5/2, 9/2, 13/2, … on one side, 3/2, 7/2, 11/2, … on the other — so writing 𝑆 5/2 in the one and

6.1 The chain (1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 4) 

𝐷 = 22 occupies a single chain with two members, |det | 44 and |det | 88 ; both are rows of the census table (§3.3) and every number in this section is one of its columns. # Appendix B — the menu convention, and a note on reproducing 

𝜆 depends on which reflective classes are prescribed. Three conventions occur in the working literature of this project: the Gram diagonal with multiplicity ( dup ), the Gram diagonal deduplicated ( ded ), and the reflective menu refl(G) obtained by enumerating primitive 𝑣 with 𝑛 = 𝑣 𝑇 𝐺𝑣 > 0 and 2 gcd (𝐺𝑣) ≡ 0( mod 𝑛) .
