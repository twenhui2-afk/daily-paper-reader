ON THE CLASSIFICATION OF SMALL CYCLOTOMIC INTEGERS 

JITENDRA BAJPAI, SRIJAN DAS, KIRAN S. KEDLAYA, AND JORGE MELLO 

Abstract. We give a general classification theorem for cyclotomic algebraic integers with all complex absolute values bounded by a fixed constant c, modeled on the theorem of Cassels which treats the case c = √5 up to finitely many exceptions. As a corollary, we establish that the range of the function taking a cyclotomic integer to its maximum complex absolute value is a well-ordered (but not closed) subset of the real numbers. We also formulate analogous statements for algebraic numbers in the maximal cyclotomic extension of a fixed number field. The proofs combine a result of Loxton, which bounds the number of roots of unity in the shortest additive representation of a cyclotomic integer in terms of the maximum complex absolute value, with an equidistribution theorem of Bilu et al. for Galois orbits of torsion points on algebraic tori.

The problem of classifying cyclotomic algebraic integers which are small (i.e., which have small absolute value under any embedding into C) was originally raised by Robinson [12] and subsequently studied in detail by numerous authors, notably including Cassels [4]. In this paper, we describe a general form for the classification of small cyclotomic integers, modeled on a slightly modified formulation of [4, Theorem I]. To give this formulation, we must introduce some notation. For any number field K,we denote by Kcycl its cyclotomic closure (i.e., the compositum with the maximal abelian extension of Q). For a given algebraic integer α, we define the house of α, denoted α , to be the maximum of |β| as β varies over conjugates of α in C; this is evidently invariant under multiplication by a root of unity. It is often more convenient to work with the quantity α 2; following [1], we refer to this as the castle of α.In this notation, [4, Theorem I] can be stated as follows. 

Theorem 1.1 (Cassels) . For α a cyclotomic algebraic integer in Qcycl , α 2 < 5.01 if and only if α is a root of unity times one of the following.

In this paper, we consider what happens when the constant 5 .01 is replaced by an arbitrary positive real number. To state our first result, we let μ ⊂ Qcycl denote the group of roots of unity. 

Theorem 1.2. For any real number C > 0, the set of cyclotomic integers α with α 2 ≤ C

can be written in the form 

{Pi(ζ1, . . . , ζ ni ) : i ∈ I; ζ1, . . . , ζ ni ∈ μ}

for some finite family {Pi(z1, . . . , z ni )}i∈I of polynomials Pi with cyclotomic integer coeffi-cients. 

We also establish a “semicontinuity” theorem generalizing the following result of Robinson–Wurtz [11]: the function α 7 → α 2 on cyclotomic integers takes no values in the range (5 , 5.01). 

Theorem 1.3. For any real number C > 0, there exists ϵ > 0 with the property that there is no cyclotomic integer α with C < α 2 < C + ϵ.

An equivalent formulation of Theorem 1.3 is that the range of the function α 7 → α 2 on cyclotomic integers is a well-ordered subset of R: every infinite nonincreasing sequence valued in this range is eventually constant. For comparison, we note that this range is not a closed subset of R: whereas every element of the range is itself a (totally real, totally positive) cyclotomic integer, Calegari [3] observed that 97 + 26 √13 27 ≈ 7.064604 is a limit point of the range which is visibly not a cyclotomic integer. (Finding the smallest such limit point will be the topic of subsequent work.) The proofs of Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 depend on two key inputs: a theorem of Loxton [9] showing that a cyclotomic integer of bounded castle can be written as a sum of a bounded number of roots of unity, and an equidistribution theorem of Yuan [13] concerning Galois orbits of torsion points on tori, generalizing an earlier result of Bilu [2]. (We note a strong resemblance with the argument given in [2, §5] to recover Laurent’s theorem classifying algebraic subvarieties of tori in which the torsion points are Zariski dense [8].) Using similar ingredients, we also obtain similar results about algebraic integers in Kcycl 

for any number field K. These generalizations are naturally stated and proved in terms of 

S-integers in Kcycl where S is an arbitrary finite set of places of K; see Theorem 5.6 and Theorem 5.7. Although we have not included any statements about effectivity in our main results, for any fixed bound the resulting classification is effectively computable. 3. Equidistribution of Galois orbits on tori 

We next introduce an equidistribution theorem for Galois orbits in algebraic tori. This requires a few definitions in order to formulate the result. 

Definition 3.1. A sequence {αk} of points in (Q∗)N is strict if any proper algebraic subgroup of (Q∗)N contains αk for only finitely many values of k.

In connection with the previous definition, we make the following observation. 

Lemma 3.2. Let T ∼= GNm be an algebraic torus. The set of proper closed algebraic subgroups of T is countable. Proof. Let H ⊊ T be a proper closed algebraic subgroup, and let H◦ denote its identity component. Since H is an algebraic variety, it has finitely many connected components, and so H is a finite disjoint union of cosets of H◦. Since T is a commutative algebraic group consisting of semisimple elements, any connected closed subgroup is again a torus; in particular, H◦ is a subtorus of T .We show that there are only countably many such subgroups H. First, any subtorus 

H◦ ⊆ T is uniquely determined by its annihilator in the character lattice X(T ) ∼= ZN .Since ZN is a finitely generated module over the noetherian ring Z, it has only countably many subgroups. Thus there are only countably many possibilities for H◦.Next, fix such an H◦ and consider the possible cosets. The group H/H ◦ is finite, so each coset has finite order. Moreover, H◦, being a torus over an algebraically closed field, is divisible. Therefore any coset can be represented by a torsion point of T (Q): if x ∈ T (Q)is such that xH ◦ has order m, then xm ∈ H◦, and by divisibility there exists y ∈ H◦(Q)such that ym = xm; then ζ = xy −1 satisfies ζm = 1 and represents the same coset. Finally, the torsion points of T (Q) correspond to elements of μN . Since μ is countable, it follows that the set of torsion points of T is countable. Hence there are only countably many possibilities for the cosets, and therefore only countably many such subgroups 

H. □

5. Nonarchimedean analogue 

Throughout this section, fix a number field K. We next formulate a more flexible setup to remedy the issue raised in Theorem 2.3. The idea is to put the nonarchimedean places of K on an equal footing with the archimedean places, and use a similar strategy to replace polynomials that do not always evaluate to K-cyclotomic integers. 

Definition 5.1. Let C = ( Cv)v be a tuple of real numbers indexed by all places of K. We say a tuple C = ( Cv)v is valid if Cv > 0 for all v and Cv = 1 for all but finitely many v.For a valid tuple C, we say an element α ∈ Kcycl is C-bounded if, for every place v of K

and every place w of Kcycl above v, we have |α|w ≤ Cv.This definition relates to previous ones as follows. Take K = Q and let C be a tuple with Cv = c for v archimedean and Cv = 1 for v nonarchimedean. Then α ∈ Qcycl is

6. Remarks on effectivity 

As noted in the introduction, we have not formulated any of our results in an effective manner. We now justify the claim that they can all be made effective, in the following senses. 

• In Theorem 1.2, there is an algorithm that, upon input of C, returns a finite family of polynomials as indicated. 

• Similarly, in Theorem 5.6, there is an algorithm that, upon input of K and the 

Cv, returns a finite family of polynomials as indicated. 

• In Theorem 1.3, there is an algorithm that, upon input of C, returns a suitable value of ϵ and a proof certificate. 

• Similarly, in Theorem 5.7, there is an algorithm that, upon input of K and the 

Cv, returns a suitable value of ϵ and a proof certificate. All of these claims boil down to two essential points. The first is that there exists an explicit choice for the Loxton function L in Theorem 2.1 (as this then leads to an effective construction in Theorem 2.2 as indicated in [7]). The proof in [9] does not explicitly report such a function, but one can trace through the steps in the proof to obtain one. The second point is that the replacement step in Theorem 4.4 and Theorem 5.5 can be made effective.
