THE GEOMETRY OF SOLUTIONS TO THE GENERAL SEXTIC 

SIDHANTH RAMAN 

Abstract. We study the geometry of the three simplest known formulas for roots of a general sextic polynomial. In contrast with work of Green on the quintic, we show that no “simultaneous uniformization” of the normal forms associated to the solutions exists. This demonstrates an intrin-sic qualitative complexity of sextic equations that is not present in quintic equations. Additionally, we give a formula for the roots of a general sextic in terms of Hilbert modular cusp forms and the uniformizing differential equation of the associated Hilbert modular surface. 

1. Introduction 

In this article, we study the geometry of three projective surfaces endowed with a faithful A6-action. Our interest in these varieties stems from their role in the 19th century theory of formulas: they are central to the three simplest known algebraic formulas for the roots of a general sextic polynomial. Let σk denote the kth elementary symmetric polynomial in 6 variables. The projective surfaces are defined as follows: (1) The Hamilton surface is the complete intersection VHam = Z(σ1, σ 2, σ 3) ⊂ P5.(2) The Klein projective plane is VKle = P2.(3) The modular surface is the complete intersection VMod = Z(σ1, σ 2, σ 4) ⊂ P5.1

In Section 3, we will see that VHam is a K3 surface and that VMod is a surface of general type. Since they are defined using symmetric polynomials, the Hamilton and modular surfaces come equipped with a natural A6-action given by permuting coordinates on each surface. 2 The A6-action on P2, called the Valentiner action , is more subtle to define — we give explicit generators for this action in Section 2.4. One of our main results is concerned with the existence of A6-equivariant maps between these surfaces: 

Theorem 1.1 (Nonexistence of equivariant maps) . There are no A6-equivariant generically finite dominant rational maps between any of the projective A6-surfaces VHam , VKle , or VMod .

Remark 1.2. The nonexistence of any dominant rational map P2 → VHam , P2 → VMod , or VHam →

VMod is elementary to see: Kodaira dimension κ is non-increasing under dominant morphisms between surfaces, and the results of Section 3 imply that κ(VMod ) = 2, κ(VHam ) = 0, and κ(VKle ) = 

−∞ . To prove Theorem 1.1, it suffices to obstruct the existence of equivariant maps between these surfaces when the Kodaira dimension drops. To prove Theorem 1.1, we will apply tools from Hodge theory, A6-representation theory, and equivariant birational geometry. When we interpreted this statement in the context of formulas for the roots of the general sextic, we found Theorem 1.1 rather surprising. To provide further context and motivation, we now discuss previous work of Green on the quintic [Gre1978] which inspired this article.

1.1. Formulas for the roots of a quintic. The Abel–Ruffini theorem shows that the general quintic polynomial cannot be solved by radicals. However, there are two known formulas for the roots of the general quintic in algebraic functions of one variable: Bring solvably reduced a general quintic to a single variable form using Tschirnhaus transformations [Bri1786; Jer1858], and Klein leveraged the icosahedral action of A5 on P1 to reduce the general quintic to a form which could be solved using the single variable icosahedral function [Kle1884; Mor1956]. Associated to each formula is its normal form — we postpone formalizing the notions of a formula and its normal form until Definitions 2.2 and 2.3. One can informally think of a normal form as the variety whose closed points are in bijective correspondence with sets of ordered roots obtained using the chosen formula. Klein found, and Green reproduced, a 3-sheeted A5-equivariant dominant rational map from the Bring normal form VBri (a genus 4 curve) to the Klein normal form (the projective line P1), thereby inducing a map from Bring’s solution to Klein’s solution [Gre1978]. Moreover, Green uniformized both VBri and P1 by the upper half plane H2, recovering Hermite’s analytic solution to the quintic by elliptic modular functions [Her1858]. Concretely, this 3-sheeted A5-equivariant map found by Klein and Green gives an algebraic transformation that converts Bring’s formula for the roots of a quintic into Klein’s formula. 1.2. Formulas for the roots of a sextic. Moving up one degree, there are three known solutions to the general sextic polynomial: (1) Hamilton provided a straightforward generalization of Bring’s quintic solution to the sextic case using Tschirnhaus transformations [Ham1836]. This formula’s normal form is the surface VHam .(2) Klein outlined a generalization of his method for solving quintics to the sextic case by replacing the icosahedral A5-action on P1 with the exceptional Valentiner A6-action on P2

[Kle1905; Sut2019]. This formula’s normal form is the surface VKle .(3) Quite recently, Farb, Kisin, and Wolfson gave an alternate solution to the general sextic using Tschirnhaus transformations that differ from Hamilton [FKW2023]; we call this the 

modular solution . This formula’s normal form is the surface VMod .These surfaces are used to build formulas for the roots of sextics out of algebraic functions of at most 2 variables. We give a modern overview of each formula in Section 2. In light of Green’s work on the quintic, it is natural to ask how these normal forms, and their associated formulas for the roots of sextics, compare and relate to each other. We reformulate Theorem 1.1 in the context of solutions to the general sextic, and give its restatement below: 

Theorem 1.3 (Incompatibility of sextic solutions) . There are no A6-equivariant generically finite dominant rational maps between any of the normal forms defining solutions to the general sextic polynomial. Thus there is no algebraic transformation from one formula for the roots of a sextic into any other formula. 

We now describe the content of each section of this paper. In Section 2, we give an overview of the theory of Tschirnhaus transformations, and formally define algebraic formulas and their normal forms. We also describe each solution to the general sextic, and the tower of operations required to define their respective formulas. In Section 3, we discuss the geometry of each formula’s normal form, and then we obstruct the existence of equivariant maps between them in Section 4. In Section 5, we give a coda to Green’s uniformization of the Bring and Klein normal forms by explaining how one can use the uniformization of VMod by H2 × H2 to analytically solve sextic polynomials using Hilbert modular forms. This expands on prior work of Farb–Kisin–Wolfson [FKW2023, Proposition 4.14 (3)], and offers a formula for the roots of a general sextic in terms of the uniformizing differential equation of the Hilbert modular surface VMod .

2.3. The quintic and the icosahedron. Before we describe Klein’s approach to solving sextics, it will be helpful to first sketch his solution to quintics, which the icosahedron is at the center of. Adjoining the square root of the discriminant √∆ of a general quintic reduces the Galois group from the symmetric group S5 to the alternating group A5. The icosahedron, which has isometry

group isomorphic to A5, can be inscribed in the projective line P1. This induces the icosahedral 

A5-action on P1, and defines a branched cover P1 → P1/A 5. Klein calculated the ring of invariants of this A5-action on P1 to prove the isomorphism of varieties P1/A 5 ∼= P1; this quotient is given by a degree 60 rational map I([ z0 : z1]) called the icosahedral function .The key idea of Klein is that the general quintic can be reduced to a normal form for which inverting the icosahedral function I allows one to calculate all the roots of the quintic. To execute this proposed formula, one needs to extract an accessory square root, and to determine the correct invariants on P1 associated to the quintic needed in order to solve for the roots of the quintic; the latter was accomplished by Klein when he calculated the ring of invariants. O. Nash gives a nice modern exposition of Klein’s solution to the quintic, including how to produce the icosahedral invariant Z attached to a given quintic [Nas2014]. 4. Obstructing maps of formulas via geometry 

We now come to the heart of this work, showing that the three simplest known formulas for the roots of sextics are fundamentally incompatible: concretely, we will prove Theorem 1.3, showing that there are no equivariant branched covers between any of their normal forms. Before obstructing the existence of maps that relate the distinct solutions of the sextics, we begin with some generalities that will apply to our proofs. In what follows, we will often trade between the notations VHam =

τ1,2,3, VMod = τ1,2,4, and VKle = P2, depending on whether we want to emphasize the surface’s role as a normal form or its construction as a complete intersection in the appropriate projective space. 

Lemma 4.1. If there were an A6-equivariant dominant rational map between any of the normal forms VHam , VMod , or VKle as Kn-varieties, there would be an induced A6-equivariant map of C-varieties. Proof. This is a simple consequence of base change: given any field extension K ⊆ L and a K-scheme X, let XL := XK ×Spec( K) Spec( L). Any morphism φK : XK → YK of schemes over K

induces a unique morphism fL : XL → YL of schemes over L by the universal property of fiber products. Given such a morphism of normal forms over Kn, it would induce a unique morphism over the algebraic closure Kn ∼= C. To briefly explain the last (non-canonical) isomorphism, recall that for a fixed characteristic, there is exactly one model up to isomorphism for an algebraically closed field of a given uncountable cardinality [Ste1910]. Thus any characteristic 0 algebraically closed field of cardinality 2 ℵ0 is isomorphic to the complex numbers. □

To obstruct any connections between the three algebraic formulas for roots of sextics, we must prove that no A6-equivariant maps between their normal forms exist. By Lemma 4.1, it suffices to obstruct A6-equivariant branched covers between these varieties over the complex numbers.
