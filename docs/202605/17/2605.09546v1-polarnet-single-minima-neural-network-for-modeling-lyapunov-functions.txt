# Abstract 

Learning control strategies with provable stability guarantees continues to be a challenging problem. In this work, we examine a family of training-time behaviors exhibited by existing neural Lyapunov control methods under specific conditions, which can hinder the synthesis of a provably stable controller. We identify the root cause as the lack of neural network architectural guarantees on the learned Lyapunov function, and propose PolarNet, a network architecture that provably addresses these issues by structurally guarantee to have a single critical point. We provide theoretical guarantee regarding the properness and universality of PolarNet for modeling Lyapunov functions, and show that using it as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods can effectively circumvent particular difficulties in training. We conduct a set of numerical experiments to verify that PolarNet consistently maintains a single critical point and, when used as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, successfully avoids training failures caused by the lack of architectural guarantees. The code of this paper is available at https://github.com/23-zy/PolarNet .

# 1 Introduction 

Lyapunov functions are a cornerstone of modern control theory, serving as the primary tool for analyzing the stability of dynamic systems modeled in state space. Despite their near-dominant role, the search for a Lyapunov function has, for over a century, relied heavily on the physical insights and mathematical ingenuity of domain experts. Constructing such functions remains a longstanding open problem in both control theory and mathematics [ 1 ]. Although various analytical methods have been developed within the control community to assist in constructing Lyapunov functions, such as solving the algebraic Riccati equation [ 13 ] and sum-of-squares programming [ 17 ], none of these approaches are universally applicable. Even for relatively simple systems, these methods may fail to produce a valid Lyapunov function or may incorrectly conclude that no such function exists. Recent research (e.g., [ 4 , 14 , 21 –24 ]) has explored an intriguing learning-based alternative that automatically synthesizes provably stable controllers by jointly learning a control strategy and a Lyapunov function, with the latter serving as the stability certificate. Specifically, by embedding the Lyapunov conditions into the learning objective, these methods, collectively known as neural Lyapunov control, are applicable to complex uncertain nonlinear systems without sacrificing a theoretical stability guarantee. This line of work has achieved remarkable empirical success on a wide range of challenging control tasks, demonstrating a promising direction beyond conventional analytical approaches.

However, the reliance on flexible neural networks to learn Lyapunov functions introduces a new challenge that is absent in traditional analytical methods. We identify a family of training-time behaviors that can arise under specific conditions in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, that can potentially prevent the learning process from properly converging and thus blocking the synthesis of a provably stable controller. We trace this issue to the absence of a suitable structural guarantee in the neural networks used to learn Lyapunov functions. Specifically, we show that for a certain class of general nonlinear systems, any valid Lyapunov function must possess exactly one critical point, yet the network architectures commonly adopted in existing methods do not enforce this property structurally. This issue is specific to learning-based paradigms and has therefore been overlooked in the recent shift from analytical methods to learning-based controller synthesis. To fill this gap, we propose PolarNet, a neural network architecture that provides a rigorous structural guarantee by exploiting an interesting mathematical fact. A key theoretical result is that PolarNet always possesses exactly one critical point, thereby circumventing the training difficulty described above. Furthermore, we show that under a mild condition, PolarNet is capable of constructing Lyapunov functions for any asymptotically stable equilibrium of general dynamic systems. We validate the effectiveness of PolarNet through numerical experiments on both synthetic function approximation tasks and a challenging nonlinear control problem. The results demonstrate that PolarNet consistently maintains a single critical point and, when used as a drop-in replacement in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, successfully avoids training failures caused by the lack of architectural guarantees. To the best of our knowledge, PolarNet is the first architecture that structurally enforces the single-critical-point property for Lyapunov function learning, offering a principled and practical solution to a previously overlooked failure mode in neural Lyapunov control.

2.1 Problem formulation 

Consider the following general dynamic system, which is an ordinary differential equation defined as: 

˙x = f (x, u ), x(t0) = x0, (1) where x ∈ X ⊂ Rm is the system state, u ∈ U ⊂ Rn is the control input, ˙x denotes the derivative of x w.r.t. t, and f : Rm × Rn → Rm is an unknown nonlinear function that is locally Lipschitz continuous in X . Suppose the system (1) is controllable, and xe ∈ X is an equilibrium point of (1) ,that is, f (xe, 0) = 0 . The goal of the task is to synthesize an appropriate input u(x), such that the closed-loop system is asymptotically stable in the sense of Definition 1. 

Definition 1 (Asymptotic stability [ 8 ]) . Let xe be an equilibrium point of the closed-loop autonomous system ˙x = f (x, u (x)) . Then xe is said to be asymptotically stable if there exists δ > 0 such that for every initial condition satisfying ∥x0 − xe∥ < δ , we have lim t→∞ x(t) = xe.Lyapunov functions V (x) : Rm → R are special functions whose existence imply that a dynamic system is asymptotically stable. Definition 2 (Lyapunov stability criteria [ 8]) . Without loss of generality, let x = 0 be an equilibrium point of ˙x = f (x, u (x)) . Suppose V : Rm → R is a radially unbounded C1 function satisfying: 

i) V (0) = 0 , ii ) V (x) > 0, ∀ x̸ = 0 , and ˙V (x) < 0, ∀ x̸ = 0 , then x = 0 is asymptotically stable. In this case, V (x) is called a Lyapunov function of this system. Throughout this work, we consider the case where xe = 0 is the only desired stable equilibrium. Recent research (e.g., [ 4, 21 , 22 ]) has found that, by directly learning to satisfy the Lyapunov stability criteria, it is possible to synthesize a controller u that stabilizes a general dynamic system. The general idea is to parameterize the controller and a corresponding Lyapunov function with two neural networks uθ and Vθ , and to train them simultaneously by minimizing the empirical risk of violating the Lyapunov stability criteria. If at x = P (or x = Q), the learned controller satisfies uθ = 0 , then ˙x = 0 and hence the system trajectory will remain at x(t) = P (or x(t) = Q), and the true equilibrium x = 0 is not globally asymptotically stable, which hinders the successful synthesis of a provably stable controller. An experimental demonstration of such a scenario is provided in Section 4.3. Existing methods adopt various strategies to mitigate this issue. One common approach adds a small positive constant ϵ to the loss term in (5), making it non-zero unless ˙Vθ < −ϵ, thereby expanding 3the region where the loss provides useful gradients [ 21 , 22 ]. Another approach actively seeks out points xi̸ = 0 where ˙Vθ (xi) ≥ 0 via numerical optimization and re-construct the training set to include these points, allowing the model to focus on eliminating these violations [ 4]. Similarly, a third strategy simulates closed-loop trajectories to assess the stability of initial states, constructing the training set around the current region of attraction (RoA) to maintain and gradually expand it during training [ 14 , 21 ]. While these strategies may alleviate the issue during training, they do not completely eliminate it. We demonstrate this with an experiment in Section 4.3. To the best of our knowledge, no existing method fundamentally resolves this issue by enforcing a network structural guarantee on the learned Lyapunov function itself. It should also be noted that this issue is unique to learning-based methods that use flexible neural networks to model Lyapunov function, and is not present in analytic methods [8]. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 Network architecture design 

To address the issue described in Section 2.2, we propose PolarNet, a network architecture that guarantees a single-critical-point shape in addition to positive-definiteness. By construction, this architecture strictly enforces (4) to be true, regardless of how its parameters are set. Formally speaking, PolarNet models a single pole function, which we define as: 

Definition 3 (Single pole function) . A single pole function is a function V (x) : X → R, X ⊂ 

Rm, 0 ∈ X , such that it: 

i) is C∞ smooth ∀x ∈ X ;

ii ) is positive definite: V (0) = 0 ; V (x) > 0, ∀x̸ = 0 ;

iii ) has exactly one critical point at x = 0 : ∇V̸ = 0 , ∀x̸ = 0 ;

iv ) is proper on X , i.e., every sublevel set {x ∈ X | V (x) ≤ c} of V is compact. 1

We additionally show in Section 3.2 that, there exists a Lyapunov function, which is also a single pole function, for the equilibrium of the general dynamic system (1). We first reveal an interesting connection between a single pole function and a smoothly invertible mapping Ψ( x) : Rm → Rm. Exploiting this connection, by integrating invertible neural networks (i.e., neural networks that are invertible by design, e.g., [ 5, 10 , 11 , 18 ]) into the model architecture, we can naturally construct a neural network that guarantees to be a single pole function. We prove that, under a mild condition, any single pole function can be expressed in the form 

V = ∥Ψ( x)∥2, where Ψ( x) : Rm → Rm is a C∞ smooth, invertible and zero-crossing function, and 

∥ · ∥ is the standard Euclidean norm. Geometrically, this means V can always be smoothly deformed to match an Euclidean ball ∥ · ∥ 2, as illustrated in Fig. 2:

3.2 Theoretical guarantee 

Theorem 1 has shown that all single pole functions V (x) can be formulated as V (x) =

∥Ψ( x)∥2, ∀x ∈ X \ { 0}. As has been proved in [ 20 ], the affine coupling flow architecture is a universal approximator of C2 diffeomorphisms in Rm. In other words, all C2 differentiably invertible mappings in Rm can be approximated by the affine coupling flow architecture to arbitrary precision. And by restricting all components of this architecture to be C∞, we can obtain a universal approxima-tor for Ψ. Hence, a single pole function can always be approximated by Vθ . Now we also show that 

Vθ (x) = ∥Ψ( x)∥2 is always a single pole function, regardless of how its parameters are set (as long as they are finite): 

Theorem 2. If Ψ : Rm → Rm is a C∞ smooth, zero-crossing invertible mapping, then ∥Ψ( x)∥2 is a single pole function. 

Proof. We prove for each property: 

i) Since both ∥ · ∥ 2 and Ψ are C∞ smooth, their composition ∥Ψ( ·)∥2 is also C∞ smooth. 

ii ) Invertibility of Ψ implies that Ψ( x)̸ = Ψ(0) , ∀x̸ = 0 , hence Ψ( x)̸ = Ψ(0) = 0 , ∀x̸ = 0 . By definition of the squared norm ∥ · ∥ 2, we have ∥Ψ( x)∥2̸ = 0 , ∀x̸ = 0 . Since ∥ · ∥ 2 ≥ 0, ∥Ψ( x)∥2 is positive definite. 5iii ) Since Ψ is invertible, it has no critical point. ∥ · ∥ 2 has exactly one critical point at 0, Ψ( x) = 0 

only at x = 0 . By the chain rule of derivative computation, ∥Ψ( x)∥2 has exactly one critical point at 

x = 0 .

iv ) N (y) = ∥y∥2 is proper on Rm (with codomain [0 , +∞)), hence for any compact set K ⊂

[0 , +∞), its preimage N −1(K) is also compact. Invertibility of Ψ implies continuity of Ψ−1, and the continuous image of any compact set is also a compact set. Thus Ψ−1(N −1(K)) is also compact. Hence ∥Ψ( x)∥2 is proper on Rm ⊃ X .We have thus shown that ∥Ψ( x)∥2 is always a single pole function. We also show that, for all asymptotically stable equilibrium of a general dynamic system (1), there exists a single pole function V , such that V is a Lyapunov function of this equilibrium: 

Theorem 3. Let x = 0 be an asymptotically stable equilibrium of a dynamic system ˙x = f (x, u (x)) ,where f (x, u (x)) is locally Lipschitz. Let RA ∈ X be the region of attraction of x = 0 . Then there exists a Lyapunov function V (x) for this equilibrium, that is also a single pole function. 

Proof. By Kurzweil’s converse Lyapunov theorem ( Theorem 4.17 in [ 8]), and by the conclusion shown in (4), for an asymptotically stable equilibrium x = 0 of a dynamic system, there exists a Lyapunov function V (x) : X → R that is: i) C∞ smooth, ii ) positive definite, iii ) has exactly one critical point at x = 0 , and iv ) satisfies ∀c > 0, {x ∈ X | V (x) ≤ c} is compact, i.e., V (x) is proper in RA. In other words, it is a single pole function (By Definition 3, letting X = RA, one can conclude that V is a single pole function). Theorem 3 shows that for any controller synthesis task demanding an asymptotically stable equilib-rium, there exists a Lyapunov function that can be accurately modeled by PolarNet. Combining with Theorem 2, this provides a principled method to circumvent training difficulties caused by the lack of architectural guarantees in existing neural Lyapunov control methods.

# 5 Limitations and future directions 

The theoretical guarantee provided by our work assumes a locally Lipschitz dynamic system. When the system is not Lipschitz, PolarNet may still be applied, but does not guarantee in general that it can always represent a valid Lyapunov function for the system (i.e., Theorem 3 does not hold in this case). Future works may seek to generalize PolarNet to work with a broader continuum of dynamic systems. Our method also only focus on continuous time dynamic system and stability in the Lyapunov sense, while discrete systems and other stability definitions are also of interest in many cases. Our work does not consider the case in discrete systems and stability definitions other than the Lyapunov stability. It should also be noted that PolarNet is specifically designed for controller synthesis task with a single desired equilibrium. For out-of-scope tasks such as finding stability certificate of existing dynamic systems [ 3 ], PolarNet does not have clear advantages over existing architectures, and may even be less expressive if the intention is to find multiple stable equilibriums. # 6 Related works 

Neural Lyapunov control methods follow a common scheme: they simultaneously learn a controller 

uθ and its Lyapunov function Vθ , thereby synthesizing a controller with provable stability guarantees. Several works ([ 4, 23 , 24 ]) adopt a learner-falsifier architecture, where a falsifier actively searches the state space for points where Lyapunov stability criteria are violated. These points, termed counterexamples, are added to the training set for learning uθ and Vθ . The learner then minimizes the empirical risk of Lyapunov stability criteria violation on this training set by using gradient descent to update uθ and Vθ . When no counterexample can be found, the synthesized controller uθ is considered stable, and Vθ is a valid Lyapunov function certifying its stability. Among these methods, [ 4, 24 ]formulate the counterexample generation problem as a satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) [ 2]problem and solve it using numerical SMT solvers. In contrast, [ 23 ] propose a projected gradient descent (PGD) algorithm to approximately find counterexamples, improving the training efficiency. Some other methods, such as [ 14 , 21 ], deviate from this learner-falsifier architecture. They iteratively evaluate the region of attraction (RoA) of uθ during training and construct training sets that cover the current RoA and a surrounding region, thereby maintaining a contiguous RoA and gradually expanding it. Among these, [ 14 ] determines the RoA solely by checking the Lyapunov stability criteria with the current uθ and Vθ , whereas [ 21 ] additionally collects numerically simulated state trajectories to assess the stability of initial states more reliably. Most of these methods target continuous-time dynamical systems, while [ 23 ] apply neural Lyapunov control to discrete-time systems. [ 22 ] propose a different approach that trains uθ and Vθ on a finite grid over the state space without constructing more sophisticated training sets, and report empirical success. [ 6, 21 , 22 ] use neural networks that are positive definite by construction to model Vθ , eliminating the need for an 9additional loss term that enforces positive definiteness. To the best of our knowledge, PolarNet is the first architecture that structurally enforces the single-critical-point property for Lyapunov function learning. 

# 7 Conclusion 

In this work, we analyzed the cause of a family of training-time difficulties that may present in existing neural Lyapunov control methods, identifying their root cause as lack of neural network architectural guarantees, and propose a new network architecture, PolarNet, that addresses this issue by construction. We derived theoretical guarantees that PolarNet possess the desired mathematical properties by construction, and can be applied to all asymptotically stable equilibrium of locally Lips-chitz dynamic systems. We then verified the effectiveness of PolarNet with numerical experiments, demonstrating that PolarNet indeed possesses the desired properties in practice, and can circumvent certain difficulties encountered in the training process of existing neural Lyapunov control method, as we have analyzed. We also noted on the theoretical assumptions we made. Future works may seek to generalize PolarNet to be provably effective in a wider range of scenarios.
