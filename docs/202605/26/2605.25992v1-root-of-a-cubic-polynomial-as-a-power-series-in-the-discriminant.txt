ROOT OF A CUBIC POLYNOMIAL AS A POWER SERIES IN THE DISCRIMINANT 

JASON BLAND, SKIP GARIBALDI , AND JOEL ROSENBERG 

Abstract. An observation by J-P. Serre implies that cubic polynomials are unique among generic monic polynomials of degree 2 or higher in that they have a root that is a power series in the discriminant of the polynomial. We provide formulas for this root of a cubic that work in any characteristic. In the special case of a cubic real polynomial with positive discriminant, the series converges and therefore provides an explicit formula for a root; when that polynomial is depressed, the root we provide is the longest root. The proofs are a combination of elementary techniques from algebra, combinatorics, and analysis and employ the notion of a field with an absolute value.

1. Introduction 

An observation by Jean-Pierre Serre implies that a generic cubic polynomial has a root that can be written as a power series in the discriminant of the polynomial, and that generic polynomials of higher degree do not. (See (7) and Proposition 39 for precise statements of his observation.) The first main result of this paper is to write down an explicit formula for this root α of a cubic, which we call the 

discriminant root , answering a question he posed. We state it in the context of polynomials over a field with an absolute value, a more general setting than Serre’s question.

We call the series displayed in (2) the discriminant series . It provides a root also for non-depressed cubics, see the discussion around (18). We prove an analogous formula for fields of characteristic 3, see Theorem 25. Note that the characteristic 3 formula is stated for non-depressed cubics. We show by example that no such formula exists for depressed cubics in characteristic 3, see Example 15. The discriminant root of a cubic polynomial, when it is defined, is distinguished among the roots of the polynomial, because it is obtained by specializing the dis-tinguished root of the generic monic cubic. 

Second main result: real and complex numbers. Theorem 1 is stated in the generality of fields with an absolute value, which contains as special cases the setting for Serre’s question, which we call the generic case , and the real and complex numbers. Inspired by this, we use the real and complex numbers for a concrete illustration of the theorem. In order to give a complete picture, we compare the discriminant series versus other series solutions for cubic polynomials in the literature, of which there are many since mathematicians have been producing them since the 1700s. Our second main result shows that the discriminant root of a depressed real cubic, if that series converges, is the longest root of the cubic. (Recall that a cubic polynomial is called depressed if it has no quadratic term.) We also show that a series solution known to Lagrange, which we call the trinomial root , gives the shortest root of the depressed cubic, if the corresponding series converges. These characterizations of the roots in terms of their lengths seem to be new, even though they are about cubic real polynomials, a well-trodden subject of study.

∆ of f is not zero: (a) If the discriminant series converges unconditionally, then the discriminant root is the root of f in C with the largest absolute value and it has the same sign as −q.ROOT OF A CUBIC POLYNOMIAL AS A POWER SERIES IN THE DISCRIMINANT 3

(b) If the trinomial series converges unconditionally, then the trinomial root is the root of f in C with the smallest absolute value. (c) If ∆ > 0, then both the discriminant and trinomial series converge uncon-ditionally to roots of f and the trinomial root has the same sign as q.

Case (c) is the famous casus irreducibilis, where producing roots of the cubic by Cardano’s formulas requires the use of complex numbers despite all the roots being real, whereas the roots are easily exhibited as sums of convergent series with real-valued terms. In case (c), we can determine the third root as well, because the three roots sum to 0. The third root has length between that of the short and long roots, so it has the same sign as the short root, i.e., the same sign as q.

Third main result: quartics. Serre’s statement about generic cubics, made precise in (7) below, is the n = 3 case of a statement about generic polynomials of degree n, made precise in Proposition 39. For each n ≥ 3, there is a corresponding computational question, and Theorem 1 answers the n = 3 case. Our third main result is to answer the computational question for the n = 4 case of generic quartic polynomials, see Theorem 45. 

Plan of the paper. Part I of the paper focuses on answering Serre’s computa-tional question, i.e., it provides a formula for the root of the generic monic cubic polynomial as a power series in the discriminant. We begin by giving an alternative proof of Serre’s observation that such a root exists, which we do by computing the constant term of the root, see section 2. Next, in section 3, we recall background material on fields with an absolute value so that we may state our results in that level of generality. Section 4 provides a proof of our first main result, Theorem 1. The last section in this part, section 5, fills in the up-to-that-point-postponed details for characteristic 3, namely a proof of Serre’s observation and a proof of Theorem 25, a characteristic 3 analogue of our first main result. In Part II, we restrict our attention from the setting of fields with an absolute value to focus on the real and complex numbers. In section 6, we describe what we call the trinomial root of a cubic polynomial, an expression for a root in terms of a power series which dates back to Lagrange, and prove that it is indeed a root. The trinomial root makes sense in the more general context of fields with an absolute value, and it coincides with the discriminant root for fields of characteristic 2. In section 7, we prove our second main result, Theorem 3. In a last section, section 8, we survey real depressed cubics and conclude that asymptotically 100% or 48% of monic depressed real cubics have a discriminant or trinomial root, depending on how you count. In the final part of the paper, Part III, we formally state Serre’s observation about the factorization of generic monic polynomials and explain how the case of cubic polynomials is connected to Part I. This part of the paper culminates in the proof of Theorem 45, which answers the quartic case of Serre’s question, just as Theorem 1 answered the cubic case. 4 J. BLAND, S. GARIBALDI, AND J. 3. Absolute values 

With (7) in mind, Serre asked for an explicit formula for the root of the generic cubic f in C. We answer this question in a more general setting that includes both an answer to Serre’s question (which is about generic monic polynomials) and many cubic polynomials with real or complex coefficients. 

Definition 11. An absolute value on a field C is a function |·| : C → R≥0 such that for x, y ∈ C:(i) |x| = 0 if and only if x = 0. (ii) |xy | = |x| | y|.(iii) |x + y| ≤ | x| + |y| (triangle inequality). The usual absolute value on R or C is an absolute value in the sense of this definition. An absolute value turns C into a Hausdorff metric space where the distance between x, y ∈ C is |x − y| [Rib99, §1.5, K]. Just like one says in real analysis, the field C is said to be complete if every Cauchy sequence of elements of C has a limit in C. Completing C (as a metric space) with respect to an absolute value yields a field [Rib99, §1.5, M].

for d̸ = 0 and set vπ (0) = ∞. This gives an absolute value on the fraction field K

Serre’s observation about cubic polynomials is a corollary of a more general statement about “generic” polynomials of degree n ≥ 2, namely that they have a distinguished quadratic factor. Proposition 39. There exist unique monic polynomials r(t), u (t) ∈ A[t] such that

The meaning of ramified is this: For an irreducible polynomial g ∈ C[t], the absolute value on C extends uniquely to the field extension C′ := C[t]/g [Rib99,

12. Conclusion 

Serre observed that cubics are special among generic monic polynomials because they have a root that can be expressed as a power series in the discriminant. In this paper we made that root explicit, showed how it behaves over fields with an absolute value, and identified it concretely in the real case as the longest root. Along the way we saw how this discriminant root compares with other classical formulas: Cardano’s messy radicals, the elegant trigonometric solution, and the hypergeometric series of Birkeland and Zucker. It turns out the discriminant root coincides with one of Zucker’s hypergeometric roots, but arises here in a way that makes sense over any field. When the discriminant root exists (because the dis-criminant series converges), we can divide by it and apply the quadratic formula to obtain concrete expressions also for the other roots of the cubic. Finally, we extended Serre’s question beyond cubics. For quartics, the relevant object is not a single root but a distinguished quadratic factor. We calculated this factor explicitly. It is natural to ask for a general formula for the quadratic factor for higher degrees.
