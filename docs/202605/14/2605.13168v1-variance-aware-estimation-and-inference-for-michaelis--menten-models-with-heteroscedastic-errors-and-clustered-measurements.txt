# Variance-Aware Estimation and Inference for Michaelis–Menten Models with Heteroscedastic Errors and Clustered Measurements 

Mijeong Kim a, Minkyoung Cha a, Ah Young Jeong a, ∗

> aDepartment of Statistics, Ewha Womans University, Seoul 03760, South Korea

Abstract 

Michaelis–Menten analysis is often conducted by nonlinear least squares un-der a constant-variance assumption, even though enzyme-kinetic data fre-quently display concentration-dependent heteroscedasticity and often include repeated or clustered measurements. We develop a variance-aware procedure for Michaelis–Menten estimation and inference that is motivated by condi-tional moment restrictions and implemented through simple conditionally Gaussian working models. For single curves, the method reduces to one-dimensional root finding for Km followed by closed-form plug-in updates for 

Vmax and a variance scale parameter; the same score logic yields a cluster-level extension through a random-effect-induced working covariance. In simula-tion, modeling heteroscedasticity improved variance recovery and interval ef-ficiency relative to homoscedastic nonlinear least squares, while cluster-aware semiparametric and NLME fits restored fixed-effect coverage far more effec-tively than pooled analyses that ignored clustering. In self-driving laboratory and soil exoenzyme data, heteroscedastic models achieved lower information criteria than homoscedastic nonlinear least squares, with the square-root variance function giving the most stable empirical fit among the prespeci-fied working models. We implement the workflow in the companion inferMM 

package for single-curve, grouped, and clustered Michaelis–Menten analy-sis. These results show that simple variance-function and covariance model-ing can stabilize original-scale Michaelis–Menten inference when variability changes with substrate concentration or measurements are clustered.

1. Introduction 

The Michaelis–Menten model remains one of the standard tools of enzyme kinetics because it provides a parsimonious and interpretable relationship be-tween substrate concentration and reaction velocity [1, 2]. A large literature has therefore focused on how the two canonical kinetic parameters, the max-imal reaction velocity Vmax and the substrate concentration at half-maximal velocity Km, should be estimated, from direct statistical fitting to graphical and distribution-free alternatives [3, 4, 5, 6]. In modern applications, direct nonlinear fitting on the original scale is generally preferred because it pre-serves the scientific interpretation of the mean model and supports formal uncertainty assessment [7]. In practice, however, uncertainty quantification can be more fragile than point estimation. Confidence limits for Vmax and especially Km depend on design quality and on how the error structure is handled [8, 9, 10, 11]. Routine analyses still often rely on homoscedastic nonlinear least squares even when assay variability increases with substrate concentration. In enzyme-kinetic applications, this kind of nonconstant variance has long motivated weighting and transformation strategies [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], yet those approaches either require ad hoc variance choices or make original-scale inference less direct [17]. Standard software such as base R, drc , and renz remains useful [18, 19], but variance modeling is typically left to the analyst. Motivated by this gap, we adapt the heteroscedastic semiparametric score framework of Kim and Ma [20] to Michaelis–Menten regression. We model the error variance as Var( ε | S) = γh (S), where γ > 0 is an unknown scale parameter that sets the overall error level and h is a simple working variance function, thereby preserving the original-scale interpretation of Vmax and Km

while allowing concentration-dependent noise. Our contribution is not a new general semiparametric efficiency theory beyond Kim and Ma [20], but rather a Michaelis–Menten-specific reduction of that framework to a simple original-scale workflow: a scalar profile equation for Km, closed-form up-dates for Vmax and γ, and a plug-in covariance formula that remains easy to use in practice. Because routine enzyme assays also often include repeated 2readings within concentration level, plate, or sample, we additionally ex-tend the same semiparametric score logic to clustered Michaelis–Menten data through a random-effect-induced working covariance, following the latent-variable semiparametric estimating-equation perspective of Ma and Genton [21]. We study the resulting procedures in parallel single-curve and clustered simulation benchmarks, illustrate the method with two real enzyme data sets, and implement the workflow in a companion R package, inferMM , for single-curve fitting, variance-model screening, and grouped analysis. Section 2 introduces the model and estimators, Section 3 presents the simulation study, Section 4 analyzes the real data, and Section 5 concludes. 

2. Semiparametric estimation for heteroscedastic and clustered Michaelis– Menten models 

2.1. Michaelis–Menten mean model 

We begin with the Michaelis–Menten mean structure common to both the single-curve and clustered settings. Following the classical Michaelis– Menten formulation [1, 2], we write the conditional mean reaction velocity at substrate concentration S as 

μ(S; Vmax , K m) = Vmax SKm + S , (1) where Vmax denotes the maximal reaction velocity and Km is the substrate concentration at which the mean velocity reaches one half of Vmax . We write the corresponding Michaelis–Menten mean parameter vector as β =(Vmax , K m)⊤. Equivalently, the model can be written as 

Yi = μ(Si; Vmax , K m) + εi,

where, in the single-curve formulation, (Si, Y i) denotes the ith observation and εi satisfies E(εi | Si) = 0 . In the clustered formulation, cluster i contains observations (Sij , Y ij ), j = 1 , . . . , n i, and the same mean function is applied componentwise as E(Yij | Sij ) = μ(Sij ; Vmax , K m). Thus the same Michaelis– Menten mean parameter vector β governs both settings considered below. These two parameters provide a standard and interpretable summary of enzyme kinetics, which is why the Michaelis–Menten mean function remains a widely used model in enzymology and related applications [1, 2]. In this paper, we retain the familiar mean structure in (1) and focus instead on ex-tending the error specification to allow for heteroscedasticity. Thus, the main 3methodological development in the following sections concerns the variance structure and the resulting semiparametric estimation procedure, rather than a modification of the Michaelis–Menten mean model itself. 

2.2. Single-curve heteroscedastic model 2.2.1. Variance structure and semiparametric framework 

We first consider the single-curve case with independent observations 

(Si, Y i), i = 1 , . . . , n , and allow the random error to have mean zero but a variance that depends on substrate concentration. Specifically, we write 

E(εi | Si) = 0 , Var( εi | Si) = γh (Si), (2) where h(·) is a prespecified working variance function and γ > 0 is an un-known scale parameter. We use this multiplicative decomposition to separate the overall variance level γ from the concentration-dependent shape h(Si),so that heteroscedasticity is retained while the variance model remains low-dimensional. This parsimonious specification is motivated by Kim [22], who emphasizes reducing the number of parameters in limited-sample settings. This specification allows the error variability to change with Si, rather than imposing the homoscedastic assumption commonly used in standard non-linear least squares. The Michaelis–Menten mean model itself does not de-termine the conditional error distribution or variance function, and in prac-tice this part of the data-generating mechanism is rarely known a priori .Throughout the methodological development, we keep h(s) in a general form and defer specific choices of the variance function to the simulation study and the real-data application. Our framework is semiparametric in the sense that the conditional mean is modeled parametrically by the Michaelis–Menten mean function, whereas the conditional distribution of the error is not fully specified. In other words, the modeling restrictions enter through the mean and low-dimensional vari-ance structure, while the error law beyond those conditional moments is left open. The conditionally Gaussian specialization introduced in Section 2.2.2 is therefore used as a parsimonious working model for tractable score reduc-tion, not as a claim that the data are exactly Gaussian. Inference is based on conditional moment restrictions, and our construction starts from the het-eroscedastic semiparametric efficient score functions derived by Kim and Ma [20] for nonlinear regression. 2.2.2. Reduced score and estimator 

Starting from the efficient score functions in Section 2.2.1, we specialize the general heteroscedastic nonlinear regression framework to the Michaelis– Menten mean model. Although the Kim–Ma formulation can also be adapted to asymmetric or nonparametrically modeled errors, doing so would require additional moment, shape, or smoothing components. In many enzyme-kinetic applications, however, each Michaelis–Menten curve is supported by only a modest number of distinct concentration levels and a small number of replicates per level. In that regime, introducing a more elaborate error model can quickly increase the nuisance-parameter burden relative to the information available in a single curve. This is especially problematic here because the mean parameters (Vmax , K m) already enter the model nonlinearly, so overparameterizing the error distribution or variance shape can weaken the separation between mean and variance effects, produce unstable estimating equations, and inflate the sampling variability of ˆVmax and ˆKm. Following the general recommendation of Kim [22] for limited-sample regression settings, we therefore use a parsimonious parametric working model for the errors: the goal is not to claim that the full conditional law is exactly known, but rather to retain the dominant heteroscedastic pattern with as few nuisance 5components as possible so that estimation remains numerically stable and first-order inference remains usable. This perspective is attractive because it occupies a middle ground be-tween marginal estimating-equation analyses and fully parametric nonlinear mixed-effects models. Relative to a standard GEE-style approach, the Ma– Genton construction uses the nuisance-tangent projection to target a semi-parametrically efficient score under the chosen first-two-moment model, so the working covariance can improve fixed-effect precision rather than serving only as a device for robust standard errors. Relative to NLME, the same construction avoids committing to a specific latent random-effects distribu-tion, which is appealing when assay-to-assay heterogeneity may be skewed, 10 heavy-tailed, or otherwise poorly approximated by a Gaussian mixing law. In simpler settings this projection can be written explicitly, while in more nonlinear mixed-model formulations the corresponding implicit version re-mains available as a principled fallback [21]. We therefore view the repeated-measurement extension not as a universal replacement for GEE or NLME, but as a robustness-oriented and still efficiency-conscious alternative when distributional assumptions for the latent effects are scientifically hard to jus-tify. We treat this repeated-measurement formulation as a substantive com-panion to the single-curve development rather than as a side remark. Sec-tion 3.2 studies it in an empirical-design clustered benchmark calibrated to the Alves assay layout and compares the resulting Ma–Genton-style working-covariance fit with pooled analyses and NLME competitors. Together with the single-curve derivations above, this makes clear that the present frame-work is not conceptually limited to distinct-concentration curves and that a principled random-effects extension is available when within-cluster correla-tion becomes scientifically important. 

3. Simulation study 

3.1. Single-curve simulation benchmark 3.1.1. Design 

We first evaluated the independent single-curve estimator on a fixed-grid benchmark. Competing estimators and performance measures 

We compared four estimators in the main simulation: standard nonlin-ear least squares (NLS) and three semiparametric estimators based on the working variance functions h(s) = log( s + 1) , h(s) = s1/2, and h(s) = s1/3.NLS serves as the conventional homoscedastic baseline. These working variance functions were chosen as simple monotone ap-proximations to plausible concentration-dependent noise patterns. For a fixed h(·), the resulting mean estimator already matches the corresponding weighted nonlinear least-squares score equations, so a separate fixed-weight WNLS row would mainly duplicate the same benchmark under a different label. 3.1.3. Results 

The two main-text tables summarize point-estimation and interval-diagnostic performance for the fixed-grid n = 50 benchmark under Normal errors. 3.2.2. Results 

The first striking feature of Tables 3–4 is that the point-estimation RMSE for Vmax changes little across methods, whereas the inferential behavior changes 19 dramatically once clustering is either acknowledged or ignored. When the data were pooled as if independent, the 95% coverage for Vmax collapsed to roughly 0.31–0.36 across the 3-cluster and 6-cluster templates, despite the fact that the corresponding Km intervals became strongly overconservative. In contrast, the Ma–Genton-style clustered fit and the two NLME competi-tors substantially improved Vmax coverage, raising it to about 0.76 in the harsher 3-cluster setting and to about 0.85 in the 6-cluster setting. Even so, these values remain below the nominal 95% level, confirming that first-order fixed-effect inference is still challenging when the number of clusters is small. The second feature is that the cluster-aware procedures also recovered the marginal variance structure substantially better than the pooled alternatives. In the 6-cluster setting, for example, the marginal variance-surface RMSE was about 1006–1010 for the pooled methods but only about 728–746 for the clustered and NLME fits. The matched Skewed/Skewed robustness tables in Supplementary Tables S12–S13 show the same qualitative ordering. Thus, the main practical benefit of acknowledging clustering is not a dramatic shift in the fitted mean curve, but rather a much better separation of cluster-level variation from concentration-dependent residual noise. Within the cluster-aware group, the Ma–Genton-style working-covariance estimator and the NLME benchmarks were broadly comparable for Vmax , but the semiparametric clustered fit was often slightly more competitive for Km

RMSE than the homoscedastic NLME fit and remained close to the more flex-ible varConstPower alternative. That pattern is useful because it shows that one need not fully specify a latent Gaussian mixed-effects model to obtain most of the practical gain from cluster-aware covariance modeling. At the same time, the 3-cluster template remains challenging for all methods, and the clustered benchmark confirms that repeated-measurement inference is more sensitive to small-cluster information loss than the single-curve bench-mark is to moderate heteroscedastic misspecification.

5. Discussion and conclusion 

This paper adapts a semiparametric score framework to Michaelis–Menten regression when the response variance changes systematically with substrate 27 concentration. By combining the Michaelis–Menten mean function with a low-dimensional working variance model σ2(S; γ) = γh (S), the proposed pro-cedure preserves the interpretability of the classical kinetic parameters while directly incorporating heteroscedasticity into estimation and inference. The semiparametric aspect lies in the conditional moment formulation rather than in a completely distribution-free implementation: for tractability, the esti-mator is specialized to a conditionally Gaussian working model, and for fixed 

h(·) its mean-parameter equations coincide with Gaussian weighted nonlinear least squares. The contribution here is therefore a Michaelis–Menten-specific score reduction and inference workflow, not a claim of a new general efficiency theory or universal robustness to arbitrary outliers and heavy tails. The simulation results show that explicitly accounting for heteroscedastic-ity often improves finite-sample performance relative to standard homoscedas-tic nonlinear least squares across a range of variance shapes and under both symmetric and moderately skewed errors. Although no single working vari-ance function was uniformly optimal, simple monotone forms were often suf-ficient to recover much of the efficiency gain, with the log-based specifica-tion performing well in the MM-type and Exp scenarios and the square-root specification performing especially well in the Hill scenario. To keep that benchmarking exercise transparent, the main-text tables focused on a few prespecified working models and on the Normal-error benchmark, while Sup-plementary Table S11 and Supplementary Table S6 report the correspond-ing single-curve point-estimation and extended-diagnostic summaries under a standardized right-skewed error law; those robustness runs showed the same broad ranking. Supplementary Tables S7–S8 provide a related design-density check under the original n = 100 fixed grid. Relative to the n = 50 

benchmark, the denser grid substantially shortens interval lengths and im-proves variance-function recovery, while coverage under Normal errors moves only modestly and not always monotonically. Under the skewed benchmark, coverage for the semiparametric methods is generally closer to nominal at 

n = 100 , especially in the MM-type and Hill scenarios. This pattern sug-gests that the main gain from increasing design density is improved precision and variance calibration rather than a uniform finite-sample coverage correc-tion. For applied screening, the companion package also allows log( S + 1) 

to be compared with power-family candidates h(S) = Sp over a positive grid by quasi-AIC or quasi-BIC. We did not benchmark that adaptive screening step in the main simulation because it would combine variance-model selec-tion uncertainty with the core method comparison, which is better studied 28 separately. The remaining scope limitations are also intentional: this is not because the framework cannot, in principle, accommodate richer variance or error models, but because in the modest sample sizes typical of enzyme assays, adding nuisance complexity can materially reduce finite-sample accu-racy and obscure the core method comparison. For that reason, the present benchmark does not yet extend beyond this moderate skewness to intercept-augmented noise models such as a + Sp, substantially heavier-tailed or more asymmetric error laws, or more irregular assay artifacts. The real-data applications are consistent with these conclusions. In both the self-driving laboratory enzyme data and the soil exoenzyme data, the het-eroscedastic models yielded lower AIC and BIC than homoscedastic nonlinear least squares, and the square-root variance specification provided the most stable empirical fit among the prespecified models shown in the main text. From a practical perspective, the companion package inferMM turns these methodological results into a reusable workflow for fitting single Michaelis– Menten curves, screening simple variance functions, optionally comparing 

log( S + 1) with power-family candidates h(S) = Sp, analyzing grouped en-zyme panels, and carrying out optional studentized wild-bootstrap sensitivity checks. Those checks should be interpreted cautiously: targeted supplemen-tary fixed-grid sensitivity runs at n = 20 and n = 30 with the calibrated bootstrap option (Supplementary Tables S9–S10), intended to probe the same overall scale as routine assays with a modest number of substrate lev-els and replicates, show that the procedure is computationally feasible and stable, but they also reveal genuine small-sample limits. At n = 20 , for ex-ample, the Km coverage probability in the square-root working model ranges only from 0.875 to 0.950 across the six settings, and the studentized wild bootstrap does not deliver a uniform improvement over the default Wald in-tervals. We therefore view the bootstrap as a sensitivity analysis rather than as a universal fix, and we regard score-inversion or profile-type intervals as a more promising route for future very-small-sample refinement. The clus-tered benchmark adds a parallel message for repeated measurements: once clustering is ignored, fixed-effect coverage can deteriorate badly even when point-estimation RMSE changes little, whereas Ma–Genton-style working-covariance fitting and NLME comparators improve both fixed-effect cover-age and marginal variance structure substantially. That comparison also clarifies why a semiparametric clustered formulation may be attractive when one wants more covariance-aware efficiency than a marginal GEE analy-sis but less distributional commitment than a fully parametric NLME fit. 29 At the same time, the 3-cluster template remains genuinely difficult, and even the 6-cluster template does not fully restore nominal Wald coverage, so repeated-measurement inference still has sharper small-cluster limits than the single-curve benchmark does. In applications with repeated indepen-dent measurements at the same substrate concentration, the same fitting and inference framework still applies; when technical or biological replicates induce stronger cluster-level dependence, the repeated-measurement score construction in Section 2.3 provides a natural next layer of generalization. Likewise, the pooled Alves analysis should be read as descriptive evidence that variance-aware fitting can improve upon homoscedastic NLS on a het-erogeneous assay collection, not as a replacement for a hierarchical model that explicitly represents depth, temperature, or core-level structure. Im-portant next steps are therefore to extend the working variance family to intercept-augmented forms such as a + Sp when baseline instrument noise is non-negligible, to study score-inversion/profile-type intervals for very small samples, and to incorporate more structured hierarchical analyses for assays that vary across temperature, depth, or other experimental factors.
