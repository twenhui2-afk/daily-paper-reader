THE DISTRIBUTION OF SUBTREES IN DENSE GRAPHS AND THE ROOTS OF THE SUBTREE POLYNOMIAL 

STEPHAN WAGNER AND RUOYU WANG 

Abstract. For a graph G with n vertices and a positive integer k ≤ n, let sk (G) be the number of subtrees (subgraphs that are trees, not necessarily induced) of G with k vertices. The subtree polynomial of G is S(G; x) = ∑nk=1 sk (G)xk . In this paper, we consider dense connected graphs with a minimum degree that is linear in the number of vertices. We prove that the number of missing vertices in a random subtree is asymptotically Poisson-distributed and deduce that all the roots of the subtree polynomial have to be close to 0.

1. Introduction

Related to this result, we remark that Luo, Xu and Tian [8] proved unimodality of the subtree polynomial for various special classes of graphs. The distribution of the subtree sizes in a tree was shown to be typically (but not always) asymptotically normal, see [11]. The first aim of this paper is to show that a weaker assumption suffices to obtain a result similar to Theorem 1: an asymptotic Poisson law holds for arbitrary (non-random) graphs if the minimum degree is linear in the number of vertices.

Under the same assumption, it was already shown in [12] that the probability for a random subtree to be spanning is bounded below by a positive constant that only depends on α. The key ingredient that we will use here to refine the argument, which is based on double counting, is a theorem of Pemantle and Peres [10] on the concentration of certain functionals. We can use Theorem 2 to infer information on the roots of the polynomial S(G; x) when 

G is a dense graph. Heuristically, the above theorem implies that S(G; x) ∼ sn(G)xneβ(G)/x ,and since the exponential function has no zeros, one expects the roots of S(G; x) to be close to 0. We make this argument precise and prove the following theorem, which is the second main result of this paper. 

Theorem 3. Let α > 0 be fixed, and consider a connected graph G with n vertices whose minimum degree δ is at least αn . Then we have, for every constant C > 6,

max {| x| : x ∈ C, S (G; x) = 0 } ≤ Cα log n

if n is sufficiently large.
