LLM system capabilities depend not only on model parameters but also on the executable harnesses 

that organize information acquisition, tool use, reasoning, and memory. Recursive harness evolution can automate their design, but how it can reliably discover generalizable systems from limited, noisy, and repeatedly reused feedback remains unclear. We study this question in agentic video understanding, where rich multimodal evidence and coupled mechanisms make harness optimization challenging. We first introduce VideoHarness-BRSI , a recursive self-improvement framework that evolves video harnesses around a frozen vision–language model. Analyzing over 4,000 evolved candidates, we find it competitive with hand-engineered systems but identify three interconnected failure modes: validation overfitting , search-budget dilution , and the early-experience trap . We trace all three to a common root: the same imperfect validation evidence drives both harness revisions and the proposer’s search strategy. We therefore propose VideoHarness-SRSI , an evidence-governed framework in which every search decision rests on evidence that is transferable, sufficient, and gathered before the search commits. It restricts the proposer to content-free feedback, informs exploration with instrumented warm-up experiments, and develops mechanisms in isolated probe–race branches before factorial composition and joint refinement. Across four video-understanding benchmarks, VideoHarness-SRSI improves the equally weighted mean held-out score by 6.2% relative to VideoHarness-BRSI and reduces the mean validation–test gap by 37 .9% . Reliable harness evolution thus depends on how search is organized and informed, not only on how well it optimizes validation performance.

## 1 Introduction 

An agentic system is shaped not only by its foundation model but by its harness : the executable code around the frozen model that decides what the model observes, which tools run, and how intermediate outputs become an answer (Ning et al., 2026). A well-designed harness can substantially improve performance (Lee et al., 2026), but designing one is hard, since each mechanism admits many implementations whose effectiveness hinges on other components. Automated agent design and recursive harness evolution replace manual engineering with an LLM proposer that iteratively generates, evaluates, and refines harnesses from accumulated feedback (Hu et al., 2025; Shang et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Yet, as harnesses become more complex, a fundamental question arises: How can recursive evolution reliably discover generalizable designs when experimental evidence is limited, noisy, and repeatedly reused, and the search space contains many interacting mechanisms? 

We study this question through agentic video understanding , a particularly demanding setting for harness optimization where a video harness must organize extensive, heterogeneous visual evidence via frame selection, retrieval, perception tools, multi-turn reasoning, reflection, memory, and multi-agent collaboration. These mechanisms are interdependent—retrieving more frames may recover missing evidence but also add distractors— so no mechanism can be judged in isolation. Moreover, evolution must infer useful changes from repeated evaluations on finite video–question pairs, whose rich content invites the proposer to specialize to seen 1arXiv:2610.19424v1 [cs.CV] 14 Oct 2026 examples. Video thus couples two challenges: learning reliable lessons from complex feedback, and efficiently exploring a combinatorial space of interacting mechanisms (Wang et al., 2025; Long et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2025). To study these challenges, we first build VideoHarness-BRSI (Baseline Recursive Self-Improvement), in which a multimodal LLM coding proposer iteratively revises the executable harness around a frozen vision– language model, guided by accumulated multimodal evidence and execution feedback. Evolution discovers competitive harnesses: for example, in a Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025) run evolving only retrieval, the evolved harness matches the held-out accuracy (68.4%) of a manually designed Adaptive Keyframe Sampling (AKS) variant (Tang et al., 2025) with 38 .7% fewer frames. Yet across 4,000+ evolved candidates, validation success does not reliably translate into generalization. We trace this gap to a single source: the loop learns only from validation verdicts —score changes on a finite, repeatedly reused validation set—which mix transferable improvements with instance-specific fit and noise, yet decide both which harness is kept and where the proposer searches next. This evidence misleads evolution in three ways. (1) Validation Overfitting concerns what a verdict measures: instance-level multimodal feedback lets the proposer fit observed examples, so verdicts reward non-transferable gains. (2) Search-Budget Dilution concerns how much evidence a verdict rests on: joint evolution spreads a limited budget over many mechanism combinations, and coupled edits change many answers at once, so each configuration is evaluated too rarely and noisily to attribute its gains. (3) 

Early-Experience Trap concerns how verdicts are acted on: a single noisy early outcome persists as a judgment in the proposer’s memory, so the proposer abandons useful mechanisms prematurely and over-explores early successes. Motivated by these findings, we further propose VideoHarness-SRSI (Structured Recursive Self-Improvement), an evidence-governed framework built on one principle: every search decision should rest on evidence that reflects transferable quality, suffices for the decision, and is gathered before search commits. The proposer sees only aggregate scores and content-free execution statistics , never the validation videos, questions, or answers, letting it diagnose harness behavior without tailoring revisions to seen data. Evolution then proceeds in stages: a warm-up complements free proposals with controlled experiments that ablate a mechanism or vary its intensity, measuring its contribution while keeping under-tested mechanisms open. This evidence seeds mechanism-specific branches , each evolved in isolation from a shared starting harness under a probe–race 

budget that tries every mechanism before focusing on the most promising. Finally, factorial composition tests whether branch improvements transfer and how they interact, before joint refinement co-optimizes them. Each stage thus supplies the evidence the next acts on. We evaluate on four video question answering and temporal grounding benchmarks: Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025), LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024), OVO-Bench (Niu et al., 2025), and MomentSeeker (Yuan et al., 2026). VideoHarness-SRSI yields stronger, better-generalizing harnesses than VideoHarness-BRSI ,raising the equally weighted mean held-out score by 6.2% relative and cutting the mean validation–test gap by 37 .9% ; ablations validate its design. These findings suggest that the organization and evidence quality of evolution matter beyond validation optimization. Our main contributions are: • Diagnosis. An empirical investigation of recursive harness evolution in agentic video understanding shows that it discovers competitive harnesses and characterizes three failure modes rooted in shared imperfect validation evidence. • Framework. We propose VideoHarness-SRSI , an evidence-governed framework combining content-free proposer feedback, instrumented warm-up experiments, and isolated mechanism development before joint refinement. • Evaluation. On four benchmarks, VideoHarness-SRSI improves mean held-out score by 6.2% relative and cuts the mean validation–test gap by 37 .9% over VideoHarness-BRSI .

## 3 Investigation of Video Understanding Harness Evolution 

Building on prior work in recursive harness self-improvement and hand-designed video understanding harnesses, we first introduce VideoHarness-BRSI , a baseline recursive self-improvement framework for agentic video understanding, to understand how harness evolution behaves in this more complex setting. As shown in Figure 1(a), an agentic coding proposer P evolves executable harnesses around a frozen VLM M and directly accesses the multimodal evidence behind each outcome: the validation videos and questions Ival , prior implementations, and execution traces. This full access deliberately extends the design of Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026) to video understanding. The loop is a simple propose–evaluate–accumulate cycle: starting from an archive D0 of seed harnesses, iteration t proposes candidates Ht,j ∼ P (· | D t−1, Ival ), evaluates each on the validation set Sval to obtain a record Et,j of scores, frame counts, and traces, and adds (Ht,j , Et,j ) to the archive Dt. Unless otherwise specified, M is Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) and P is Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026). VideoHarness-BRSI is specified in Appendix B. 

3.1 Can VideoHarness-BRSI Evolve Effective Video Harnesses? 

We first examine whether recursive evolution can discover harness designs competitive with expert-engineered alternatives. A fair test needs a strong hand-designed reference for the same mechanism, so we evolve only the 

retrieval strategy and compare with Adaptive Keyframe Sampling (AKS) (Tang et al., 2025), a state-of-the-art retrieval method; evolved and manual harnesses then differ only in how that mechanism is designed. We run on Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025), a widely used benchmark on which AKS was evaluated. We use 450 questions for validation and the remaining 2,250 for held-out evaluation. 

Finding 1: Recursive evolution discovers harnesses competitive with manual designs. Evolution steadily improves the harness. Figure 2(a) tracks the best validation accuracy found so far. Starting from the clip_topk seed at 

66 .2% , VideoHarness-BRSI improves it in several distinct steps rather than a single lucky jump: to 66 .9% 

in the first iteration, 68 .9% around iteration 6, 72 .4% around iteration 17, and finally 74 .7% at iteration 50 with medium_augspread , 8.5 points above the seed. Validation accuracy thus keeps rising throughout the 50 iterations. The evolved harness rivals manual design on held-out data. 3.2 Diagnosing VideoHarness-BRSI in Video Understanding 

Despite these gains, VideoHarness-BRSI shows three systematic failure modes, as shown in Figure 3. Since our baseline shares the core logic of prior harness evolution—propose new candidates from past ones and their scores, then keep those that win on validation (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Lee et al., 2026)—these failures expose limitations of this general strategy rather than of our baseline. We explain all three through the one signal the evolution loop learns from, the validation verdict ˆδ = V (H) − V (Hinc ): how much a candidate H’s validation accuracy exceeds that of the current champion Hinc . This verdict decides both what is kept and where the proposer searches next. What we actually want to improve, however, is accuracy on unseen questions, so the quantity that matters is the candidate’s true gain ∆: how much more accurate it is than the champion on new questions. Evolution works only as far as the verdict ˆδ reflects the true gain 

∆. To make the relation between the two explicit, we write each validation score as the sum of three parts, 

V (H) = μ(H) + S(H) + e(H): the accuracy μ(H) the harness would reach on new questions, a fit S(H) that helps only on the validation questions (zero for harnesses written without seeing them), and a zero-mean noise 

e(H), the chance deviation of its accuracy on this particular set of validation questions from μ(H). The true gain is then ∆ = μ(H) − μ(Hinc ). Subtracting the two scores would recover ∆ up to the fit and noise terms, if the champion’s noise were zero on average; but it is not, because the champion is not a random harness: it is the candidate with the highest validation score so far. Among many candidates of similar quality, the highest score tends to belong to one whose noise happened to be positive, so the champion’s noise is positive on average: E[e(Hinc )] = ω > 0. We call ω the champion inflation ; it is the same as the selection bias known as the optimizer’s curse (Smith and Winkler, 2006), here arising because the champion is selected by its validation score. Every new candidate is compared against this inflated score, so its verdict is shifted down by 

ω. Separating this bias from the noise difference, i.e., writing e(H) − e(Hinc ) = −ω + ε with the zero-mean remainder ε, splits the verdict into four terms:

VideoHarness-BRSI , following Meta-Harness, lets the proposer inspect the validation videos, questions, and execution traces directly. If this access drives the fit, the gap between validation and held-out accuracy should grow with how much the proposer observes. Finding 2: Richer feedback widens the validation–test gap by letting harnesses fit the validation questions. We call a harness’s validation accuracy minus its held-out accuracy its gap . As shown in Figure 3(a), the more the proposer sees, the larger the gap: it grows from 2.0 points (Blindfolded) to 3.2 points (Multimodal) on average, whereas hand-written baselines, which are written without seeing validation data, show none. The gap alone, however, does not tell how much of the validation progress is real. We therefore look at all evolved harnesses of a run together. By Eq. (1), their validation scores differ for three reasons: true gain ( ∆), validation fit (S), and noise ( ε); ω plays no role here, since all of them face the same champion. Only the true gain also shows up on held-out data, so we can attribute the spread of validation scores to the three sources. The 

true-gain share ρ∆ is how much of a validation difference reappears on held-out data: a harness one point higher on validation is, on average, ρ∆ points higher on held-out data. The noise share ρε is measured by scoring each harness on two random halves of the validation set, which differ only by noise. The rest is the 

validation-fit share ρS . As shown in Figure 3(d), ρ∆ falls from 54% (Blindfolded) to 35% (Trace) and 30% 

(Multimodal), while ρε stays at 14 –16% , so the loss goes entirely to ρS , which rises from 31% to 56% . Richer feedback thus does not make the validation score noisier; it makes it reward fitting the validation questions rather than real improvement. The fit is also visible in the code itself: proposers that see question text quote validation questions in their harnesses more often than chance, mostly in comments that record the cases motivating a design, whereas Blindfolded proposers do not. > Hide the validation instances from the proposer.

The more the proposer sees of the validation questions, the more of its progress is fit to those questions rather than real improvement ( 56% versus 31% of the validation spread). It should see how a harness behaves, not what it is evaluated on.  

> 3.2.2 Search-Budget Dilution

We next turn to the noise ε. Video harnesses combine mechanisms with distinct roles: frame selection decides which moments the model sees (Tang et al., 2025), retrieval gathers question-relevant evidence (Luo et al., 2025), tools extend perception (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026b), and memory carries information across long videos (Yeo et al., 2026; Long et al., 2025b). Letting evolution edit more of these mechanisms enlarges the search space and can, in principle, reach higher accuracy. But a larger space also spreads a fixed evaluation budget over more mechanism combinations , i.e., over more distinct choices of which mechanisms a harness uses and how, and a real gain can be told apart from noise only after enough verdicts on it accumulate to average the noise out. Whether the added expressiveness or the thinner evidence dominates is thus an empirical question. Keeping everything else fixed, we compare on Video-MME the smallest space, retrieval only (one mechanism family, as in Section 3.1), with the full harness (six families), at a matched budget of 87 candidates and three seeds each.  

> Finding 3: Larger search spaces leave too little evidence to tell real gains from noise, slowing evolution without improving held-out accuracy.

As shown in Figure 3(b), the larger space is not better: after 87 candidates, retrieval alone gains 6.4 validation points, whereas the full harness gains only 5.1 and lags for most of the run. Its deployed champions are also no better on held-out data, so the added expressiveness goes unused. To see why, recall that a single verdict is noisy: the loop can trust that a mechanism combination truly helps only after several candidates using it have been evaluated. We therefore measure each run’s statistical power ,the chance that its verdicts detect a real 1-point gain. This is a lenient test, since 1 point is already larger than most improvements the loop accepts. Power depends on two quantities: how many evaluations r each mechanism combination receives, since more evaluations average the noise out, and how noisy each verdict is (σ), since more noise hides the gain. A larger space can lower power through two channels. Breadth spreads the same budget over more mechanism combinations, which lowers r. Coupling lets mechanisms feed into one another: a change to retrieval, for example, alters what every downstream stage receives, so even a local edit flips the correctness of more answers, and the more answers an edit flips, the noisier its verdict, which raises σ. As shown in Figure 3(e), where each run is placed by its r and σ and the curves mark equal power, both channels are at work: from one family to six, r falls from 3.5 to 1.7, moving the runs left, and σ rises from 1.0 to 1.5 points, moving them up. Together they push the full harness into the shaded region of power below 0.2: its search misses more than four in five real 1-point improvements. Coupling is also visible in the edits themselves: even edits that touch a single mechanism family flip 47 .6 validation answers on average in the full harness, against 19 .8 with retrieval alone, and edits that flip more answers are more often harmful. Intermediate spaces with two and three families, the power analysis, and edit footprints are in Appendix F. 

Takeaway  

> Develop mechanisms separately before combining them.

Searching all mechanisms jointly leaves each verdict too noisy to detect most real gains (power below 0.2). Concentrating evaluations on one mechanism at a time, with local edits, yields verdicts that can be trusted before interactions are optimized.  

> 3.2.3 The Early-Experience Trap

The first two failure modes concern whether a verdict is right; this one concerns what the evolution loop does with it. In VideoHarness-BRSI , a verdict decides not only which candidate is kept but also which direction 

the proposer pursues next, where a direction is a kind of change, such as raising the frame budget or adding re-ranking. Early verdicts therefore shape the rest of a run. For each verdict, we ask two questions. How did 7the loop react: do later proposals build on the candidate ( continuation )? And was the verdict right: does the candidate beat the champion on held-out data, which the proposer never sees? We analyze 14 Video-MME runs; 12 of them evolve retrieval and form the frame-budget contrast below.  

> Finding 4: A single noisy early verdict can fix a run’s course, as weak verdicts drive irreversible decisions.

Figure 3(c) traces each run’s champion frame count: the same kind of probe can meet opposite verdicts, and the first verdict decides the rest of the run. In two runs of the same setting (thick lines), a probe raising the frame budget (circled) wins by ten validation questions in one run and loses by ten in the other. The first run then keeps raising the frame budget; the second writes into its memory that the frame count is “MINED OUT”, i.e., not worth exploring further, and its champion never exceeds 16 frames. This single verdict matters: across 12 runs, champions that expand reach 67 .6% held-out accuracy, versus 64 .9% for those staying near 16 frames. Eq. (1) explains why such a verdict misleads: a direction’s first verdict is ∆ − ω + ε, and both error terms are large. The noise ε dominates early verdicts: in the first ten iterations, only 8% of their variation reflects true differences, and the champion inflation starts every new direction about ω ≈ 1 point behind. A loop that weighed such weak evidence properly would treat verdicts as graded: the better a verdict, the more likely the direction is continued, without a sharp jump at zero. Figure 3(f) shows that VideoHarness-BRSI does not. Against the verdict z, measured in standard errors, it plots how often later proposals build on a candidate (black) and how often the candidate truly beats the champion on held-out data (blue). The loop acts as a step function, continuing about 77% of winning candidates but only 8% of the rest, whereas the held-out win rate rises gradually and already reaches one half for slightly negative verdicts. In fact, among dropped candidates with held-out evaluations, 37% beat the champion on held-out data; the shaded gap marks these abandoned candidates. Because a rejected direction is rarely retried, an unlucky first verdict is never corrected. A simple model of this policy predicts that the frame-budget direction is lost in 54% of runs, consistent with the 6 of 12 runs in Figure 3(c) that never expand. The verdict model, the persistence analysis, a replication on LongVideoBench, and case studies are in Appendix G. 

Takeaway  

> Do not let one noisy verdict close a direction.

Early verdicts are mostly noise, yet the loop treats them as final: one unlucky outcome can rule out a useful mechanism for the rest of the run ( 64 .9% versus 67 .6% 

held-out for the frame budget). Early evidence should come from controlled comparisons collected regardless of earlier outcomes. 

3.3 Implications for Harness Evolution 

One root, three symptoms. The three failure modes share one root: the loop learns from validation verdicts, and the same imperfect verdicts steer both which harness is kept and where the proposer searches next. Each failure mode lets a different term of Eq. (1) overwhelm the true gain ∆: the validation fit S under content-level feedback, the noise ε in a large and coupled search space, and the champion inflation ω together with ε in early, irreversible decisions. The three also reinforce one another: a misleading early win narrows the search, a broad space leaves little budget to revisit it, and each further verdict rests on too few evaluations to correct the course. 

Simple remedies are insufficient. Standard evolutionary levers act on where the next verdict is taken, not on what it measures. Changing how parents are selected reshapes the search as intended: on Video-MME, the share of candidates bred from the champion falls from 47% under greedy selection to 30% under clade-UCB and 8% under random parents. Yet the deployed harnesses of the three rules differ by at most 0.5 held-out points, and no statistic of the search shape predicts held-out gain across 18 runs. Starting from a stronger harness, AKS, speeds up early validation progress, but the final harness ( 68 .2% held-out) lies within the range of those evolved from simple seeds. In both cases, every verdict still compares a single candidate with a selected champion on the same validation questions, so S, ω, and ε are unchanged (Appendix H).

## 4 VideoHarness-SRSI 

Section 3.2 traced all three failure modes to the validation verdict that VideoHarness-BRSI learns from: rich feedback lets it reward fitting the validation questions ( S), a large search space leaves it too noisy to separate real gains ( ε), and noisy early verdicts close directions for good ( ε and ω). VideoHarness-SRSI 

therefore keeps the propose–evaluate–accumulate loop of VideoHarness-BRSI and changes only the evidence behind its decisions (Figure 4). Each component implements one takeaway: information isolation limits what the proposer can fit (Section 4.1), and three-stage evolution concentrates evidence on one mechanism at a time (Section 4.2), beginning with an instrumented warm-up that keeps early directions open (Section 4.2.1). The full algorithm (Algorithm 2) and further details are in Appendix I. 94.1 Information Isolation 

Finding 2 demonstrates that the validation fit grows with what the proposer observes about the validation instances, and that the Blindfolded arm kept the largest transferable share of its progress ( 54% ) while deploying harnesses at least as accurate on held-out data as the richer arms. Since a harness can fit the validation questions only through what the proposer learns about them, we close this channel at its source instead of penalizing edits afterwards, when the information has already been used. In VideoHarness-SRSI , the proposer never accesses the validation videos, questions, options, or answers; only the harness it writes does, at execution time. The proposer receives each candidate’s validation accuracy and a content-free trace T (H):per question, its correctness under an opaque identifier plus behavioral counters such as model and tool calls, frames, retrieval, and memory operations. This is exactly the Blindfolded feedback, so isolation lowers the fit without starving the search. A deny list on all proposer tools enforces it; details are in Appendix I.1. 

4.2 Three-Stage Evolution 

Finding 3 shows that searching the full harness jointly loses power in two ways: breadth spreads the budget over many mechanism combinations, and coupling lets each edit change many answers. Restricting the search to a single mechanism would avoid both, but forfeits the expressiveness of the full space. We instead 

order the search: first collect controlled evidence about every mechanism (Stage I), then improve each mechanism separately, where evaluations concentrate and edits stay local (Stage II), and only then optimize their interactions among a few mechanisms whose effects are already measured (Stage III). The search thus keeps the full space while each decision gets the evidence of a small one.  

> 4.2.1 Stage I: Instrumented Warm-up

Finding 4 reveals that VideoHarness-BRSI gathers evidence only where earlier verdicts were favorable, so one unlucky verdict can close a beneficial direction for the rest of a run. The warm-up runs the ordinary loop but reserves part of each round for controlled interventions that are collected regardless of earlier verdicts. Besides three free proposals, each round includes one removal , which deletes one of the seven mechanism families into which VideoHarness-SRSI groups the harness primitives (a finer grouping than the families chartered for VideoHarness-BRSI ; Appendix I.2), and one intensity change, which roughly doubles or halves how much one family is used, such as the retrieval budget, the number of frames a detector inspects, or the number of reflection passes. For each warm-up candidate H with parent p(H), we record its verdict and its change in mechanism usage, 

w(H) =  ˆδ(H, p (H)) , u(H) − u(p(H)) , (2) where u(H) summarizes runtime mechanism usage from T (H); slot requests are audited from this usage change rather than trusted. Evidence on each mechanism thus accumulates over many comparisons instead of resting on its first verdict, and because all removals are judged the same way, the champion’s inflation shifts them alike and does not distort how mechanisms compare. The warm-up summarizes this evidence in a per-mechanism warm-up report ; mechanisms without enough evidence are flagged as under-explored rather than discarded. When accuracy plateaus, the six best warm-up harnesses form a seed pool B, and the best of them becomes the backbone H0 shared by all later stages, so later stages draw on several strong lineages rather than a single, possibly lucky, champion (ablation in Section 5.4). The mechanism families, slot audit, stopping rule, and warm-up report are detailed in Appendix I.2.  

> 4.2.2 Stage II: Isolated Branch Evolution

To remove coupling, Stage II splits the harness into six mechanism axes— retrieval , frame budget , read schedule ,

multi-role , pixels and detectors , and derived state —plus a free-form axis that guards against a misspecified split, giving the axis set A = {α1, . . . , α 6, α free }. Each axis α ∈ A evolves from H0 in its own isolated branch, and a fixed template states which parts of the harness the branch may edit and a test that every edit must pass (e.g., only frame-budget edits may change the number of frames). Edits therefore stay local, and a branch’s gains are attributable to its mechanism. Templates are fixed in advance, so the choice of axes cannot itself fit the validation set; axes defined after the warm-up generalize worse (Section 5.4). From the warm-up 10 report, the proposer writes each axis a charter cα: the mechanism to try, a first edit, and a criterion that would falsify it. To counter breadth, a probe–race schedule concentrates evaluations without leaving any axis untried: every axis is probed with three candidates, the top half by best probe verdict against H0 race on to fifteen, and at most three raced axes, those with a clearly positive mean verdict first, proceed to composition. Since all axes share the backbone H0, the champion’s inflation shifts them alike and leaves their ranking intact. More details are given in Appendix I.3.  

> 4.2.3 Stage III: Factorial Composition and Joint Refinement

Gains found in separate branches need not add up. Greedy merging cannot tell whether they do. The proposer therefore distills each selected axis α ∈ A comp into a fixed, portable modification dα, the smallest edit of H0

carrying the mechanism. We evaluate every combination as a factorial design: 

HU = Apply  H0, {dα : α ∈ U} , U ⊆ A comp , H∅ = H0. (3) With three axes this costs seven evaluations, yet each modification’s effect is averaged over four comparisons rather than resting on one, and the same comparisons measure interactions. Joint refinement then starts from all combinations and may modify the selected mechanisms together: coupling returns, but only among at most three mechanisms whose effects are already measured. The final harness H⋆ is the validation-best one after refinement; this last selection on validation reintroduces some fit and champion inflation, a source of the residual gaps in Section 5.2. The composition lattice, interaction estimates, and refinement are detailed in Appendix I.4. 

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate VideoHarness-SRSI against fixed hand-written harnesses and VideoHarness-BRSI on four video-understanding benchmarks (Section 5.2), and ask whether its gains come from a larger inference budget (Section 5.3) and which components drive them (Section 5.4). Appendix K further verifies that the gains hold on videos never seen during evolution, and Appendix L re-measures the three failure modes of Section 3.2 on the runs of VideoHarness-SRSI .

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks and splits. We use four benchmarks covering general, long-form, and streaming video question answering and temporal grounding: Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025), LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024), and OVO-Bench (Niu et al., 2025), scored by accuracy, and MomentSeeker (Yuan et al., 2026), scored by mIoU. Each benchmark is randomly split once into validation and held-out test sets shared by all methods and seeds (Table 7 in Appendix A.2). Evolution and selection see only the validation set, and held-out results never reach the proposer. The split unit is the question, except on MomentSeeker, where whole source videos are assigned to one side; Appendix K checks that our conclusions do not depend on videos shared across the split. All benchmarks are used video-only: subtitles and metadata are withheld from harness code. 

Models. The executor is a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) with greedy decoding; it is the only model that sees video or answers questions. The proposer is Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026), which writes harness code but never answers questions or runs the benchmark. Harnesses may call CLIP retrieval (Radford et al., 2021) and Grounding DINO detection (Liu et al., 2024) under fixed per-question limits on frames, executor calls, and tool calls (Table 8). 

Baselines. The six hand-written harnesses of Table 9 (Appendix A.4) serve as fixed baselines and seed every evolution run. They cover the main mechanism families of video harnesses: uniform sampling, query-conditioned retrieval, self-reflection, tool-gated selection, multi-role debate, and a working memory. 5.2 Main Results 

Evolution outperforms hand-written harnesses. Both evolved frameworks beat every fixed baseline on held-out data on all four benchmarks (Table 1). Averaged over benchmarks, they exceed the strongest baseline on each benchmark by 5.12 (VideoHarness-BRSI ) and 8.52 points ( VideoHarness-SRSI ). No single fixed design is best everywhere: clip_topk leads on Video-MME, LongVideoBench, and MomentSeeker, and reflect_loop 

on OVO-Bench. Nor does adding mechanisms by hand reliably help: debate_pair spends five times the frames of clip_topk on Video-MME (Table 9) but is less accurate on held-out data on every benchmark. Which mechanisms help, and how to combine them, is benchmark-dependent, which is exactly what evolution searches for. 

VideoHarness-SRSI finds stronger and better-generalizing harnesses. VideoHarness-SRSI improves on VideoHarness-BRSI on all four benchmarks, raising the mean held-out score from 54 .83 to 58 .23 , a 

6.2% relative gain. The gains are largest on OVO-Bench ( +5 .3), MomentSeeker ( +4 .0), and Video-MME (+3 .6), and smallest on LongVideoBench ( +0 .7). At the same time, VideoHarness-SRSI lowers the mean validation–test gap from 4.95 to 3.08 points ( −37 .9% ), with the largest reduction on OVO-Bench ( 5.7 → 1.3)and essentially no change on LongVideoBench ( 3.6 → 3.8). 

The gains come mostly from better transfer. On every benchmark except LongVideoBench, VideoHarness-SRSI gains more on held-out data than on validation. The clearest case is OVO-Bench, where held-out accuracy rises from 67 .2 to 72 .5 while validation accuracy rises only from 72 .9 to 73 .8; on MomentSeeker, the corresponding gains are +4 .0 and +1 .5 points. VideoHarness-SRSI thus selects harnesses that transfer better rather than fitting validation more tightly. 

Overfitting is reduced, not eliminated. Both evolved frameworks still score higher on validation than on held-out data on every benchmark. Information isolation and structured search mitigate overfitting (Section 4), but any procedure that repeatedly selects on the same validation set can still favor harnesses specialized to it.

## 6 Conclusion 

We studied how recursive harness evolution can reliably discover generalizable designs, using agentic video understanding as a test bed. Analyzing over 4,000 harnesses evolved by VideoHarness-BRSI , we found that evolution discovers competitive designs, but its validation gains do not reliably generalize: the same imperfect validation evidence adapts both the harness and the proposer’s search strategy, which leads to validation overfitting, search-budget dilution, and the early-experience trap. VideoHarness-SRSI governs this evidence through content-free proposer feedback, an instrumented warm-up, and isolated mechanism branches followed by factorial composition. Across four benchmarks, it raises the mean held-out score by 

6.2% and cuts the mean validation–test gap by 37 .9% relative to VideoHarness-BRSI , and about half of the gain remains at a matched frame budget. Reliable harness evolution thus depends not only on how well search optimizes validation performance, but on how it is organized and informed. 15 References

Multiple-choice benchmarks report accuracy, parsing the first valid option letter from the harness output (none scores as incorrect). Persistent memory. Besides the archive, the only state that survives an iteration is a persistent memory directory of the proposer’s agent environment, which later sessions load automatically (up to 160 KB per run in our logs). Evaluation. Each candidate is evaluated once on the full validation split, and its score is never recomputed. Executor and tool outputs are cached by content, so calls that repeat an earlier input, within or across candidates, return the cached output. Before evaluation, a static leak guard rejects any candidate that accesses the file system, the network, or evaluation logs; references dataset-identity or metadata handles such as the video path, question and video IDs, or the task type; hashes text; or embeds lookup tables (collection literals with more than 12 strings or strings longer than 4,000 characters). Harness code sees only the question, the options, and the video’s frame count, frame rate, and duration. A candidate that fails to import, violates a leak rule, or crashes during evaluation is recorded as failed and cannot become the champion. 

Frontier and deployment. The loop maintains the Pareto frontier of validation performance against mean frames per question. After the last iteration, the frontier members, the validation-best harness, and the seeded harnesses are evaluated once on the held-out split. The deployed harness is the validation-best harness. Held-out results are stored outside the proposer’s workspace.

Budgets. Parent suggestions. The default loop leaves the choice of parent entirely to the proposer. The parent-selection study of Appendix H.1 adds a list of suggested parents to the task description and varies only the rule that produces it. All other runs, including the full-harness runs of the search-space ladder and the main results, receive no parent suggestions; deployment is the validation-best harness in all cases. 

## C Evolving Capability of VideoHarness-BRSI 

This section gives the experimental setting behind Section 3.1 and explains its three design choices: the evolved mechanism family, the validation/held-out split, and the iteration budget. 22 C.1 Experimental Setting 

Evolving retrieval for a fair comparison. The question of Section 3.1 is whether recursive evolution can match expert manual design, so the comparison needs a strong hand-designed reference. We use Adaptive Keyframe Sampling (AKS) (Tang et al., 2025), which is a retrieval method: it scores frames by their relevance to the question and allocates the frame budget accordingly. We therefore let VideoHarness-BRSI evolve only the retrieval strategy in the main text. Evolved and hand-designed harnesses then differ in the same mechanism and run on the same frozen executor, so a gap between them reflects how the mechanism was designed rather than which mechanism was used. Evolving other or more mechanism families would give 

VideoHarness-BRSI components that AKS lacks, and a win could no longer be credited to evolution alone. Appendix C.2 reports evolution in other mechanism families and on other datasets. 

A 1:5 validation/held-out split. We split Video-MME at random into 450 validation and 2,250 held-out questions, a ratio of 1:5 (Table 7). This split keeps most of the benchmark away from evolution, for two reasons. • Evaluation cost. Every candidate is run in full on the validation split, which takes a frozen-VLM call on the sampled frames of every question. Evaluation is therefore the slow step of each iteration, and a small validation split shortens every one of the 150 evaluations in a run. • A stricter test of generalization. A small validation split gives the proposer less of the benchmark to fit, while the large held-out split measures the deployed harness precisely: with 2,250 questions, the standard error of a held-out accuracy near 68% is about one point, against about two points on the 450 validation questions. A larger validation share would let evolution fit a larger part of the benchmark itself and leave a smaller, noisier held-out split to detect it. Under the 1:5 split, a harness evolved on one sixth of the data must match the hand-designed methods on the remaining five sixths, which it never sees. That the evolved harness still matches the strongest of them on this split (Figure 2(b)) therefore strengthens, rather than weakens, the conclusion that VideoHarness-BRSI is competitive with human design. 

Iteration budget. Each run evolves for 50 iterations of three candidates, 150 candidate evaluations in total. 

C.2 Evolution Across Mechanism Families and Datasets 

The retrieval-only comparison isolates one mechanism for a fair test against AKS, but the evolving capability of VideoHarness-BRSI is not specific to retrieval or to Video-MME. 

Other mechanism families. The search-space ladder of Appendix F evolves, on the same Video-MME split, retrieval together with frame selection, with perception tools, and the full harness of six families, which adds reflection, role-based collaboration, and working memory. In every space, evolution raises validation accuracy over the best seeded harness by 5.1–7.6 points within 87 candidates, and the deployed champions reach 65 .7–66 .8% held-out accuracy (Table 15), above the best fixed baseline on Video-MME ( clip_topk ,

63 .4% ; Table 1). Evolution thus improves harnesses whichever mechanism families it may edit; what the families change is how efficiently it does so (Section 3.2.2). 

Other datasets. The main results evolve the full harness on all four benchmarks (Table 1). On held-out data, VideoHarness-BRSI exceeds the strongest fixed baseline on every benchmark: 66 .5 versus 63 .4% on Video-MME, 66 .6 versus 62 .1% on LongVideoBench, 67 .2 versus 55 .4% on OVO-Bench, and 19 .0 versus 18 .0

mIoU on MomentSeeker, 5.12 points on average. Evolution therefore transfers across general, long-form, and streaming question answering and temporal grounding, although, as Section 3.2 shows, its validation progress overstates these held-out gains.

## E Validation Overfitting 

This section supports Section 3.2.1. Its conclusions are: 24 • Richer feedback, larger gaps. The mean validation–held-out gap of evolved harnesses grows from 2.0 (Blind-folded) to 2.9 (Trace) and 3.2 points (Multimodal), while hand-written baselines show none (Appendix E.2). • Theory. Selecting on validation accuracy transfers only a fraction β of the validation advantage, and the non-transferable fit a harness can acquire is bounded by the information its feedback carries about the validation sample, which grows along nested channels (Appendix E.3). • Measurement. Validation scores are equally reliable in every arm, yet β falls from 0.54 to 0.30 , and the lost share reappears as instance-specific fit; the model predicts the winners’ held-out accuracy to within 0.34 

points (Appendix E.4). • Mechanism. Proposers that see question text quote validation questions in their code 1.55 × (Trace) and 

1.27 × (Multimodal) as often as chance, whereas Blindfolded proposers do not (Appendix E.5). 

E.1 Protocol 

All 27 runs use LongVideoBench (video only), the frozen Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct executor, and the full-harness space with perception tools disabled, evolving for 30 iterations of three candidates. E.5 Direct Evidence of the Content Channel 

Validation and held-out questions are a random split of one pool, so text chosen without regard to the split matches validation questions at the base rate π = |validation |/|pool |. Using the 18 runs of the 33% and 50% ladders, for which question text of both splits is available within the 75% validation set ( π = 0 .445 and 0.666 ), we collect every word 5-gram in evolved code that occurs in at most two question stems and test its matches against π by re-drawing the split 20,000 times. The leak guard (Appendix B.1) rules out lookup tables by construction. Table 13 shows that proposers that see question text quote validation questions beyond chance, and Blindfolded proposers do not. The quotations sit almost entirely in docstrings and comments that record the validation 28 cases motivating a design (e.g., “a white bowl containing potatoes”): of the 168 matched Trace questions, 144 are quoted in docstrings, 25 in comments, and only 4 in functional literals (a question can be quoted in several places), where vocabulary is at most weakly enriched (ratios 0.94 –1.08 ). Content thus enters through design decisions rather than copied keywords, producing the behavioral fit measured above.

E.6 Robustness 

The pooled ordering Blindfolded > Trace > Multimodal of β holds under Theil–Sen slopes, trimming, median per-run slopes, within-run Spearman correlations, and gain slopes relative to the best baseline (Figure 8b). Blindfolded exceeds Multimodal in every leave-one-ladder-out fit, whereas the adjacent orderings can reverse, so the contrast between the two ends of the information range is the robust one. ## F Search-Budget Dilution 

This section supports Section 3.2.2. Its conclusions are: • Larger spaces do not help. Deployed champions reach 66 .8, 65 .8, 66 .0, and 65 .7% held-out accuracy with one, two, three, and six mechanism families, and 94–100% of promotions in every space are statistically unresolved (Appendix F.2). • Theory. The power to detect an advantage ∆ is bounded by the evidence λ = r∆2/¯σ2, which a larger space erodes through breadth (fewer evaluations r per mechanism combination) and coupling (larger footprints, hence larger σ2) (Appendix F.3). • Measurement. From one to six families, r falls 2.05 × and the verdict variance rises 2.24 ×, so the power for a 1-point gain drops from 0.45 to 0.14 . Full-harness edits flip a median of 50 answers against 10, and since only 37% of flips are fixes, larger edits are also more harmful (Appendix F.4).

## G The Early-Experience Trap 

This section supports Section 3.2.3. Its conclusions are: • Verdicts are weak and biased. On Video-MME only 18% of verdict variance reflects true differences ( 8% in iterations 1–10), and the champion’s cached score is inflated by bω ≈ 1.0 point (Appendix G.2). • The policy ignores this. The held-out win rate changes gradually with the verdict, yet the loop contin-ues winners with probability 0.77 and non-winners with 0.076 , and treats false losers like true losers (Appendix G.3). • Theory. Because rejected directions are rarely resampled, champion inflation ( ω > 0) makes a weak beneficial direction more likely to be lost; for the frame budget the loss probability is 0.54 , consistent with the 6 of 12 runs that never expand it (Appendix G.4). • Consequences. Seeds of one setting diverge 2.8× more than independent sampling predicts, and runs that stay at 16 frames lose 2.7 held-out points (Appendices G.5 and G.6).

G.5 Path Dependence across Seeds 

If early noisy verdicts steer exploration, seeds of one setting should diverge more than independent samples would. Dividing the cross-seed standard deviation of each family’s candidate share by its binomial value, the median ratio is 2.8 on Video-MME and 1.7 on LongVideoBench , the signature of reinforcement processes that amplify early outcomes (Arthur, 1989); re-ranking, for example, takes 93% of one seed’s candidates but 2–11% 

in the other four. The held-out cost of staying at the baseline budget ( 64 .9 versus 67 .6% , p = 5 × 10 −5) holds under alternative definitions of “stays” and without the AKS-seeded run ( p ≤ 0.003 ). 

G.6 Case Studies 

A weak loss becomes a prohibition, or not. In retrieval-only seed 1, a 32-frame probe loses by 10 questions at iteration 8 ( z = −1.41 ); memory then records frame count as “MINED OUT—do NOT re-propose”, and no later candidate exceeds 16 frames. In seed 4, a 33.9-frame probe loses by 12 questions ( z = −1.52 ), but a sibling at 18.3 frames wins by 10 questions ( z = +1 .44 ); memory keeps the direction open, and the champion reaches 25–30 frames and 67 .3% held-out, against 64 .8% for seed 1. A Bayes-rational policy (Proposition G.1) would still give seed 1’s rejected direction probability ≈ 0.29 .

Over-commitment to an early winner. In the same seed 1, after a re-ranking candidate wins by four questions at iteration 5 ( z = +0 .76 ), all 105 later candidates are re-ranking variants and 96% fail to beat the champion (Figure 12a). Four promotions add 2.4 validation points but only 0.04 held-out points, and 23 of 115 tested candidates beat the deployed champion on held-out data (panel b). 

## H Why Simple Remedies Are Insufficient 

This section supports the discussion of simple remedies in Section 3.2. Its conclusions are: • Parent selection. Changing which harness the proposer builds on reshapes the search as intended, but does not improve held-out accuracy (Appendix H.1). • Stronger initialization. Slots and audit. Each round evaluates five candidates in three slots: Slot A makes three unrestricted free

We re-measure the three diagnostics of Section 3.2 on the runs of Table 1.
