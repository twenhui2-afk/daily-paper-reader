# Near Minimax Rate for Quantile Distributional Reinforcement Learning 

Zijie Cheng ∗ Zhihua Zhang †

October 22, 2026 

Abstract 

In this paper, we study quantile distributional policy evaluation, where the return distribution under a given policy is approximated by the quantile Bellman fixed point ηm. We investigate the statistical accuracy of estimating ηm across quantile resolutions under Wasserstein distances. Assuming access to a generative model, we construct a quantile fixed point estimator ηpnq

> m

from an empirical Markov decision process and establish non-asymptotic error bounds under Wasserstein distances. When reward densities are uniformly bounded away from zero, we obtain a root-n high-probability bound simultaneously over all resolutions m and all Wasserstein orders 1 ď p ď 8 . When reward densities vanish at the support endpoints, we show that continuity and endpoint monotonicity suffice to preserve the same resolution-independent rate for 1 ď p ď 2. For 

p ą 2, we establish a root-n bound with coefficient m1{2´1{p, together with a slower, resolution-independent n´1{p bound for every finite p ą 2. We further obtain resolution-independent W8

consistency governed by the continuity modulus of reward quantile functions. To investigate the sharpness of these bounds, we characterize the exact local asymptotic minimax risk at each fixed resolution as the expected Wasserstein-coordinate norm of the efficient Gaussian limit and prove that our estimator attains it. We then examine the resolution dependence of this optimal local risk. A regular one-state instance has resolution-independent local risk constants, whereas a single endpoint-vanishing two-state instance exhibits growth of order m1{2´1{p, up to logarithmic factors along a resolution subsequence, for every p ą 2.

Reinforcement learning has achieved substantial progress across a wide range of domains, including game-playing [27, 33], robotics [14], and large language models [19, 18]. In the classical formulation of reinforcement learning based on the reward hypothesis [29, 30], policy performance is evaluated through expected returns ( i.e. , the expected discounted cumulative reward). While this criterion provides a principled objective for sequential decision-making, it ignores the distributional charac-teristics of returns, such as variability, tail risk, and higher-order distributional information. Such considerations are important in many real-world applications. In healthcare, for instance, one is concerned not only with average outcomes but also with adverse or long-tail effects of dynamic treatment regimes, as poor outcomes may have severe consequences for patients [15]. In financial decision-making, investors must balance return and risk, since higher expected returns are typically associated with greater uncertainty [10]. Distributional reinforcement learning [17, 1] generalizes the classical expected-return framework by characterizing the full distribution of return rather than only its expectation. This distributional perspective provides a richer description of uncertainty in the performance of learning agents, capturing variability arising from both the stochastic nature of environments and the randomness induced by the agent’s policy. However, return distributions are generally infinite-dimensional objects, and hence cannot be represented or learned exactly. This motivates finite-dimensional approximations of return distributions, among which categorical and quantile-based representations are two prominent choices. In particular, a widely-used family of algorithms for distributional reinforcement learning is based on the notion of quantile representations, an approach that originated with Dabney et al. [9]. By approximating the return distribution through a finite collection of quantiles, quantile representations provide a flexible and computationally tractable approximation while preserving key distributional information. As a result, quantile-based approaches have been widely adopted in practice and have become a standard paradigm in modern distributional reinforcement learning, underpinning methods such as quantile regression deep Q-network (QR-DQN) [9], implicit quantile network (IQN) [8], and non-crossing quantile regression method [37]. This paradigm has also been successfully applied in a range of settings, including high-altitude balloon guidance [2], robotic manipulation [4], and benchmark simulated domains such as the Arcade Learning Environment [9, 8]. The statistical analysis of a quantile representation must account for both the sample size and 3the number of quantiles. Increasing the number of atoms improves the representation of a return law, but it also introduces more parameters and moves the outermost quantile levels closer to zero and one. A small density at such a level can amplify the estimation error of an individual coordinate. In a Wasserstein distance, however, each coordinate has probability mass 1 {m. Whether this averaging compensates for the instability of extreme quantiles is not determined by a coordinatewise analysis. Cheng et al. [7] developed a model-based analysis of the quantile Bellman fixed point, including a non-asymptotic supremum W8 bound with coefficient of order ?m up to logarithmic factors, fixed-resolution asymptotic normality and efficiency, and a limiting covariance analysis as the quantile resolution increases. These results leave a more specific question: which dependence on the resolution is intrinsic to estimation, and which dependence comes from the choice of loss or from bounding all quantile coordinates by the worst one? In this paper, we answer this question by studying estimation of the projected fixed point under the full family of Wasserstein distances. The role of the density assumptions is central. A positive lower density bound, familiar from quantile estimation, rules out local degeneracy in the reward law. We also consider a complementary regime in which the reward density vanishes at the support endpoints and is monotone near them. This second regime imposes no quantitative rate of boundary decay. We show that the statistical cost of removing the lower density bound depends on the Wasserstein order: resolution-independent root-n estimation survives through W2, whereas higher orders can have an unavoidable polynomial dependence on m in their first-order statistical constant. From a theoretical perspective, a quantile Bellman fixed point is not a collection of ordinary sample quantiles. Each continuation quantile changes the Bellman target distribution at other states, and the target must then be projected back onto the midpoint quantile grid. Although the projected Bellman map is globally contractive in supremum W8 metric, that property alone does not preserve the averaging that makes an m-free supremum W2 bound possible. The proof must control the empirical perturbation, invert it on the actual finite grid, and propagate it through the nonlinear fixed-point equation without adding a discretization remainder.

1.2 Related Works 

Distributional reinforcement learning. Our operator framework uses distributional Bellman operators and finite-dimensional representations [1, 3]. Quantile representations underlie quantile regression methods [9], implicit quantile networks [8], fully parameterized quantile functions [35], and non-crossing quantile regression [37]. For categorical representations, Rowland et al. [23] analyzed convergence of categorical temporal-difference learning. Peng et al. [20] studied its statistical efficiency, and Peng et al. [21] established finite-sample results with linear function approximation. For quantile temporal-difference learning, Rowland et al. [25] established convergence, and Rowland et al. [24] studied its statistical benefits for expected-value estimation. Our object is instead a model-based empirical quantile Bellman fixed point, and our focus is the dependence of its statistical error on the quantile resolution. 

Statistical analysis of reinforcement learning. Statistical inference in reinforcement learning has been developed for value functions and off-policy evaluation, including high-confidence procedures [32, 13], bootstrap methods [11], and inference in infinite-horizon settings [26]. Distributional and risk-sensitive evaluation has been studied through return-CDF estimation [6, 12] and distributional off-policy methods [34, 22]. In the generative-model setting, Zhang et al. [36] developed estimation and inference for unprojected return distributions. The companion work of Cheng et al. [7] supplies the regularity framework and canonical gradient for quantile Bellman fixed points. The present paper addresses resolution-uniform Wasserstein estimation and exact local expected-distance risk. In particular, our local model is the same general joint-law model used for efficiency there, rather than a model restricted to independent reward and successor-state perturbations. Section 3 presents the estimator and all main results. Section 4 gives their proofs, with the main intermediate steps stated as lemmas. Section 5 concludes. # 5 Conclusions 

In this paper, we have investigated the statistical accuracy of model-based quantile distributional policy evaluation as the quantile resolution increases. Although a finer quantile representation introduces more parameters and increasingly extreme quantile levels, it does not necessarily lead to greater estimation error. Under uniformly positive reward densities, root-n estimation remains possible uniformly over all resolutions in every Wasserstein distance. Even when reward densities vanish at the support endpoints, this resolution-independent rate persists through W2 without any quantitative restriction on boundary decay. In contrast, higher-order Wasserstein distances are more sensitive to the estimation errors of extreme quantiles, leading to a potentially stronger dependence on the resolution. Beyond these non-asymptotic guarantees, we have characterized the exact local asymptotic minimax risk at each fixed resolution and established its attainability by the quantile fixed point estimator. This characterization provides a statistical benchmark for examining the resolution dependence of the estimation error. Our fixed-instance analysis shows that the optimal local risk can remain bounded across resolutions, while under endpoint-vanishing reward densities it can also exhibit the polynomial growth predicted by our non-asymptotic bounds, up to logarithmic factors. Thus, the resolution dependence of the estimator reflects an intrinsic statistical difficulty at a fixed MDP. 33 References
