## Abstract 

EIP-1559 has become the dominant transaction fee mecha-nism in permissionless blockchains, regulating scarce block space through an adaptive base fee. The design balances a trade-off between responsiveness to demand fluctuations and stability of long-term resource usage. On a single chain, this trade-off is well understood and empirically stable. In multi-chain systems, however, cross-chain flows introduce delayed economic interactions across otherwise independent fee mar-kets. These interactions alter the control dynamics and create stability risks that existing models do not capture. Deployed systems have already polarized. Polkadot enforces conser-vative update rates, effectively disabling fee adaptivity and reverting the market to inefficient first-price auctions. Cosmos adopts aggressive update rates without formal guarantees. To fill this gap, we propose a control-theoretic framework that formalizes multi-chain fee markets as a nonlinear dy-namical system with delayed feedback. We identify delay-amplified over-correction as the structural root of instability. Extensive experimental evaluations yield quantitative and qualitative findings: cross-chain coupling erodes stability rel-ative to monolithic blockchains, reducing the safe parameter space for base fee update rates by up to 84.8%. Moreover, instability propagates across chains, contaminating the oth-erwise stable peer chains. At Ethereum’s update rate, which leaves a monolithic chain stable, the multi-chain system ex-hibits extreme load oscillation between full and near-empty blocks, with over half of all blocks at full capacity. Main-stream Cosmos chains already exceed their safe bounds under their default configurations, and the resulting load oscilla-tion raises their block failure rate by an order of magnitude. Guided by this mechanism, we design a lightweight protocol-level mitigation that removes the over-correction and stabi-lizes dynamics in multi-chain environments. 

## 1 Introduction 

The transaction fee mechanism [9, 41] is a fundamental com-ponent of permissionless blockchains [37, 40, 43]. It allocates scarce block space and deters spam, serving both security and usability. On security [6,35], the mechanism uses gas as a uni-versal unit of computation and enforces a strict per-block re-source cap, namely the gas limit , which prevents system over-load and preserves decentralization, consensus security, and protocol resilience [11, 21, 42]. On usability, each transaction consumes gas determined by its execution, and its initiator pays a fee. The fee per unit of gas acts as a market-based price signal that incentivizes validators to include transactions and regulates user demand under congestion. Ethereum’s original first-price auction produced highly volatile fee markets, be-cause users competing for inclusion had no pricing guidance and relied only on discretionary tips to the block proposer. This limitation motivated Ethereum Improvement Proposal 1559 (EIP-1559) [16]. EIP-1559 replaces the first-price auc-tion with a market-driven adaptive base fee , and introduces elastic block capacity that absorbs short-term demand spikes by allowing blocks to temporarily exceed the target gas usage up to twice the pre-EIP-1559 limit. At its core, EIP-1559 is a negative feedback control loop. The base fee increases when block usage exceeds the target, scaled by a critical update parameter δ, and decreases symmetrically otherwise, driving the long-run average load toward a fixed gas-usage target. The mechanism improves usability, because fees become pre-dictable and less volatile, and preserves security, because the effective operating load converges to the target [35]. The effectiveness of EIP-1559 hinges on δ, which presents a fundamental trade-off between usability and security. If δ is set too conservatively, the mechanism responds sluggishly and collapses into an unpredictable first-price auction [41]. If δ is set too aggressively, the negative feedback effects can desta-bilize the system and induce load oscillations [31]. Moreover, stability is a security requirement. Stable operation keeps the realized long-run normalized load at the target and thus at the pre-EIP-1559 baseline. Instability drives the load into a per-sistent oscillation [31–33], which deepens from a pattern that never fills a block to one in which blocks alternate between the gas limit and near-emptiness, with more than half of all blocks full in the extreme case. A monolithic chain never 1arXiv:2610.23305v1 [cs.CR] 16 Oct 2026 enters this load oscillation at any deployed update rate. We discuss the security implications in Section 2.1. Monolithic blockchains such as Ethereum currently operate within a stable regime, and empirical evidence confirms that it remains robust despite high responsiveness [17, 35]. This sta-bility has led to widespread adoption of EIP-1559controllers beyond Ethereum [31], and also in multi-chain systems such as Polkadot [44] and Cosmos [29]. These systems scale through functional specialization. They run multiple inde-pendent blockchains in parallel, referred to as parachains and 

zones , respectively, and each chain typically implements an independent instance of EIP-1559. Unlike monolithic chains, however, the chains are coupled through interoperability pro-tocols [26, 45] that enable cross-chain composition, introduc-ing new interactions between otherwise isolated fee markets. 

Motivation. Transplanting the responsiveness-stability trade-off of EIP-1559 into multi-chain architectures creates a criti-cal dilemma, and deployed systems have already polarized. At one extreme, Polkadot enforces update rates as small as 10 −5 (Table 2) and ensures stability, which turns the base fee into a quasi-static tax . Users fall back on tips under con-gestion and the market regresses to an inefficient first-price auction [41]. At the other extreme, Cosmos zones such as Osmosis adopt aggressive Ethereum-like update rates without formal guarantees. Cosmos has not yet exhibited instability in production, because weak demand keeps these chains out of the high-load regime where the feedback dynamics become visible, so their traces characterize only the low-load regime and cannot establish stability when user demand is active. This dilemma reflects a structural shift introduced by multi-chain systems. Unlike atomic transactions in monolithic blockchains, a single user action unfolds as a sequence of sub-transactions executed across blockchains. Such work-flows introduce delayed economic coupling, two effects ab-sent from monolithic chains. A load disturbance on one chain is regulated by its own EIP-1559 feedback, but it also prop-agates through cross-chain flows into the feedback loops of connected chains. Second, cross-chain delay makes the fee mechanism respond to outdated states. The two effects trans-form stability from a local property into a systemic concern. 

Research problem. Existing models [31, 32] fail to capture the dynamics introduced by cross-chain flows. It is therefore unclear whether the monolithic stability still applies, how the safe regime of the update rule changes, and what security consequences arise when stability is lost. This motivates a dedicated multi-chain analysis. However, the multiplicative update rule makes the dynamics nonlinear, and cross-chain latencies convert them into a delayed discrete-time dynami-cal system. The stability depends on historical states and is difficult to analyze [25]. We therefore linearize the dynamics around the load target to derive a local asymptotic analysis, and simulate the full nonlinear system to quantify the security consequences for realistic multi-chain architectures. 

Our contributions. We systematically analyze the stabil-ity of EIP-1559 in multi-chain systems. The instability we identify is structural . Within the unstable regime defined by the system parameters, it arises without any adversary, and once a single chain crosses its own safe bound, it propagates to conservatively configured chains. Building on the vali-dated mechanisms that incur instability, we further propose a protocol-level mitigation. Our contributions are threefold. • Trade-off characterization. We characterize the responsiveness-stability trade-off, which is manageable in monolithic settings but destabilized in multi-chain systems. Suppressing responsiveness, as in Polkadot, degrades into an inefficient first-price auction, while re-taining high responsiveness, as in Cosmos, can incur severe security outcomes once the chain becomes active. • Stability analysis (Section 6). We formalize the dy-namics induced by cross-chain interactions through a multi-chain model. We conduct an asymptotic stability analysis. The analysis identifies delay-amplified over-correction as the instability mechanism, where cross-chain latencies cause negative corrective feedback to act on stale, larger past deviations. This over-correction is stronger with higher cross-chain inbound loads. • Experimental validation (Section 7). The safe regime of the EIP-1559 update rate, ∆safe , shrinks by 84.8% rel-ative to the monolithic baseline. The standard rate of 0.125, which keeps a monolithic chain stable, causes in-stability that reaches the deepest stage of the oscillation. About half of all blocks saturate the gas limit, creating consistently large blocks that the system does not pro-vision. The deployed rates of Cosmos Hub and Osmo-sis already exceed their safe bounds, and the saturation raises their block failure rate by an order of magnitude. 

## 2 Background 2.1 Security Implications of Instability 

Stability of EIP-1559 is a security requirement explicitly specified in the EIP-1559 official document [16]. Ethereum adopted EIP-1559 and doubled its gas limit, setting the target normalized load to 0 .5, so the target load equals the pre-EIP-1559 capacity. Under stable operation, the load asymptotically converges to the target. Under instability, the load instead al-ternates between two levels, one below the target and one above it, and the two levels spread apart as the configuration moves further past the stability boundary. Finally, blocks al-ternate between the gas limit and near-emptiness [31], and the share of blocks at the limit grows as the instability deepens. In the extreme case, more than half of the blocks reach the gas limit. EIP-1559 sets the gas limit at twice the target, so occasional full blocks are expected by design, but it does not anticipate that more than half of the blocks are full. 2This saturation has two consequences. First, it intensifies 

centralization . Hardware-constrained validators are priced out, shrinking the active validator set [11]. Second, it degrades 

consensus resilience . Larger blocks take longer to propagate and execute, and a validator that has not finished the current block cannot react to the next one. The result is an honest power split, and each consensus family pays for it in its own timing budget. The honest-majority guarantee of Nakamoto consensus weakens as blocks grow [15, 21, 24, 42]. Under LMD-GHOST, an attester must vote before a deadline, but only after execution, so a late attester casts no vote, and a block with too few timely votes is easier to override. Syn-chronous BFT [1] assumes message delays below a threshold, and added latency can break this security threshold. Partial-synchronous BFT [23,34,36,46] triggers costly view synchro-nization [34] once delays exceed the timeout. Two past incidents show that unprovisioned load is severe. The 2016 Shanghai DoS attacks exploited Ethereum’s under-pricing of the I/O cost of EXTCODESIZE and continuously cre-ated slow-to-execute blocks, reducing the block-production rate 1. Ethereum’s gas refund mechanism allowed a load of up to twice the gas limit 2, so EIP-3529 removed most of the refund due to security concerns, and EIP-7778 3 was proposed to eliminate the residual refund that still introduces unpro-visioned load. Both cases required either an adversary or a mispriced opcode. Multi-chain instability produces the unpro-visioned load with more than half of the blocks being full just from an update rate where monolithic chains are stable. 

## 2.2 Multi-Chain Architectures 

Polkadot and Cosmos are two representative systems. At a high level, such systems execute a single user action as a 

cross-chain workflow , namely a sequence of dependent sub-transactions distributed across distinct chains. This introduces structural constraints across chains. Consider a two-chain workflow. A sub-transaction is initiated on source chain i at time t (measured in block steps). The system generates a cryp-tographic inclusion proof and relays it to destination chain j,incurring a rigid physical latency d determined by consensus finality and relay overhead [7, 26], and the destination-side sub-transaction commits on j at time t + d.

Past the stability boundary, the load splits into two branches around the target, and the branches spread apart as δ grows, until they pin at the gas limit and near zero. The progression contains two stages. In the unsaturated stage , right past the boundary, the peak load stays below the gas limit, and no block is ever full. In the saturated stage , the peak load reaches the gas limit, and a share of blocks are full. The unsaturated stage is faint in the prior diagrams, and the reason is that the prior analyses assume users have similar valuations for block inclusion. Therefore, a fee below the common level lets every user in, and a fee above it lets none in, so the load pattern enters the saturated stage shortly after instability. However, valuations on deployed chains are instead dispersed. The fee response spreads over a wide price range, and on-chain estimates measure it as an elasticity [38]. We therefore model the fee response through the demand elasticity and a non-strategic demand function. Monolithic chains sit inside the stable region and enter neither stage. We find that cross-chain coupling pushes configurations that are stable as a single chain into the saturated stage, and Section 7.2 shows that some reach the far end of that stage at Ethereum’s δ = 0.125.

We survey the two largest multi-chain systems, Polkadot and Cosmos. We select chains that explicitly adopt the EIP-1559 feedback mechanism and carry substantial cross-chain traffic, and examine the dynamics on their peak-activity day in 2025. Since EIP-1559 updates the base fee per block and block intervals are below tens of seconds on those chains, a single day yields thousands of block-level observations, sufficient to reveal the pattern.

## 4.2 Delayed Economic Coupling 

The workflow structure in Section 2.2 introduces two systemic effects that the single-chain model does not capture. First, the workflow from chain j to i consumes gas twice, on the source chain j at time t and on the destination chain i at time t + d

as inbound load. The cost of a user action therefore depends on the fees of all chains involved, and a fee change on one chain alters cross-chain demand and the loads on connected chains. We term this effect economic coupling . Second, the two consumptions are separated by the rigid latency d, so inbound load on chain i at time t reflects price conditions on 

j at time t − d rather than the current state. We refer to this temporal misalignment as feedback lag .These two effects compose into a closed feedback loop across chains. When chain i’s fee change shifts the load on a connected chain j, chain j’s EIP-1559 controller adjusts its own base fee, which in turn modulates the cross-chain traffic flowing from j. Each chain’s local controller remains a negative-feedback loop, but the loops are wired together through delayed cross-chain traffic. Stability becomes a sys-temic property of the coupled network, governed by coupling strength and delay magnitude, two factors absent from δλ < 2. Capturing these dynamics requires three extensions, includ-ing load decomposition into local, outbound, and inbound components, a demand specification responding to multiple prices, and an explicit latency model. 

## 4.3 Research Questions 

The above discussion leads to two central questions. • RQ1: How does delayed economic coupling alter the stability condition, and which mechanism drives it or propagates it across chains? • RQ2: Does this coupling erode the safe update rate regime, and if instability occurs, how severe is the result-ing load pattern and what harm does it do to consensus? We answer RQ1 through formal modeling (Section 5) and stability analysis (Section 6), and validate the driving and propagating channels experimentally (Sections 6.4 and 7.4). We answer RQ2 through evaluation of the mechanism that causes instability in Section 7.

## 6.3 Potential Instability Pathways 

Beyond the local Channel (I), the recurrence (Eq. 6.2) in-cludes three additional channels that the monolithic case does not capture. Each opens a potential route to instability. We group them into two candidate pathways. Whether either is practically dominant is outlined in Section 6.4. 

Pathway 1: Delay-amplified over-correction (Channel (III), Figure 2). In a multi-chain system, the stabilizing force of EIP-1559 becomes composite. Evaluation overview. Sections 7.2 and 7.3 assume a uniform 

δ. Section 7.2 quantifies how delayed coupling erodes the stability boundary and measures which oscillation stage the resulting configurations land in, and Section 7.3 maps the load pattern to consensus security. Section 7.4 then turns to non-uniform update rates, and shows that each chain’s boundary is governed by its own update rate and load structure, and an unstable chain destabilizes peers that stay inside their own boundaries. ## 7.1 Experimental Setup 

We use a discrete-time nonlinear simulator, producing a full trajectory of blocks. Simulating such a trajectory requires evaluating demand at every price the system may enter, so we extend the local elasticity into a global one, G ∝ x−λ for all prices x. Appendix B.3 evaluates a variant whose elasticity varies with the price and shows that the extension does not affect our results. Because the EIP-1559 controller observes only the load value, the simulator models demand directly without generating exact transactions, and base fees start at 

x∗ 

> i

= 1. Moreover, when the price falls well below equilibrium, the demand arriving in a single step can be many times a full block, so we assume a mempool to cache the loads. A real mempool is finite, so we cap ours at Q block gas limits and drop arrivals beyond it. The default pools of go-Ethereum, Cosmos CometBFT, and Polkadot Substrate hold a few thou-sand transactions, tens of blocks at current occupancies, so we set Q = 20. Our results are insensitive to Q (Appendix B.4). We use simulation rather than historical traces for two rea-sons. First, on-chain traces are system outputs realized under a specific trajectory. Replaying them under different control pa-rameters (e.g., a higher δ) violates feedback integrity, because user decisions frozen under past prices would be forced to exe-cute regardless of new costs. Second, quantifying risk requires iterating each critical parameter, while realistic configurations only sparsely sample the parameter space. Intrinsic variables such as elasticity are further unobservable, and only synthetic simulation can iterate them across their full theoretical ranges. We initialize a 3-chain system with each chain’s target load set to one half of its gas limit. All chains start at equilibrium, and we apply only a one-block 1 .01 × multiplier to Chain 0’s source-side demand at the first active step of each run, af-ter which demand follows the calibrated function. With the source-side demand multiplied, the inbound traffic of Chain 0 is not directly amplified and evolves with delay, so instabil-ity arises from the delayed feedback loop rather than from an injected inbound surge. Compared with the 15 × stress in Ethereum’s tests [17], our stress is tiny, yet sufficient to move the dynamics away from equilibrium. We run each configu-ration for 50 ,000 steps and classify a trajectory as converged if its load over the final 50 steps stays within 0 .1% of the target. Since any finite-step simulation cannot exactly trace asymptotic stability, we cross-check this classification against the exact criterion ρ(Mext ) in Section 7.2, which covers the slow-convergence cases that a finite run may misclassify.

## 7.3 Security Implication 

Consensus degradation. Our simulations show that insta-bility can deliver a severe load pattern. However, whether it arises depends on the elasticity, for which these realistic chains provide no reliable estimate as their base fee is inactive. We therefore ask a conditional question, namely how much consensus risk the instability introduces once the chain be-comes active. As Cosmos chains enforce an aggressive δ, we evaluate the security implications for them. Cosmos chains typically run CometBFT, whose core is Tendermint [5], and execution lies on the critical path of its consensus. A validator can vote on the current block only after finishing execution of the preceding block. Larger blocks therefore prolong the execution, so more validators miss the voting deadline, and each block collects a lower proportion of votes. Among Cosmos chains, we select Cosmos Hub and Os-mosis, whose on-chain data allows us to relate load to the block failure rate. Concretely, each block carries a failure tag indicating whether it gathered sufficient votes. The block is not committed without enough votes. This failure is similar to an Ethereum block reorg, whose rate is low, measured at 0.112% 4. Moreover, cross-chain transactions carry a timeout deadline, so even if a transaction in a failed block can be reproposed, the added delay can make it past that deadline. We build a per-chain mapping from a block to the proba-bility of failure. We use approximately 9.5 million Cosmos Hub blocks produced between October 30, 2024 and August 5, 2026, reporting a failure rate of 0.166%, and 10.2 million Osmosis blocks produced between March 24 and August 6, 2026, which report 5.25%. Osmosis exhibits a high fail-ure rate because it sets an aggressive block interval. Ideally, the mapping should key on a block’s exact load, the gas its transactions actually consume ( GasUsed ). However, Cosmos chains pack blocks by the user-declared GasWanted , which typically exceeds the actual gas consumption. Empirically, GasUsed never exceeds 83% of the limit in our investigated Cosmos Hub traces, so a GasUsed mapping is unreliable. We instead build the mapping from the declared gas and feed our load trajectories into it. The derived failure rate is there-fore a conservative lower bound. We defer the full mapping to Appendix C.1. To construct different levels of instability, we iterate the Pathway 1 parameters of Chain 0 and δ. ## 7.4 Evaluating Non-Uniform Update Rate 

All experiments above scan the uniform δi ≡ δ. This sec-tion examines the non-uniform case, and two questions mat-ter. First, whose update rate binds each chain’s stability, the chain’s δi or a peer’s δ j? Second, is there a mechanism that 

propagates the instability, or does the instability stay within the unstable chain? The answers are as follows. First, each chain’s boundary is governed by its own δi and load struc-ture. Second, an unstable chain saturates its peers, and the propagation runs not through the unstable chain’s outbound traffic, but through the stable chains’ outbound traffic toward it, which is their Channel (II), the same route as Pathway 2-2. Channel (IV) neither creates nor forwards instability, and strengthening Channel (I) even makes a chain more stable. 

Setup. We use a three-chain system with dmax = 5 spike la-tency. For baseline evaluations in Figure 6a and 6b, Chain 0 has an inbound ratio of 80%, a local ratio of 20%. This confirms that activating Channel (II) of Chain 0 propagates the instability of peer chains to Chain 0. 

Channel (I) strengthens stability. The baseline of Figure 6a and 6b leaves one question. Can Channel (IV) of Chain 0 propagate the peers’ instability to Chain 0? In Figure 6a, the forwarding fails because λ j0 = 1.5 is too weak, so we raise λ j0 to 16 (Figures 6e and 6f). However, it demonstrates that the peer chains become more stable, with their bound moving from 0 .058 to beyond the top of the scanned grid at 0.125. We discover that strengthened Channel (I) of the peer sup-presses the peers’ own Channel (III). Consider a price regress-ing to equilibrium. While the price is above equilibrium, both channels push downward, and the stronger Channel (I) merely speeds up the return. The difference appears when the price crosses the equilibrium. ## 9 Discussion 

Methodology. Our analysis is ex ante because the weak de-mand keeps the base fees at the floor, so the controller is not ac-tive and the traces leave the elasticity unidentified. Ethereum’s blob market spent its first year in the same regime 5, described as an inelasticity horizon. Note that elasticity governs the slope of the demand curve, not the demand level, so low de-mand on Cosmos does not indicate a low elasticity. The de-mand level is a separate degree of freedom, which is extremely low on Cosmos and rarely produces a load above the target even at the price floor. Once instability arises after demand activates, it can shift the load between empty and the gas limit, affecting about two million monthly active users and several billion dollars of value across the two chains. 

Price-dependent elasticity. Our experiments above hold the elasticity constant in the price. Prior analysis has a similar assumption. Roughgarden fixes the demand curve as a static primitive [41], Ndiaye assumes a Pareto valuation distribu-tion whose elasticity is constant [38], and Crapis et al. eval-uate the elasticity only at the target point [10]. In realistic chains, elasticity may vary with prices. We redo Figure 4c in Appendix B.3 with different price-elasticity shapes. The experiment demonstrates that the shape affects neither the boundary nor the severity of the instability past it. 

Extension to the current model. We keep the smallest model in which we analyze how the instability arises and propa-gates, namely two-hop workflows under fixed delays with non-strategic demand. Each extension below would push the system toward more instability. First, our measured delay is based on the long-term statistics, while finality and relaying slow down under congestion. Since the correction budget shrinks with lag, load-dependent delay can only tighten the bound. Second, though two-hop workflows dominate, longer workflows carry more delayed loops and should amplify the mechanism. Third, we assume demand is memoryless and non-strategic, matching the monolithic analyses [31]. Hold-ing the demand model fixed attributes the instability to cou-pling and delay, rather than to a new behavioral assumption. 

> 5https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7918

Real participants, in contrast, respond with lag, for example through wallet fee estimators. Such lag is additional delayed feedback, and we expect it to widen the unstable region. 

## 10 Related Work 

Transaction fee mechanism. Research on transaction fee mechanisms (TFMs) pivoted with the introduction of EIP-1559. Prior work documented the high fee volatility and poor user experience of Ethereum’s first-price auction [9,14,27,41], and subsequent analysis confirmed that EIP-1559 stabilizes gas prices and reduces fee variance [27, 35, 41]. Within the monolithic setting, several works refine EIP-1559, for exam-ple, through adaptive update rates [39]. A separate line ex-tends TFMs to multi-dimensional resource pricing through a control-theoretic approach [10]. This paradigm now informs upgrades such as EIP-4844 [18] and EIP-8037 [19]. 

Dynamical analysis of EIP-1559. Most relevant to our work is the dynamical analysis, all confined to monolithic settings. Leonardos et al. [31] model monolithic EIP-1559 and char-acterize the stability condition, with a progression of the os-cillation patterns. Follow-ups [32, 33] bound the resulting load inflation at 6 .27%. An empirical study [35] confirms that monolithic EIP-1559 are stable. We take the oscillation stages and the load cap from this line and do not reprove them. What prior analysis leaves unexplored is the multi-chain setting. We identify the mechanism that drives and propagates instability, and show that the load oscillation reaches the far end of the saturated stage at an update rate at which a monolithic chain is stable. We further quantify the consensus consequences, which prior monolithic analysis leaves unmeasured. 

Game-theoretic fee mechanism design. A separate line tar-gets a fee mechanism design problem. Lavi et al. [30] first analyze the Bitcoin fee market with strategic participants, covering miner manipulation and side payments. Roughgar-den [41] develops a general formalism and introduces spe-cific incentive compatibility notions. Chung and Shi [8, 9] refine collusion-resilience and establish impossibility results. Other works extend the analysis to base-fee manipulation [2], MEV-aware block producers [3], and multi-proposer architec-tures [22]. This line is orthogonal to our analysis. 

## 11 Conclusion 

In this work, we examine the stability of EIP-1559 in multi-chain systems. We model the chains as a delayed dynamical system, and show that delayed cross-chain feedback strictly shrinks the safe regime relative to the monolithic baseline. We identify and validate the mechanisms that drive instability and propagate it across chains. On realistic configurations, the deployed update rates of Cosmos Hub and Osmosis already exceed their safe bounds. We finally propose a protocol-level mitigation that cancels the destabilizing delayed feedback. 13 Open Science 

We release the evaluation artifact behind this paper 6. The artifact holds the code and the data to reproduce every experi-mental figure and table in the paper. 

Layout. The artifact has three parts. The first part is the sim-ulator. It is a discrete-time nonlinear model of N coupled EIP-1559 chains, written in Go, and it runs every simulation in the paper. ## C Real-World Statistics C.1 Load-Failure Mapping 

Section 7.3 collects block statistics from Cosmos Hub and Osmosis, and maps a simulated load onto a block failure probability. We describe the mapping here. A block fails when it does not gather two-thirds of the votes, which CometBFT records as a non-zero round index in the commit message. We count these events over each chain’s history, which yields 15 ,831 events on Cosmos Hub over 9 ,515 ,098 blocks and 534 ,882 events on Osmosis over 10 ,186 ,290 blocks. Execution lies on the critical path, so the quantity that delays a vote on block h is the load of block h − 1. We key the load on GasWanted, for the reason given in Section 7.3, and normalize it by the block gas limit. We group the block history into 100 load bins of width 0 .01. For each bin, we divide the failures whose predecessor falls in that bin by the blocks in that bin, thereby estimating one failure probability per bin. The numerator is exact, because we hold every failure event. For the denominator, we count blocks at two rates. At low load (less than 0 .5) blocks are plentiful, so we count every 50th block of the history and multiply the count by 50. At high load, there are fewer blocks, so we count all of them. The per-bin rate rises with load over most of the range. Where it does not, we pool the affected bins by isotonic regression, because a heavier block is expected to receive fewer votes. 

## C.2 Cosmos and Polkadot Statistics 

The collection of real-world operational data serves a critical objective in our study. It validates the parameter ranges used in our general stability analysis, ensuring that our iterations of cross-chain ratios, latencies, and elasticities cover the realistic operating conditions of modern multi-chain blockchains. However, acquiring high-quality, explicit data for individ-ual chains presents significant challenges. While systems like Polkadot and Cosmos host hundreds of parachains and zones, a vast majority remain inactive, and centralized data aggrega-tors often report only system-wide statistics, which obscures the specific load dynamics of individual chains. To address this, we conducted a detailed investigation. Specifically, we combine blockchain explorer data with direct on-chain mea-surement. For Polkadot, we extract XCM message counts and total load from Subscan . For Cosmos, we sample blocks from archive nodes and classify every transaction by its IBC mes-sage content, which yields gas-denominated traffic shares. We take the update rates δ from official protocol documentation and runtime code on GitHub, and we measure block intervals and cross-chain latencies directly from on-chain data. This effort allowed us to reconstruct the operational profiles of the most active and representative chains, with a summary presented in Table 2. 

Cross-chain ratio. A straightforward proxy for cross-chain ratio is the message count. However, it is an imperfect proxy 24 for load. An XCM message of Polkadot does not map one-to-one onto a sub-transaction, and IBC packets of Cosmos batch transfers. We therefore measure the ratio directly in gas wherever the chain records it. For Cosmos chains, every transaction’s gas and message types are recoverable on-chain. We can classify every transaction by the IBC message types its body carries, and compute the inbound share of executed gas directly. However, the total blocks consume more than 1 TB of data for both Cosmos Hub and Osmosis. Therefore, we sample blocks uniformly at random inside each chain’s measurement window. The samples hold 60 ,000 blocks of Cosmos Hub’s 2025 calendar year and 80 ,000 blocks of Os-mosis between April 15 and August 6, 2026. For Cosmos Hub, packet-receive transactions execute 12 .9% of all gas, and IBC infrastructure transactions (client updates, acknowledgements, timeouts) execute another 8 .7%. We thereby assume an in-bound ratio of 21 .6%. Outbound transfers execute 2 .4%, so the total cross-chain share measures 23 .9% in gas. For Osmo-sis, the same three classes execute 15 .7%, 9 .1%, and 1 .3%, giving an inbound ratio of 24 .8% and a total cross-chain share of 26 .1%. We sample this quantity because to get the gas share, we need the transaction bodies and the per-transaction gas of every block. Sampling is enough here. Drawing the sample again, we would land within 0 .5 points of 21 .6% on Cosmos Hub and within 1 .1 points of 24 .8% on Osmosis. We also reran the stability analysis at both ends of those two intervals. Cosmos Hub’s maximum stable update rate moves between 0.061 and 0 .052, and Osmosis’s between 0 .023 and 0 .017, so both chains stay unstable at their deployed rates throughout. For Polkadot parachains, per-message gas attribution is not publicly recoverable, so we retain the message-count proxy from Subscan . The proxy overcounts multi-message actions and therefore only overestimates coupling. We use it as an explicit upper bound. 

Update rates. We extract the precise EIP-1559 update rates currently employed by these parachains and zones from their official documentation and codebases. For the selected Cos-mos zones, the update parameters are explicitly defined. Con-versely, determining the exact rates for Polkadot parachains presents a challenge, as many projects do not enforce docu-mentation for internal fee parameters. We attribute this opacity to the modular nature of Polkadot, which provides ready-to-deploy Substrate templates that developers often adopt with-out modification. To address this, we traced the initial default configurations inherited by these chains (sources verified in Table 2). Our investigation confirms the universal adoption of an extremely conservative δ, a design choice that is also explicitly acknowledged within the protocol’s technical speci-fications 7.

Gen-erally, the process involves three sequential phases: 1) Proof generation, where the source chain generates a commitment proof after a stabilization period to ensure finality (e.g., the

We measure this delay through on-chain IBC packets for Cosmos. IBC packets carry a chan-nel identifier and a sequence number, so the transaction on the destination chain can be matched exactly to the original send. The delay in blocks is then the elapsed time between the two commitments divided by the destination chain’s block interval. For both chains, we measure packets across each chain’s active channels, and report the average number of steps it takes to receive a packet from other chains. For Cosmos Hub, we observe every IBC packet that Cos-mos Hub received during the 2025 calendar year, and every packet that Osmosis received between April 15 and August 6, 2026. The windows differ because no public archive indexes Osmosis transactions earlier. For Cosmos Hub, the statistics cover 822 ,514 packets across 102 channels. The mean delay is 59 .3 seconds, which is 10 .07 blocks at the 5 .89-second Cosmos Hub block interval. The traffic is concentrated and uneven. The largest channel carries 47 .2% of the packets and delivers in 7 .9 blocks on average, while the 97 smallest channels carry 27 .9% of the packets and average 12 .6 blocks. For Osmosis, the statistics cover 286 ,717 packets across 119 channels, and the mean delay is 29 .2 seconds. Osmosis’s 1 .15-second block interval places the latency at 25 .47 blocks. We set dmax to the measured mean, 10 for Cosmos Hub and 25 for Osmosis. The weights wd are a separate question. They describe how the load generated in one source block spreads over destination blocks, which the destination-side record can-not recover. Recovering it would require pairing every arrival with its source block, and therefore an archive for every source chain, which is practically less feasible. ## D Protocol-Level Migation 

This section develops the mitigation of Section 8. Inspired by the observations in Figures 6e and 6f, we add the compensa-tion term and show how it affects the governing recurrence. We then fix the exact term assuming accurate elasticities, and 25 explain how precise the estimation of elasticities must be. 

Synthesizing the counter-force. Recall the mechanism of Pathway 1. The load that chain i reads at time t mixes in the inbound flow priced at xi(t−d). Figures 6e and 6f point to the remedy. In that experiment, we raised a source-side elasticity to probe Channel (IV), and the peer chains became more stable instead. The raised elasticity also strengthened the peers’ Channel (I), and Channel (I) reverses at the moment the price crosses the equilibrium. From that moment on, it opposes the stale push of Channel (III) and erodes the over-shoot. In that experiment, however, the counter-force was an accident of a peer-facing elasticity, which no chain controls. The natural question is whether a chain can synthesize the same counter-force from data it owns. We synthesize the counter-force by re-pricing.
