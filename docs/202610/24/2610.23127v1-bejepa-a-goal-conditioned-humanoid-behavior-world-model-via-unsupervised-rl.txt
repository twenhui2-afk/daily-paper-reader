Abstract 

Humanoid control requires coordinating whole-body posture with position and heading in the world frame. Mainstream controllers imitate trajectories in the root frame, but struggle to regain the target world pose after displacement. We introduce BeJEPA , a goal-conditioned Behavior World Model learned through unsupervised reinforcement learning from human motion priors and robot interaction. BeJEPA learns a shared representation of body configuration and world placement through a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA). A Dynamic Predictor predicts the next state embedding under an action, while a Successor Predictor summarizes a goal-conditioned policy’s expected discounted future state embeddings in a single vector. During joint training, Dynamic grounds the initial representation in observed physical transitions, while Successor increasingly organizes it around long-term behavior. Their composition guides policy improvement through one physical prediction and one behavioral summary, without long imagined rollouts. In 20-second motion-tracking experiments, BeJEPA with online action refinement using Dynamic outperforms all evaluated baselines in global body-position error, reducing it on AMASS by 61.7% relative to BFM-Zero with world-pose inputs and 58.7% relative to SONIC. Physical deployment on a Unitree G1 demonstrates joint attainment of posture, world position, and heading goals, recovery from the ground, and closed-loop return after disturbances and goal changes. 1arXiv:2610.23127v1 [cs.RO] 16 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Whole-body control (WBC) is the physical foundation of humanoid applications, enabling manipulation, expressive motion, and the execution of higher-level commands (Ben et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026). It translates desired robot states into coordinated actuator commands. For world-coordinate tasks, the desired body posture must be reached together with the requested base position and heading. Existing motion-centered controllers can reproduce a posture while remaining displaced from its intended location. Correcting this displacement requires the robot to step, temporarily departing from the requested posture before reaching it at the right place. Whole-body control must therefore judge a movement by the goal it helps achieve, even when that movement initially worsens posture matching. Human motion references show coordinated movement, making imitation a practical starting point for learning WBC (Peng et al., 2018; He et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026). However, tracking a given trajectory does not explain how to reach a new goal when no trajectory is provided. This motivates reinforcement learning (RL), through which the robot can discover intermediate movements and evaluate how they help it reach the goal. Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero use unsupervised RL to learn reusable behaviors (Tirinzoni et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Given a reward that is linear in the learned state features, these methods can select behaviors to maximize return without specifying intermediate states. BFM-Zero uses body-relative features, so its learned objective focuses on posture and excludes absolute position and heading. To control posture and world placement together, the robot must learn how actions change its body and how the resulting behavior brings it closer to the goal. We introduce BeJEPA , a goal-conditioned Behavior World Model for humanoid control, learned through unsupervised RL from human motion priors and robot interaction. Its shared representation encodes body configuration, world position, and heading. A goal-independent Dynamic Predictor models one-step action consequences, while a goal-conditioned Successor Predictor summarizes the policy’s expected future state features. Early in joint training, Dynamic provides physical supervision that helps preserve useful features despite the unskilled policy’s noisy long-term targets. As the policy improves, Successor organizes these features around long-term behavior. Their composition trains the actor to choose movements by their future goal alignment, including steps that temporarily worsen posture matching, without long imagined rollouts. Dynamic also supports online action refinement for time-indexed tracking. Our contributions are threefold: • A goal-conditioned Behavior World Model for humanoid control. BeJEPA learns a shared representation of posture and world placement through unsupervised RL, supporting goal reaching and motion tracking. • A Dynamic–Successor predictor decomposition. We compose immediate dynamics with a goal-conditioned prediction of future state features, jointly learning the representation and guiding long-term policy improvement without long imagined rollouts. • World-anchored control in simulation and on hardware. The learned actor improves global body alignment on AMASS and LAFAN. G1 demonstrates joint posture and world-pose control, ground recovery, and closed-loop return after disturbances and goal changes. 

# 2 Related Work 

Humanoid behavioral foundation models. Motion imitation is a dominant route to humanoid behavioral foundation models (Peng et al., 2018; He et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2026). SONIC and Humanoid-GPT scale tracking accuracy and generalization across motions while retaining a reference-sequence interface (Luo et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026). Isolated or abruptly changed goals thus need an additional mechanism for intermediate motion. Hierarchical systems supply this motion with a separate planner (Tevet et al., 2025). Generated motions must remain within the tracker’s capabilities, while replanning frequency trades motion consistency against responsiveness (Huang et al., 2025). Meta Motivo and BFM-Zero instead infer behaviors directly through unsupervised RL (Tirinzoni et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). However, BFM-Zero’s body-relative formulation leaves world position and heading outside its learned goals. BeJEPA learns world placement through both physical and behavioral prediction, outperforming BFM-Zero retrained with world-pose inputs (Section 4.2). 2World models and latent-space control. Reconstruction-based models such as PlaNet and Dreamer allocate represen-tation capacity to observation detail, including variation irrelevant to control (Hafner et al., 2019b;a; 2025). Dreamer and the decoder-free TD-MPC family limit rollout horizons and estimate the remaining return with learned values (Hafner et al., 2019a; Hansen et al., 2022; 2024). These values depend on training rewards, so their long-term predictions do not automatically transfer to newly specified objectives. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn by predicting state embeddings, allowing abstraction from unpredictable observation detail (Assran et al., 2023; 2025). Yet planning with latent dynamics, as in DINO-WM and LeWorldModel, composes transitions and risks accumulating prediction error (Zhou et al., 2024; Maes et al., 2026). BeJEPA combines feature prediction with a directly learned, goal-conditioned summary of future state features, enabling long-term policy improvement without extended dynamics rollouts or downstream reward supervision. 

Successor representations and reward-free reinforcement learning. Successor features summarize a policy’s expected discounted future state features, separating behavioral prediction from reward readout (Barreto et al., 2017). Forward–Backward representations learn this structure to infer policies for new rewards and goals (Touati and Ollivier, 2021; Touati et al., 2022). TD-JEPA introduces joint-embedding learning of these long-term predictions from offline transitions (Bagatella et al., 2026). Acquiring informative transitions online remains a separate problem, motivating dedicated FB exploration strategies (Urpí et al., 2025). In online humanoid learning, the policy and its long-term prediction targets evolve together. Early movements may reveal little about how to reach a distant goal, although each executed action already provides an observed physical transition. BeJEPA learns these two timescales jointly: observed one-step transitions directly supervise the encoder while goal-conditioned continuation organizes the same representation over longer horizons. 

# 3 BeJEPA: A Behavior World Model 

BeJEPA learns a controller for goal reaching and motion tracking without task-specific retraining. It composes Dynamic’s one-step physical prediction with Successor’s goal-conditioned future-state feature summary in a shared representation of body configuration and world placement to guide policy improvement (Figure 2). Dynamic also refines actions online for spatial and temporal alignment. 

## 3.1 Problem formulation 

We formulate humanoid control as a partially observable Markov decision process M = ( S, O, A, P, Ω, p 0, γ ). Here 

st ∈ S is the full state, ot ∈ O is the controller’s observation, and at ∈ A ⊂ R29 specifies joint targets for a low-level proportional–derivative controller. Transitions follow st+1 ∼ P (· | st, a t), observations follow Ω( · | st), initial states follow p0, and γ ∈ (0 , 1) discounts future outcomes. The observation contains proprioception, estimated root pose, and recent sensor and action history; the full state additionally exposes simulated body kinematics. Root pose is represented as ρt = ( xt, y t, sin ψt, cos ψt) relative to a fixed reference anchor shared by current and target states. A fixed target state sg specifies goal reaching; a time-indexed reference sref

A retargeted human motion dataset Dref supplies goals and a conditional motion prior (Peng et al., 2021); this prior and task-independent physical penalties guide executable control without downstream task rewards.

## 3.3 Learning with Dynamics and Successor Prediction 

BeJEPA separates learning what an action changes from learning what a goal-conditioned policy subsequently achieves (Figure 2). An immediate physical consequence can be learned from every attempted movement, even before the robot 4can reliably reach the intended goal. and a goal-conditioned Successor Predictor Sξ (zt, z g ) ≈ Sπg (st), both operating in the shared representation. ## 3.4 Aligning Behavior in Space and Time 

Motion tracking requires the right body configuration at the right place and time . Successive goal embeddings guide the actor, but its objective evaluates discounted future similarity to each goal, leaving room for spatial offset and temporal lag. Finite-dimensional task features can also blur spatial precision, as analyzed in FB-AWARE (Cetin et al., 2024). We use Dynamic to compare a short predicted trajectory with time-indexed references and refine the actor’s actions online, using gradient-based latent MPC similar to Dream-MPC (Spieler and Behnke, 2026). Unlike prior protocols that exclude trajectories with large spatial deviations and mark them as failures, we retain these trajectories to fairly evaluate the error in world coordinates. # C Training and Network Architecture 

## C.1 Motion data and goal relabeling 

Our motion prior contains 2,000 G1 motion clips: 862 ten-second LAFAN clips (Harvey et al., 2020) and 1,138 motions from the SONIC/Bones collection (Luo et al., 2026). Together they provide approximately 4.78 hours of motion at 30 Hz, covering upright locomotion, crouching, crawling, kneeling, recovery, and expressive whole-body movement. Retargeted body states provide reference goals and expert state–goal pairs for the conditional prior; robot interaction supplies the state–action transitions used to learn Dynamic and Successor. Half of the rollout environments follow moving reference goals in 250-step windows, with randomized initial position and heading. The remaining environments collect fixed-goal behavior, including translations of up to 3 m and heading changes of up to π. Ground initializations occur with probability 0.3, providing experience for posture recovery. Replay retains the collection goal zb for Successor TD learning. For actor and CPR/AUX critic updates, we replace this goal with probability 0.8. The replacement is an encoded replay state with probability 0.4 and an encoded motion-reference state with probability 0.6. Thus the marginal training-goal distribution is 

pg = 0 .20 pbehavior + 0 .32 preplay + 0 .48 preference . (28) Replay candidates are independently reassigned by a batch permutation. This exposes policy improvement to attained states and reference behaviors while retaining goals encountered during collection.

# D Learning Dynamics 

## D.1 Convergence of posture and world alignment 

Q2 (Section 4.2) compares the final learned behaviors; Figure 7 follows their acquisition throughout training. We compare BeJEPA with the merged predictor and two split models that stop either Dynamic’s or Successor’s gradient to the encoder, evaluating their actors directly under matched tracking and fixed-goal protocols. Local posture tracking improves quickly across models, while global tracking and fixed-goal errors expose differences in learning world alignment. The merged predictor must learn immediate action effects and goal-conditioned continuation together, without a separately supervised physical transition model. The split model with Dynamic’s encoder gradient stopped benefits from separating these predictions, but its representation receives no direct physical-prediction gradient; world alignment remains slower and less accurate than in full BeJEPA. When only Dynamic among the two predictors updates the encoder, convergence is generally better than with the merged predictor or stopped Dynamic gradient. This ablation retains a trained Successor for policy improvement, but removes its influence on the representation. Its remaining fixed-goal position and heading errors indicate that physical predictability alone is insufficient for accurate longer-term goal reaching. Full BeJEPA combines physical supervision with goal-conditioned continuation learning in the shared encoder, yielding faster world alignment and lower residual goal errors.

Late learning: the objectives settle with unequal influence.
