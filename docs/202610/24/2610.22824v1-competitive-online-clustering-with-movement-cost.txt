# Competitive Online Clustering with Movement Cost

Abstract 

We study the problem of online clustering with movement cost, which generalizes the fun-damental k-server problem. At each time step, the algorithm is given a set of points in a metric space and must choose k cluster centers. The cost incurred is the sum of the clustering cost at each time step and the total distance that the centers move over time. Prior work established competitive algorithms for the case k ∈ { 1, 2} on the line metric with the k-median clustering objective; under the k-median objective, this problem is also known as facility reallocation. We substantially generalize these results to arbitrary k, general metric spaces, and a broad class of clustering objectives. Specifically, we consider clustering costs defined as a norm of the distance vector from points to their nearest center, which subsumes the k-median, k-center, and (square root of) k-means ob-jectives, and provide an asymptotically optimal O(k)-competitive randomized algorithm against adaptive online adversaries. The algorithm is based on a reduction to the k-server problem, es-tablishing a tight connection between the problems. In this reduction, k-server requests are generated by Poisson processes, whose arrival rates evolve in continuous time and are driven by a dual representation of the clustering costs. Our method of using Poissonization in this context may also be of independent interest. We demonstrate another use of the idea by deriving similar reductions for batched set chasing problems. We also consider a different family of clustering objectives defined as a norm of the vector of cluster radii, which subsumes the sum-k-radii and the k-center objectives, and give a tight 

O(k)-competitive deterministic algorithm on the line.

Clustering is a fundamental question in algorithms and machine learning, with broad applications in data summarization, facility location, resource allocation, and beyond [CG99; LV92; AGK+01; CGTS99; You00; JV01; KSS10; Kum12; CL12; LS16; AS17]. In a clustering problem, the algorithm is given a set of points (“requests”) and must select k centers so that the requests are well served by the centers according to some objective (e.g., minimizing the total distance from requests to their nearest center). In many modern settings, however, requests are not static: they arrive and depart over time, and their locations may evolve [EMS14; FKZ21; TBK07; ANS17]. Thus, an algorithm might choose to update the locations of the centers, leading to extra costs proportional to the distance moved [FKK+21; CSC23; BLY25; LHG+24; FS25]. For example, mobile disease-testing units may relocate as new outbreak hotspots emerge, food trucks may change locations to adapt to expected demands (e.g. events, lunch hours, nightlife), and edge-computing resources may be repositioned and migrated as user demand shifts across regions [KBF23; LZ23]. This forces a trade-off between the quality of the clustering at each time and the stability of the chosen centers over time, and more challengingly, the algorithm must seek the balance without knowing the future requests. Reasoning about movement without knowledge of future requests is a central theme in online algorithms. A prominent relevant example is the k-server problem [MMS88]: k servers are located at points in a metric space ( X, d ). At each time t, a request appears at a point xt ∈ X, and the algorithm must move a server to xt. The cost is the total distance traveled by the servers. The performance of an online algorithm is measured by its competitive ratio , defined as the worst-case ratio between the cost of the algorithm and that of an omniscient optimum who knows the sequence in advance. The k-server problem has long been one of the most important problems in online algorithms and has led to many deep insights [CKPV91; KP95; BCL+18; BCR23; CKZ26]. In this paper, we study the following simultaneous generalization of the clustering problem and the k-server problem. We give the most general definition here, and later specify the assumptions on the cost functions used in our results.

The CMC framework captures k-server as a special case, since a k-server request at xt can be simulated by repeated requests to the singleton set {xt}, thereby forcing any competitive algorithm to move a center 1 to xt. Conversely, [BRS21] showed that CMC with the k-median objective and 

singleton request sets can be reduced to k-server as well. They further highlighted that the problem can be viewed in the line of chasing (well-behaved) convex functions, but in a multi-server rather than the usual single-server setting. When request sets are not singletons, the situation becomes more complex. The centers must minimize the distance to multiple requests simultaneously, possibly more than the number of centers when |Rt| > k . We substantially gen-eralize these results with respect to k, the metric space, and the clustering objective. Specifically, we consider the following two fairly broad families of clustering costs. 

Norm-of-distances. Perhaps the most natural clustering cost is a norm of the vector of distances from each request to its nearest center. This subsumes the k-median objective (sum of distances) via the ℓ1-norm, the k-center objective (maximum distance) via the ℓ∞-norm, and the square root of the k-means objective via the ℓ2-norm. In the clustering context, the norm should naturally be monotone , i.e., if 0 ≤ ui ≤ vi for all coordinates i, then ∥u∥ ≤ ∥ v∥, and we will henceforth assume monotonicity. We also show in Theorem A.1 that no competitive algorithm exists without the monotonicity assumption, even when k = 1. 

Norm-of-radii. An alternative notion is that the costs are associated with the clusters as a whole instead of individual requests, and we consider the clustering cost to be a norm of the vector of cluster radii. This subsumes the sum-k-radii objective [FK12; CP01] via the ℓ1-norm and the k-center objective via the ℓ∞-norm. 2 For this objective, in addition to monotonicity, we require the norm to be symmetric, i.e., invariant under permutation of centers. Note that in the norm-of-radii model, the cost is determined not only by the center locations but also by the association of requests to centers. Our results for this model are specific to the line metric, and we show in Appendix D that on the line it is optimal up to a constant factor to associate each request with the closest center, so we may assume this association at the cost of a constant factor loss. For other metrics, one could alternatively study a model where the association is chosen by the algorithm; we discuss this matter (and how nearest association can be suboptimal in general metrics) in Appendix D. 

Adversary Models. The performance of an online algorithm is measured by the competitive ratio against an adversary who generates the requests and also serves them. We consider both oblivious and adaptive online adversaries [BBK+94]. An oblivious adversary fixes the entire request sequence σ in advance, before seeing the algorithm’s randomness. An adaptive online adversary may choose each request based on the algorithm’s past actions, and has to serve it immediately (and thus may not be optimal in hindsight). For deterministic algorithms, the two types of adversaries are equivalent. 

1.1 Our Results and Techniques 

We show that, for randomized algorithms against adaptive online adversaries, CMC with the norm-of-distances objective is no harder than the k-server problem. 

Theorem 1.2 (Norm-of-Distances, Randomized, General Metrics) . There is a randomized O(k)-competitive algorithm for online CMC in the norm-of-distances model against adaptive online ad-versaries. 

The bound is tight due to a lower bound of k for the k-server problem [MMS88] (which also holds for randomized algorithms against adaptive online adversaries [RS94]). It is the first competitive algorithm for the problem for general k. This answers an open question from [FKK+21], whose previous online algorithm only handled the case k = 2 on the line with the k-median objective.

Poissonization (roughly, replacing a fixed number of events by a Poisson process) is, of course, a known technique for simplifying dependencies [JS98; Kin93; MU17], and it has also been used, for example, in developing algorithms for stochastic online matching (e.g., [HS21; JL14; QFZW24]). A key difference is that those applications typically concern intrinsically stochastic settings (e.g., Poisson arrivals as in stochastic matching or secretary/prophet-type models), where randomness is part of the input. In contrast, our CMC problem is deterministic, and we use Poissonization to isolate each request’s marginal contribution and aggregate these contributions independently into a randomized reduction. From this perspective, it plays a role somewhat reminiscent of rounding: the marginal contribution specifies fractional request intensities, and the Poisson process converts them into an integral request stream, in a spirit similar to [ANS17]. Moreover, unlike usual settings with fixed Poisson rates, the instantaneous rate assigned to each request r evolves in continuous time and is state-dependent , determined by its norming functional for the current clustering cost. This continuous-time Poissonization transforms each discrete batch into a time-dispersed stream that allows relating the clustering norm to the expected infinitesimal movement of the induced k-server process. We believe this idea may be of interest more broadly for “linearizing” batched objectives into continuous-time online processes. While the deterministic algorithm for norm-of-radii is similar to the algorithm for norm-of-distances, the analysis requires some new ideas. The challenge is that, unlike in the norm-of-distances setting, an offline center may simultaneously account for the farthest requests of several online clusters while paying only a single radius . As a result, a naive charging argument from online centers to offline centers can overcount the offline cost and fail to yield a valid lower bound. We overcome this by exploiting the geometry of intervals on the line, together with the convexity and symmetry of the norm, to show that such overlaps can still be controlled within a constant factor.

1.2 Related Work 

Online Clustering. Beyond facility reallocation [KW22; FKK+21], there is also a line of work on online learning for clustering, where the requests are revealed after the centers are chosen. In this line, the movement is usually free, and regret guarantees are against an optimal static set of centers [FPS21; CGKR21; HGKP25]. With movement costs, [CSC23] studies the k-median objective, but still compares against an offline benchmark with fixed centers. In our setting, the requests are revealed before the configuration is selected, and we focus on the competitive ratio, which compares against an optimal offline algorithm that could move (and pay movement costs) as we do. Another closely related line of work focuses on consistent k-clustering [FLNS21; BLY25; CJWZ25; GKLX21; LHG+24; FS25]. There, a set of k centers must be maintained over a stream of insertions and deletions of requests such that at any time t, the centers form a (constant) approximation of the optimal clustering of the current requests. A main difference is that the movement is free, up to a restriction on the number of changes of the centers (usually eO(k) in total when requests only arrive and O(1) per request when requests could arrive or leave). 

Facility Location. When there is no fixed number of centers but one can open centers at some cost (and cannot move), the problem becomes the classical facility location problem, which has 4been studied with both adversarial order [Fot07; Fot08; Mey01; NW13] and random-order in-puts [KNR23; Mey01]. The variant where the connection cost is the sum of radii of facilities is considered in [FK12], similar to our norm-of-radii objective. A related variant is facility location in evolving metrics [ANS17; EMS14], in which the metric space changes over time. Instead of paying to move facilities, the algorithm may reassign requests to facilities, incurring reassignment costs. The online version of the problem is studied in [FKZ21]. 

k-Server and Variants. The k-server problem is one of the most prominent problems in online algorithms. Deterministically, the competitive ratio for k-server is k [MMS88; CKZ26]. With randomization, it is possible to achieve a competitive ratio of O(log 2 k) on HSTs [BCL+18], which implies an O(log 2 k log n)-competitive algorithm on any n-point metric. Very recently, OpenAI announced an O(log 2 k)-competitive algorithm against oblivious adversaries [Ope26], matching the Ω(log 2 k) lower bound [BCR23]. The reader is referred to [Kou09] for a survey on the k-server problem, and [BCR23; CKZ26] for more recent results. Many variants of the k-server problem have been studied, including the ( h, k )-server prob-lem [BEJK17; BBCJ20], the infinite server problem [CKL17], the weighted k-server problem [FR94; BEK17; BMC26], the generalized k-server problem [KT04; BEKN18; SS06], and the k-taxi prob-lem [CK19; CP26; GKP24]. 

Metrical Task and Service Systems. The k-server problem (and all of the variants above except for k-taxi) belongs to the class of metrical task systems (MTS) [BLS92; BCR23; CL19; BCLL21]. Online CMC is also a metrical task system. Metrical Service Systems (MSS) [CL91] are a special case of MTS with costs in {0, ∞} , and can be viewed as a single server chasing a set of feasible locations. The works [BCR22; Bur96; FFK+98; CT26] study MSS with size-w sets and show that the deterministic competitive ratio is Θ(2 w), while the randomized competitive ratio is Θ( w2). A closely related problem is metrical service systems with multiple servers (MSSMS), in which each request is a set of w points and the algorithm must move so that at least one server reaches one of them. This problem is known to be difficult, and competitive algorithms are only known for the uniform metric [CV15; Feu98]. Although MSSMS and CMC both involve multiple servers responding to set-valued requests, the two problems capture fundamentally different objectives. In MSSMS, it suffices to serve one point in the request set, whereas in CMC the cost is determined by the distances from the centers to all points in the request set. 

Online Convex Function Chasing. Another related topic is online convex function chasing. The paper [BRS21] studies chasing convex functions with k servers. They give strong general lower bounds and provide upper bounds for a class of functions that they call well-sharpened . When 

k = 1, chasing general convex functions and convex bodies in Rd can be done deterministically with a competitive ratio O(d) [Sel20; AGGT20].

3.2 The Main Algorithm 

Our algorithm for more general norms follows the same basic framework as in the previous section: we seek to construct a k-server request sequence such that (i) the cost of the online CMC algorithm is upper bounded by that of the online k-server algorithm, and (ii) the cost of the offline CMC algorithm is lower bounded by that of the offline k-server algorithm. However, FarthestServe (A) relies heavily on the fact that the ℓ∞-norm is determined by a single request. For a general norm, issuing just one request is no longer sufficient. Instead, we aim to generate a sequence of requests in a way that captures the contributions of the different distances. To achieve this, we use a norming functional to linearize the norm at the current distance vector. By Lemma 2.1, for any vector x ≥ 0 and norm ∥ · ∥ that is monotone, there is a nonnegative norming functional z ≥ 0 satisfying ∥z∥∗ = 1 and ⟨z, x ⟩ = ∥x∥. The norming functional z provides a principled way to decompose the norm into contributions from the different requests. Since z ≥ 0, we can interpret its coordinates as sampling rates in Poisson processes and use them to generate a random sequence of k-server requests. Note that the configuration of the k-server algorithm is not rewound to S(ut) but remains S(t) before serving the next round. For intuition: when ∥ · ∥ is the ℓ∞-norm, only the farthest request is issued once in expectation, and we behave similarly to FarthestServe (A); when ∥ · ∥ is the ℓ1-norm, we have z(u) = 1, so the algorithm serves an i.i.d. sequence with unit rates for each request. The resulting trajectory tends to explore the batch evenly and leads to a configuration that is “good on average.” We make a few remarks about the algorithm. The Poisson process is well-defined because the rates are piecewise constant, and it can be implemented by sampling waiting times from exponential distributions. Since the total rate P

Finally, it is plausible (and acceptable) that a request from the original request set appears multiple times or does not appear at all in the shadow process during the interval.

# 5 Conclusion and Discussion 

In this work, we study online clustering with movement cost under two natural objectives. For the norm-of-distances objective, we establish a tight connection to the online k-server problem. In particular, we give a randomized O(k)-competitive algorithm on general metrics, substantially generalizing previous guarantees, which were known only for the k-median objective on the line when k ≤ 2. Our main technical tool is a Poissonization-based reduction, with rates driven by a time-evolving norming functional, which may be viewed more broadly as a way to linearize batched requests into a stream of singleton requests, and we extend it to yield a general reduction for chasing problems. For the line metric, we further obtain deterministic O(k)-competitive algorithms for both the norm-of-distances and norm-of-radii objectives. An interesting direction for future work is to un-derstand whether similar deterministic guarantees can be extended beyond the line, perhaps by adapting the Work Function Algorithm and its analysis. Another interesting direction, specific to the norm-of-radii objective, is to work beyond sym-metric norms, even on the line. As we discuss in Appendix E, this appears to require some funda-mentally new ideas. The asymmetric version shares some flavor of the weighted k-server problem, though competitiveness for an asymmetric norm-of-radii objective would not by itself imply a competitive algorithm for weighted k-server, since the movement costs of different centers remain identical.

# E (A)symmetric Norm-of-Radii Objective 

In the analysis of our algorithm RadAggrDC , we needed symmetry to permute the coordinates of the cost vector. We now explain that, in order to consider asymmetric norms, something funda-mentally different from the Double Coverage algorithm is required. In particular, algorithms like Double Coverage will never swap the relative order of two centers, which may be problematic in the weighted case. Consider the following example with 2 centers and the norm being B · rad 1 + rad 2 for some large 

B. The expensive center c1 starts on the left. Consider three requests with coordinates 0 , 1, B .The requests are repeated so many times that we only care about the clustering configuration that we converge to. An optimal offline algorithm will switch the locations of the two centers to make the cheap c2 cluster {0, 1} and the expensive c1 cluster B alone, yielding a total clustering cost of 33 1/2. If we do not switch (as in the Double Coverage algorithm) and keep the expensive center on the left, then we pay at least Θ( B): either the expensive center clusters {0, 1}, or the cheap center clusters {1, B }, which could be arbitrarily more expensive and result in an unbounded competitive ratio. 34
