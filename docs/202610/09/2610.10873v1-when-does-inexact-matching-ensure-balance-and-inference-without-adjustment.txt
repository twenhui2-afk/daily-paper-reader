# When Does Inexact Matching Ensure Balance and Inference without Adjustment? 

## Ying Jin 

## yjinstat@wharton.upenn.edu 

## Department of Statistics and Data Science, University of Pennsylvania 

Abstract 

One-to-one matching without replacement is a classical approach to constructing comparable treated and control samples in the design of observational studies. It pairs each treated unit with a distinct control while minimizing a covariate distance objective. With continuous covariates, the matched pairs generally remain inexact, which contributes to bias in downstream analysis. Its key theoretical properties, such as the resulting imbalance between matched pairs and when it is negligible to support valid inference, remain unclear. In this paper, we analyze one-to-one matching based on d-dimensional, continuous covariates with a quadratic covariate-distance objective. First, we find that when d ≤ 3, under standard conditions on the propensity score ensuring abundant control samples near each treated sample, the imbalance (difference between within-group averages) is root-n negligible uniformly over the family of smooth functions with a common first- and second-order derivative bound. However, such balance is subject to a dimension restriction, as we construct examples in which the imbalance is root-n non-negligible when d = 4 and dominates root-n rate when d > 4. Second, we show that when d ≤ 3, the matched design allows valid Wald-type and bootstrap inference for the average treatment effect on the treated, distributional treatment effects, and quantile treatment effects. Thus, the same outcome-blind matched design supports various downstream inferences without having to tailor the design to the targets. Finally, paired randomization inference based on the matched design is asymptotically valid in the super-population sense for d ≤ 3 but can fail when d = 4. We corroborate the theoretical results with numerical experiments. 

# 1 Introduction 

Matching is widely used in the observational studies to construct comparable treated and control groups (Ru-bin, 1973; Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1985; Rosenbaum, 2020). One canonical form is one-to-one matching without replacement, which pairs each treated unit with a distinct control using their pre-treatment covari-ates (Rosenbaum, 1989) while minimizing certain objective for the distance between matched pairs. In this process, the pairs are formed before the outcomes are examined, thereby separating the design and analysis of observational studies and promoting transparency and objectivity. One criterion of a good match is the matched pairs be close enough—and ideally identical—in the pre-treatment covariates to help reduce bias from differences in observed covariates (Rubin, 1973). However, with continuous covariates, the matching generally remains inexact, and the differences in the covariates can leave bias in the subsequent analysis. A second criterion is balance between the treated and matched control groups (Rosenbaum, 1989). Even when individual pairs are inexact, their differences may cancel across pairs, making the overall bias relevant in downstream inference smaller than the individual discrepancies suggest. The key question is thus whether such bias is negligible relative to the sampling uncertainty so valid inference is possible. Establishing this connection is challenging: because assigning a control to a treated unit can change the choices available to the others, the matching assignments need to be analyzed jointly across all samples. Although matching is intuitive, transparent, and simple to describe, the implications of inexact matching for the balance of the selected groups and the validity of downstream inference remain unclear. Existing theory provides important results for matching estimators, including matching without replace-ment and inference after matching (Abadie and Imbens, 2012; Abadie and Spiess, 2022). There, a central 1arXiv:2610.10873v1 [math.ST] 7 Oct 2026 requirement for proving inferential guarantees is that the approximation error (pairwise distance) be suffi-ciently small. This requirement typically requires a large amount of control samples, yet does not necessarily follow from that alone: S¨ avje (2022) shows that the shortage of controls in a local region of the covariate space can result in inconsistency. Even when the controls are abundant locally in the entire sample space, the magnitude of the matching bias still matters. For optimal pair matching based on ordinary Mahalanobis distance, Guo and Rothenh¨ ausler (2023) establish the validity of an unadjusted paired randomization test with one continuous covariate and conjecture an extension to a dimension of three. In this paper, we study optimal one-to-one matching with a quadratic covariate-distance objective commonly used in the literature (Rubin, 1980; Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1985; Bind and Rubin, 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). We work under an i.i.d. super-population model where the covariates are d-dimensional, bounded, continuously valued, and the dimension d is viewed as fixed as n → ∞ . We assume a favorable setting for matching in which the covariates admit a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on [0 , 1] d that is bounded above and away from zero. The propensity score is smooth, bounded away from zero and strictly below 1 /2 to ensure that control samples are abundant around every treated sample, thereby separating the key analysis from the complications pointed out by S¨ avje (2022). In this regime, the treated and control sample sizes grow at the same order, so we do not require the control reservoir to grow extremely large (Abadie and Imbens, 2012; Abadie and Spiess, 2022).

94 Asymptotic validity of randomization inference 

Paired randomization tests are commonly used after matching in observational studies. They compare the observed statistic with a reference distribution obtained by independently exchanging the treated and control roles within each completed pair. Ideally, if the matching finds pairs with exactly the same covariate values, i.e., Ti = Cˆσn(i), then conditional on the covariates, the treatment is as-if randomized in the matched pair. However, with continuously-valued covariates, the matching is often inexact and leaves bias, in the sense that the treatment conditional on the matched pair is no long as-if uniform within the pair. Recognizing that matching is inexact, Pimentel and Huang (2024) proposed to modify the permutation probabilities using estimated within-pair propensity-score discrepancies. Here, our focus is instead when the randomization inference is asymptotically valid in the super-population sense, similar to Guo and Rothenh¨ ausler (2023), without outcome adjustment or propensity-score modification.

## 5.4 Randomization inference 

Finally, we demonstrate the super-population validity of randomization inference for d ≤ 3 and the dimension boundary for d ≥ 4. We use the same covariate distribution, propensity score, outcome regression, and matching procedures as in the ATT experiment in Section 5.1. We consider two null settings. Assigning a control to one treated observation changes the controls available to the others. Consequently, the local nearest-neighbor calculations do not directly apply in our setting, which necessitates distinct analyses. 

Error cancellation, bias, and balance in matching without replacement This work expands upon the classical bias-reduction theory for matching. Under ellipsoidal symmetry and related mixture assumptions, Rubin and Thomas (1992) and Rubin and Stuart (2006) establish equal-percent bias-reduction properties for affinely invariant matching. These results describe how matching improves covariate comparability but do not study the rate for empirical imbalance. Similarly, the balancing-score identity of Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) provides a population-level perspective without controlling the finite-sample discrepancy. For matching without replacement, even consistency depends on the availability of controls in the regions occupied by treated observations. S¨ avje (2022) shows that optimal matching on the propensity score can be inconsistent when some regions have too few controls. We rule out this complication by assuming the propensity score is strictly bounded below 1 /2, providing a clean basis for analyzing the matching bias in large samples. Guo and Rothenh¨ ausler (2023) study the finite-sample matching bias using ordinary Mahalanobis distance, similarly assuming the propensity score is strictly bounded below 1 /2. They establish asymptotic validity of an unadjusted paired randomization test with one continuous covariate, conjecture validity with up to three, and give a failure example for d = 4. Our results establish a quadratic counterpart, yielding dimensionality results echoing their conjecture. Second-order bias and the relevance of dimension four also have nearest-neighbor precedents. The recent work of Viel et al. (2025) derive higher-order bias bounds and expansions under geometric and smoothness conditions in the NN matching setting, yet matching without replacement requires distinct analysis techniques. Our contribution is to show that the global use-once constraint does not create a first-order bias, and to control the resulting signed imbalance uniformly. 

Inference from matched samples. Inference after matching without replacement already has a substantial foundation. Abadie and Imbens (2012) derive a martingale representation, establish an asymptotic normal distribution for a without-replacement matching estimator, and give a consistent paired-difference variance estimator. Abadie and Spiess (2022) develop post-matching inference that allows regression misspecification and justify resampling complete matched sets (similar to our bootstrap procedures). These works isolate the approximation error from inexact matching, often assuming sufficiently small absolute distance, defined as 16 An = 1

> m

Pmi=1 ∥Ti − Cσ(i)∥. A sufficient condition for the results in Abadie and Imbens (2012, Theorem 1) and Abadie and Spiess (2022, Assumption 3) is √m A n = oP (1). The primitive sufficient conditions in Abadie and Imbens (2012, Proposition 1) include mr /N = O(1) for some r > d , requiring a control pool that grows much faster than the treated sample. By contrast, when m ≍ N ≍ n and the covariates have bounded full-dimensional densities, their argument gives an N −1/d lower bound for average absolute distance with probability tending to one. Hence the absolute-distance condition cannot hold in dimensions two and above. By contrast, our theory controls the imbalance Bn,σ ⋆n (g) directly instead of establishing the distance bound, assuming quadratic objective and smoothness conditions. 

Related balancing and distributional methods. Explicit balancing offers another route to small aggregate error. Kallus (2020) formulate matching in terms of function-class imbalance and variance, while Wang and Zubizarreta (2023) establish root-n and efficiency results for matching subject to aggregate balance constraints. Their asymptotic analysis allows replacement and directly imposes aggregate balance constraints. Our procedure concerns minimizing the matching objective only, without any other constraints. The uniform balance is a consequence of minimizing geometric cost. Our inference procedures also connect to a literature for counterfactual distribution and quantile effects, including regression-based (Chernozhukov et al., 2013), inverse-propensity weighting-based (Donald and Hsu, 2014), and matching-with-replacement based (Yang and Zhang, 2023) procedures. We provide a justification for paired bootstrap after inexact matching without replacement. 

# Discussion 

In this paper, we study when inexact one-to-one matching without replacement, on its own, yields balance sufficient for valid inference. For the quadratic objective with d ≤ 3 continuous covariates and abundant local control samples, the empirical imbalance between the treated and matched control samples is oP (n−1/2)uniformly over smooth functions, even though individual pairs may remain at distance of order n−1/d . As a consequence, a single outcome-blind matched design supports Wald-type and bootstrap inference for the ATT, distributional and quantile treatment effects, and paired randomization tests, without the need to tailor the design to the target or to fit outcome models. We also show that the dimension restriction is sharp: for d = 4 the imbalance of a fixed smooth function is non-negligible at the root-n scale, and for d > 4 it dominates the root-n scale, even under the same favorable conditions. The practical implication of this restriction is that we may rely on matching alone when few continuous covariates are matched inexactly. When there are discrete features, the matching could be performed exactly in these discrete features while being inexact in the continuous ones. Otherwise, our analysis suggests that one may need to reduce the dimension of the matching variables or combine matching with other adjustments such as outcome regression. Finally, our analysis relies on the quadratic structure of the objective, and whether the same results hold for the ordinary Mahalanobis distance, as conjectured by Guo and Rothenh¨ ausler (2023), remains open as future work.

## B.1 Matching as bipartite graph and its stability 

For preparation, we represent the matching problem as bipartite graph and prove its stability when replacing one treated/control data point. Such stability will later be used together with the Efron–Stein inequality (Lemma G.2) to bound the imbalance. These will be useful in proving several supporting lemmas in Section A.1. 

Matching and bipartite graph. Keep 0 < n 1, n 0 < n fixed. Any matching, an injection σ from {1, . . . , n 1}

to {1, . . . , n 0}, can be equivalently represented by a graph in which the n1 treated units are put on the left side and the n0 control units are put on the right side. Each unit is a vertex. An edge connects a treated unit with a control unit, i.e., Ti and Cσ(i). A control unit is connected to a treated unit if and only if they constitute a matched pair. The matching can thus be represented by the graph consisting of the vertices and edges. Since we consider one-to-one matching, each edge joins two vertices from distinct groups, so this is a bipartite group. Each treated vertex/unit must be connected by some edge to another control vertex/unit (having degree 1), but a control vertex may have degree 1 or 0. Let cij be the cost of pairing treated unit i with control unit j. This gives the cost matrix, a n1-by-n0

matrix c = ( cij ), with each row for a treated unit and each column for a control unit. For the quadratic cost (2), we have cij = 12 (Ti − Cj )⊤A0(Ti − Cj ). With these definitions ready, a matching design Mσ = {(i, σ (i) : 1 ≤ i ≤ n1} can be represented by the injection σ and equivalently the collection of edges that connects each ( i, σ (i)). ## D.2 Proof of Lemma D.1 

Proof of Lemma D.1. Take an optimal matching M1 for treated units in S and an optimal matching M2 for treated samples in S′ ∪ { x}. We use the graph language in Appendix B.1, viewing each as a graph with edges between matched pairs. We color the edges from the two matching in distinct colors, where two copies of any common edge are preserved. Starting at x, we follow its M2 edge to reach a control. If this control is unmatched in M1, then stop; otherwise, we follow its M1 edge to a treated vertex in S, and then that vertex’s edge in M2. We continue this process until stopping at a control vertex that is unmatched in M1.Every treated vertex reached after x belongs to S and has edges in both M1 and M2. This component must be a path, because along the way the vertices reached must alternate between a control matched in M2 and a treated vertex matched to the preceding control in M1, and finally stop at a control unit matched in M2 but not in M1. This path cannot be attached to a cycle because every internal vertex must have a degree of 1 and the vertex x has a degree of 1, while attaching a cycle would create a vertex with degree at least 3. By construction, edges along this path alternates between M1 and M2. We now exchange colors along the path. The first matching now covers treated vertices in S ∪ { x}, and the second covers treated vertices in S′. Each internal vertex loses one edge of each relevant color and gains one, so each matching of the corresponding colors remain injections. Their combined cost is unchanged. Minimizing separately over the two new treated sets gives 

CC (S ∪ { x}) + CC (S′) ≤ CC (S) + CC (S′ ∪ { x}),

which is equivalent to (50). This argument holds even if there are ties.
