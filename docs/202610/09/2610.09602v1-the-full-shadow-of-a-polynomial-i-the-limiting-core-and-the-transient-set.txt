THE FULL SHADOW OF A POLYNOMIAL I. THE LIMITING CORE AND THE TRANSIENT SET 

CHRISTIAN HÄGG AND BORIS SHAPIRO 

Abstract. The full shadow of a complex polynomial P of degree d ≥ 2 is the closure of all zeros of ( P n)(m), n ≥ 1, 0 ≤ m < dn . We prove that it consists of a compact connected limiting core containing all roots and critical points of P , together with isolated transient zeros, each occurring at only finitely many powers. The core is the Hausdorff limit of the zero sets pooled over all derivative orders as n → ∞ and the support of their limiting normalized root-counting measure. For m/n → α ∈ (0 , d ), we establish weak convergence of normalized root-counting measures and Hausdorff convergence of zero sets to the limiting supports, both stable under coefficient perturbations. We exhibit quartics with infinitely many transients. A bounded operator constructed from polynomial differentiators realizes the full shadow, core, and transients as its spectrum, Fredholm essential spectrum, and discrete spectrum, respectively.

The Gauss–Lucas theorem places every zero of each nonzero derivative ( P n)(m) of a positive integer power P n of a polynomial P in the convex hull conv Z(P ) of its roots; see, for example, [9, Theorem (6,1), p. 22, and Exercise 2, p. 24]. SP = KP ∪ TP , (1.1) where KP is the closed support of this limiting measure and every point of TP is isolated in SP . Repeated differentiation has been studied as a macroscopic flow of zero distributions through nonlocal transport equations and free-convolution models, including the real and rotationally invariant settings; see [15, 12, 16, 6, 7, 3]. Most of these works concern asymptotic zero distributions. In the periodic real-root setting, Kiselev and Tan [8] rigorously relate Steinerberger’s nonlocal PDE to the evolution under repeated differentiation of a class of trigonometric polynomials and obtain an all-time quantitative bound on the error between each consecutive-root gap and the gap predicted by the corresponding PDE density. Martínez-Finkelshtein and Rakhmanov develop a general Cauchy-transform flow and its Burgers/free-convolution connections in [10]; their companion chapter [11] gives a direct weighted-equilibrium treatment for zeros on [ −1, 1] with initial arcsine distribution. For a compactly supported finite measure ν, its Cauchy transform is Cν (z) := ∫ (z − ξ)−1 dν(ξ)off its support. Martínez-Finkelshtein and Rakhmanov [10, Theorem 1.1] prove convergence of such transforms outside a common compact convex enclosure for general polynomial arrays. As they note after that theorem, an exterior transform need not have a unique representing planar measure. Their result therefore does not by itself identify the full planar limit considered here or exclude individual zeros inside the enclosure. Bøgvad and the present authors established the fixed-ratio measure limit when P and P ′ have simple roots, see [2, Theorem 1.1]. Their Remark 1.3 identifies that restriction as apparently redundant, and Remark 1.8 already allows arbitrary sequences mn/n → α. Thus the sequential formulation is not itself new. The contributions here are removal of the simplicity restriction, convergence of the complete zero sets with coefficient stability, the connected structure of the limiting core, and the decomposition of the full shadow into the limiting core and transient zeros. At the lower endpoint, Theorem 4.3 also gives exact cluster counts, uniformly for every sublinear range of derivative orders: nr j − m zeros remain near a root aj , while mκi zeros cluster near a nonroot critical point ci. The word shadow also appears in [2, Section 7, item 3] for the region associated with limiting supports. The full shadow discussed here retains, in addition, all finite-level zeros; it is distinguished throughout from the limiting core. Lemma 2.4 (Boundary cycle of a full finite-graph continuum) . Let K ⊂ C be nonempty, compact, connected, and regular closed , that is, K = int K. At every vertex v ∈ ∂K , assume that the local sectors belonging to U form one nonempty proper cyclic block. Then ∂K is a single Jordan cycle in G. At each vertex it uses exactly the two half-edges that bound the U -block. Proof. In a small neighborhood of a regular point of G, each of the two sides of the edge lies entirely in U or entirely in int K, because ∂K ⊂ G. If both sides lie in U , regular closedness implies that the intervening edge also lies in U : a point of K there could not be approached by interior points of 

K. If both sides lie in int K, closedness of K puts the whole neighborhood in K. Thus a regular point lies in ∂K exactly when the two sides have opposite labels, and then the boundary is locally the entire edge. In particular, each open edge of G is either wholly in ∂K or disjoint from it. The same argument applies to the half-edges in a vertex neighborhood: a half-edge belongs to 

∂K exactly when its adjacent sectors have different labels. Half-edges between two U -sectors belong to U , so no boundary slit is overlooked. This is a nonempty finite embedded graph with degree two at every vertex. The plane is unicoherent: two closed connected sets covering it have connected intersection. Applying this to K and U shows that ∂K = K ∩ U is connected. A connected finite graph of degree two is a single cycle, and its embedded realization is a Jordan curve. This includes a loop edge or a pair of parallel edges. □
