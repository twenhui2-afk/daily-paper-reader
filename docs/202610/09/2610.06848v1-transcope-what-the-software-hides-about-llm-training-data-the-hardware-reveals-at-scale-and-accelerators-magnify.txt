# TRAN SCOPE : What the Software Hides About LLMs Training Data, the Hardware Reveals at Scale, and Accelerators Magnify 

Joshua Kalyanapu, Darsh Asher, Kaushal Mhapsekar, Bita Aslrousta, Rushiraj Chaitanyakumar Sheth, Maharshi Mukeshkumar Oza, Achyuta Kannan, and Samira Mirbagher Ajorpaz 

North Carolina State University 

Abstract —Membership is the root privacy primitive in machine learning: to date, no hardware-based out-of-distribution detection on black-box models has been demonstrated against constant-time, static neural networks with masked confidence. This paper performs the first cycle-level examination of how large language models (LLMs) and vision transformers interact with various modern microarchitecture components, including integrated accelerators, as LLMs scale in size and answers the question of whether the data that a model was trained on affects its execution footprint even without any input-dependent branch, dynamic optimization, or early exit and in constant-time models. The results confirm that the answer is yes and identify which modern hardware components, such as TLBs or on-core accelerators, reveal or amplify that effect. The results also answer whether the signal is informative enough to reliably classify the in-/vs/out-of-distribution property of membership. To understand why, we perform a systematic root cause analysis and find that the transformer’s tokenization steps, which happen during training, alter the locality of the accesses the model makes to fetch the vocabulary token later during inference and, as a result, change the page table access patterns and TLB in a previously unknown data-dependent way, causing microarchitectural state to vary significantly based on whether or not the input was in the distribution of the transformer training data. Building on the above observation, we introduce T RAN SCOPE :the first microarchitecture tool for detecting membership in-formation with low cost, no need for a surrogate model, and significantly higher robustness, e.g., 0.6 AUC for PETAL (best previously reported) vs 0.9 AUC (ours). This reintroduces hard-ware as both an opportunity, e.g., a tool for checking copyright violation for the first time, and a new channel for inferring membership (MIA). TranScope achieves a surprisingly high out-of-distribution detection accuracy of 98% (1.5% FPR, 2.5% FNR) across large-scale models ranging from 4.4 million to 1.1 billion parameters.

Out-of-Distribution Detection and Membership Inference Attacks. Out-of-distribution detection (OOD) asks whether a specific record is within the training-set distribution of a model; informally, whether the input is similar to the data the model was trained on. Out-of-distribution detection was first described by Hendrycks et al. in 2016 [40], using model outputs due to models being overconfidently incorrect on inputs not drawn from its training distribution, leading to AI safety issues described by Amodei et al. [41] which have manifested in the real world. In 2018, a pedestrian was killed by a self-driving Uber due to the software not recognizing the possibility of jaywalking pedestrians [42]. In 2016, a driver was killed when colliding with a tractor-trailer while using Tesla Autopilot, which failed to identify the tractor trailer crossing into the driver’s lane and prompt for human intervention [43]. Thus, the inability of a model to identify inputs unlike its training set to flag a human for manual intervention can yield disastrous results. Related to out-of-distribution detection are membership inference attacks (MIAs), which ask whether a specific , in-distribution input was in the training set [6], [8]. Membership is a sensitive and legally actionable fact—determining whether a cancer scan, private message, or copyrighted manuscript was used for training can establish violations of medical privacy, confidentiality agreements, or copyright. Recent head-lines make this concrete: Getty Images sued Stability AI for training on copyrighted images [44]; LinkedIn was accused of training on private messages; Apple was sued for allegedly using pirated books to train AI models [45]. Foundational MIA work [6], [8] has accumulated thousands of citations because membership is the root privacy primitive: a model that behaves differently on training-set members versus non-members enables escalation to attribute inference and, in the worst case, model inversion. TRAN SCOPE removes both constraints simultaneously while achieving over 95% membership inference accuracy in most cases. By observing 290+ hardware performance counters rather than a single timing scalar, it detects mem-bership leakage in static, branchless, production transformer networks—BERT [26], ViT [46], GPT [36], and Pythia [47]— on Intel Sapphire Rapids, without requiring any specialized accelerator or adaptive computation. We demonstrate that 27+ counter events exhibit statistically significant member-versus-nonmember divergence when timing is constant and every prior defense and attack is blind .Our 290+ event traces reveal that LLMs and vision trans-formers do not stress the processor uniformly: training famil-iarity, input entropy, and model scale reshape branch behavior, front-end delivery, TLB activity, cache locality, reservation-station occupancy, execution-port pressure, power, and latency across the full pipeline. Each counter disparity is simulta-neously a leakage signal and a design signal, motivating embedding-aware TLB partitioning, large-page-backed em-bedding tables, and branchless pooling primitives. Legal cases involving copyrighted data in AI training have proliferated: Thomson Reuters v. Ross [48], Doe v. GitHub [49], Andersen v. Stability AI [50], Getty Images v. Stability AI [44], Concord Music v. Anthropic [51], UMG v. Suno [52], NYT v. OpenAI [53], Bartz v. Anthropic [54], and Kadrey v. Meta [55]. We validate T RAN SCOPE on the same corpus at the center of the New York Times Co. v. OpenAI. 

litigation, using real copyrighted NYT articles—not synthetic benchmarks—to demonstrate that pre-2019 (potential mem-ber) and post-2019 (definite non-member) articles separate in counter space despite identical software execution.

We consider a legal threat model where TRAN SCOPE 

imposes no requirements on the victim : the attacker does not need to synchronise with, observe, or force the victim to repeat queries. In fact, there is no victim at all—the attacker, or legal auditor, is the user. 

Legal threat model (privileged auditor). The attacker down-loads a publicly-available model and runs it on hardware they own and control. This is not a hypothetical scenario; it is the default operating mode of every user who interacts with a publicly released language model today. Models such as GPT [36], LLaMA [25], and BERT [26] are distributed as open weights executable on any commodity CPU without special-ized drivers, CUDA installation, or minimum VRAM. Further-more, industry is shifting towards shipping binarized, closed-weight models runnable on commodity hardware, such as Ap-ple Foundation Models [17], Microsoft Aion Instruct [21], and Adobe Firefly [22], allowing only black-box model accesses even when running on an attacker-controlled machine. The attacker downloads the model, inputs the disputed artifact to it, and reads the hardware counters on their own machine. There is no co-location problem, no hypervisor to evade, no victim with which to synchronize, and no need for the model owner to cooperate or be aware that the measurement is taking place. The attacker controls the entire measurement stack: the hardware, the OS, the inference runtime, and the input. This is the most realistic attacker model in the side-channel literature, because it requires zero capabilities beyond those already available to every user who downloads a publicly-released model. The goal is to build a hardware footprint that separates data matching the model’s training distribution from data that does not, producing corroborating evidence for legal proceedings. The CPU is the strongest measurement platform for this purpose: Intel Sapphire Rapids exposes 263 publicly docu-mented core performance monitoring events spanning branch prediction, front-end delivery, TLB activity, cache hierarchy, scheduler pressure, execution-port utilization, retirement, and software-prefetch behavior—orders of magnitude more signal than GPU performance counters. 

Auditor and expert-witness models. In forensic settings, a court-appointed or party-retained auditor receives the ar-tifact(s) under investigation, control corpora, and controlled model access. The deliverable is a reproducible evidence bundle satisfying Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and the 

Daubert standard [56]. The T RAN SCOPE protocol anticipates both plaintiff- and defendant-assisted audits. 

Empirical validation on real copyrighted articles. We apply T RAN SCOPE to GPT on real New York Times article snippets—copyrighted content from the NYT API, not syn-thetic benchmarks. We separate articles by GPT’s disclosed training cutoff: pre-October 2019 (candidate members) versus post-2019 (definite non-members). The results reveal statisti-cally significant hardware footprint differences that appear as a monotonic trend over publication date—precisely the signal a coarse aggregate histogram would obscure. III. T RAN SCOPE 

A. Overview 

A model trained on a corpus D implicitly optimizes its hardware access pattern for inputs drawn from D. Familiar inputs access a compact, hot region of the embedding table, stressing the TLB and caches in predictable ways. On the other hand, novel inputs scatter accesses across the embedding table, accessing a larger swath of the embedding table resulting in more TLB and cache misses. These effects are invisible at the level of the Python forward pass—the same operators execute in the same order—yet are measurable via hardware performance counters. TRAN SCOPE measures the distributional distance between an inference input and the model’s training distribution, as reflected in hardware telemetry. This is why T RAN SCOPE is framed around in-distribution/out-of-distribution rather than member/nonmember: it is a statistical characterization of whether the input has the same “spirit” as the training corpus. 

Provenance witness. We define a provenance witness as a hardware-visible or model-internal signal whose behavior is statistically consistent with prior exposure to a disputed artifact, or to a narrowly defined artifact class, and which can therefore serve as corroborating evidence regarding the artifact’s origin or training influence. No single witness settles a case in isolation; when multiple witnesses shift coherently— TLB activity, token locality, and power all moving in the same direction—they collectively form a strong provenance argument. 

B. Root Cause: Tokenizer-Induced Embedding Locality 

Core claim. The counter divergence between familiar and unfamiliar inputs is not noise across independent hardware events. It is the projection of a single microarchitectural mechanism with a traceable causal chain: 

training-corpus familiarity → token-ID proximity →

embedding-row locality → cache/TLB residency →

counter divergence. 

We make this concrete with an example shown below and in Figure 4 . Suppose we have a large language model whose training cutoff was 2020. Then, if the model was trained on news articles around the cutoff time it would have encountered many headlines relating to COVID-19 (early 2020) but it would not have encountered any headlines relating to the Russo-Ukrainian war (early 2022). 

Two articles, two memory landscapes. Consider an example headline from March 2020: “COVID-19 spreads in Wuhan as cases rise; China reports new virus infections.” When the model’s tokenizer processes this sentence, it maps each word to an integer ID that serves as a row index into the model’s embedding table. Key insight. The processor is not reacting to input data semantics; it is reacting to the geometry of embedding memory accesses induced by the tokenizer. All measured counters— fewer page-table walk completions for members, fewer off-core demand reads for members, more reservation-station stalls 

for members (because loads retire faster and the back-end fills up)—are correlated projections of this single root cause. The difference is consistent across models, observable across hundreds of counter events, and invisible in end-to-end timing. 

C. Algorithm 

TRAN SCOPE is organized around four design principles: 

(P1) non-invasive measurement (the model is treated as a fixed artifact); (P2) multi-witness evidence (263 counters organized across seven subsystems form a jury, not a single gadget); 

(P3) controlled execution (core placement, SMT, frequency, prefetcher state, and input normalization form a disclosed con-figuration manifest); (P4) method separated from conclusion (output is corroborating evidence, not a binary legal verdict). The system proceeds in five stages: (1) artifact condi-tioning —defining the provenance question and constructing familiar/novel reference sets; (2) controlled execution har-ness —applying hardware configuration controls; (3) target-model inference —executing on both reference sets and the disputed artifact; (4) multi-witness footprint collection —recording counter telemetry, power, latency, and token locality; 

(5) analysis and evidence generation —computing effect sizes, subsystem grouping, and emitting an auditable report. 

D. Step-by-Step Attack Walkthrough: Example BERT 

We provide an example of the T RAN SCOPE attack on the 110.1 M-parameter BERT-base model trained for sentiment classification on 1,000 IMDB reviews [57]. 

Step 1: Reference set construction. The familiar set XF

consists of 1,000 IMDB reviews from the training split. The novel set XN consists of 1,000 sequences of uniformly sampled token IDs from the BERT vocabulary, matched in length. 

Step 2: Execution and counter collection. Each input is tokenized and padded to 512 tokens. The model executes on a single core pinned via taskset , SMT disabled, TurboBoost enabled, prefetchers at factory defaults. We collect all available performance counters on the system using PAPI [60], plus RAPL energy. 

Step 3: Key observation. Figure 7 shows the result. End-to-end latency is statistically identical ( ∼52 M cycles), yet page-table walk completions are elevated by ∼20 walks for out-of-distribution inputs—consistent with the embedding-table locality mechanism. 

Step 4: MCF score computation. For each witness w ∈ W ,compute the z-score of the disputed artifact relative to the familiar and novel reference distributions. Aggregate across witnesses to produce a subsystem-level summary and overall MCF score. 

Step 5: Evidence report. The final report includes per-counter effect sizes with 95% bootstrap CIs, a subsystem-level heatmap, the MCF score with estimated false-positive rate, and the full configuration manifest. IV. E VALUATION

C. Power Side-Channel 

Per-inference socket power (Intel RAPL, SGX enabled) reveals a third independent leakage channel orthogonal to both performance counters and timing. Figure 3 shows power nor-malised to the BERT-tiny member baseline. The member/non-member gap grows monotonically from +2 .6% at BERT-mini to +4 .5% at BERT-base, tracking model parameter count. End-to-end wall-clock time is flat to within 1.2% across all sizes, confirming that timing defenses fail—yet RAPL power provides a complementary and growing signal. 

D. BERT Member/Nonmember Classifier Study 

We collected the performance counter, energy, and power statistics for all BERT sizes on Sapphire Rapids across 100 samples of the IMDB dataset and 100 samples of verified non members of the same size which we generated with 10 repeats per sample and stacked the resulting statistics into a dataset of 2000 samples each with 1,596 features. We trained a 4-layer multilayer perceptron classifier on the the resultant data using an 80/20 train/test split, with the layers having 512, 128, 32, and one neuron, respectively. We trained for 10 epochs with a dropout of 0.4 in the first 2 layers. Table III contains our results. In most cases, we achieved an accuracy of over 90% in determining IMDB samples vs random sequences using only performance counters, regardless of model size. Similarly, we repeated the Structured v Random experiment with BERT running on Intel Extensions for Pytorch (IPEX), a backend using Intel AMX and Intel AVX-512-VNNI kernels representing modern accelerator hardware. Interestingly, we see a clear monotonic increase in AUC and accuracy for increasing model size when using the IPEX backend, implying the use of modern accelerator hardware makes T RAN SCOPE 

more effective at larger model sizes. GUIDANCE 

This paper does more than introduce a new family of side channels. Its deeper contribution is architectural : it provides one of the first detailed CPU-side characterisations of how production CNNs, LLMs, and vision transformers stress the microarchitecture, how that stress changes with input famil-iarity, and how it evolves as model size scales. Across more than 290 hardware performance counters, along with latency, energy, and power, we show that training influence does not remain confined to model outputs—it reshapes the execu-tion footprint of the processor itself. Production transformers do not stress the processor uniformly: training predictability and model scale reshape branch behavior, front-end delivery, TLB activity, cache locality, reservation-station occupancy, execution-port pressure, power, and latency across the full microarchitecture pipeline. These results therefore matter not only to privacy and security researchers, but also to performance architects and hardware designers , because each counter disparity is simul-taneously a leakage signal and a clue about where modern AI workloads stress, underutilise, or destabilise the machine. 

Translation structures. Page-table walk completions for 2 MB/4 MB pages increase with BERT model size and sep-arate familiar from novel inputs across all five parameter points. Transformer embedding-table access patterns stress the page-table walker far more than prior workloads—300+ walk completions per inference, each costing hundreds of cycles [61] This motivates embedding-aware TLB partitioning, large-page-backed embedding tables, and speculative page-walk prefetch tuned to gather/scatter access patterns. 

Speculation recovery. Pipeline clears (d ≈ 0.84 ) and 

pipeline recovery cycles (d ≈ 0.73 ) differ between familiar and novel inputs even when wall-clock time does not—further evidence that the leakage operates below the timing floor and bypasses constant-time defenses. Future CPUs targeting LLM inference could benefit from “ML-phase” hints that adapt branch-predictor confidence thresholds for out-of-distribution inputs. 

Cache hierarchy and prefetch. Embedding-table scatter drives L3 miss counts and off-core demand reads for novel inputs; familiar inputs enjoy greater cache residency because the model has been conditioned to their token distribution. Prefetcher designs that recognize embedding-table stride sig-natures could reduce both miss rates and the locality-driven leakage that T RAN SCOPE exploits. 

Scheduler pressure. Reservation-station empty cycles and 

store-buffer stalls vary with both model size and input famil-iarity, revealing phases where speculative work and memory-latency hiding interact with novelty-driven traffic. 

Power and energy. Per-inference power differs between familiar and novel inputs even when latency does not, consti-tuting a leakage channel orthogonal to timing [62] as shown in Figure 3 . Increasing training-set coverage reduces average inference energy; “training coverage” becomes a new axis in energy modeling of production LLM deployments. VI. R ELATED WORK

Hardware-assisted privacy attacks. GateBleed [13] ex-ploits Intel AMX power-gating timing for MIA on adaptive networks. FeatureBleed [69] infers private attributes from sparsity-optimized accelerators. Timing-channel attacks [10] target adaptive neural networks with observable latency vari-ation. T RAN SCOPE is the first to demonstrate membership leakage on static, branchless, production networks where timing is constant and all prior hardware-assisted attacks fail. 

Property inference. Property inference attacks [70] de-termine undisclosed properties of training data. Extensions cover GANs [71], CNNs [72], graph neural networks [73], and federated learning [74]. T RAN SCOPE subsumes property inference as a special case: when the “property” is membership in a temporal slice of a publisher’s archive, our hardware footprint provides direct evidence. 

Microarchitectural side channels. Prior work establishes that timing channels persist on formally verified kernels [75] and surveys the attack surface exhaustively [76]. Our finding that 15+ counter events discriminate training members from nonmembers while timing remains flat adds a new entry to that taxonomy: a software-optimization-mediated counter channel 

that operates below the timing floor. Our 290+ event trace dataset enables the community to compute formal channel-capacity bounds for each leaking event across three model families and two microarchitectures. 

Microarchitectural characterization of AI workloads. 

While this paper introduces a new series of side-channel tech-niques, its broader contribution is to expose the microarchitec-tural anatomy of modern AI execution on CPUs. Prior CPU characterization studies have focused on traditional server workloads; no prior work has systematically profiled how training familiarity, input entropy, and model scale reshape the full microarchitecture pipeline—from branch prediction through TLB activity and cache locality to execution-port utilization and power. This paper is therefore not only a security result; it is also a microarchitectural characterization study of production AI workloads, with direct implications for future processor optimization and hardware design. Our traces show that transformer embedding lookups generate 300+ page-table walk completions per inference on Sapphire Rapids, each costing hundreds of cycles—a bottleneck invisible to prior workload characterization and one that motivates embedding-aware TLB partitioning and large-page-backed embedding table layouts. VII. C ONCLUSION 

Membership inference is the root of privacy in modern AI. This paper establishes that hardware detectors can bypass state-of-the-art software defenses in constant time models, and alarmingly, these hardware-level leakage signals am-plify as models—such as LLMs, Vision Transformers, and CNNs—scale and as new accelerators gets integrated in CPUS. Consequently, user privacy is fundamentally vulnerable at the hardware level. As AI becomes ubiquitous across all com-puting domains, and hardware accelerators becoming more integrated, relying solely on software defenses is no longer sufficient. T RAN SCOPE takes the first step in analyzing how these privacy-compromising hardware signals manifest as we relentlessly scale and deploy these models or integrate aggres-sive on core acceleration for AI.
