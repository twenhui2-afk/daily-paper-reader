# Separating comb inequalities is NP-hard 

## Yohan Finet ∗1 and Victor Drouin-Touchette †1 1Institut Quantique and Département de Génie Électrique et de Génie Informatique, Faculté de Génie, Université de Sherbrooke, Sherbrooke, Québec, J1K 2R1, Canada October 8, 2026 

Abstract 

Comb inequalities are important cutting planes for the symmetric travelling salesman problem, yet the complexity of their exact separation has remained a longstanding question in polyhedral combinatorics. We present a reduction from 3-SAT proving that deciding whether a comb inequality is violated is NP-complete and that the corresponding separation problem is NP-hard. This result holds even when the input vector belongs to the subtour elimination polytope, every edge value is zero, one half or one and the support graph is nonplanar with maximum degree four. The reduction constructs a graph in which six-vertex ladder gadgets encode Boolean relations and cubic graphs enforce consistency among occurrences of each logical variable. For a propositional logic formula with v variables and m clauses, the constructed graph has 40 v + 70 m + 30 vertices and a linear number of positive edges. The reduction also proves hardness when every permitted tooth has two or four vertices with half of the tooth in the handle. We discuss consequences for approximating the maximum comb violation, optimization over the comb relaxation and explain why hardness of separation does not automatically transfer to larger inequality families. 

# 1 Introduction 

The symmetric travelling salesman problem (TSP) asks for a minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle in an undirected weighted complete graph. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex exactly once before returning to its starting vertex. Beyond its applications in logistics [20, 34, 39, 41], biology [7, 8, 17], electronics [6, 25, 29, 31, 38, 40] and astronomy [5, 10], the TSP has provided a setting in which many of the principal methods of combinatorial optimization were developed and tested [3]. Among these methods, cutting planes strengthen a linear programming (LP) relaxation by adding inequalities satisfied by every tour but violated by the current fractional solution [19]. Their integration with branching yields the branch-and-cut approach used in exact TSP solvers [1, 46]. The effectiveness of this approach depends in part on the ability to find useful violated inequalities. This task is called separation . An important class of such inequalities for the TSP is the class of comb inequalities (see details in Section 1.2). Introduced in their general form by Grötschel and Padberg [27, 28] in 1979 following earlier work of Edmonds [20] and Chvátal [12], the computational complexity of their separation has remained unknown ever since [3, 15]. Neither a polynomial-time exact separation algorithm nor an NP-hardness proof was known. It was however conjectured by

Several positive results supporting this conjecture established tractability for restricted comb families or related classes of inequalities. A selection of these are discussed in Section 1.2. In this paper, we prove NP-completeness of deciding if a comb inequality is violated by a reduction from 3-SAT. The reduction shows how Boolean relations, a ladder gadget and cubic graphs can be used to generate a fractional TSP solution from the propositional logic formula. The resulting fractional solution already satisfies every subtour elimination constraint and uses only the edge values 0, 12 , and 1. An important difficulty is to ensure that an arbitrary violated comb encodes a satisfying assignment. Constructing a violated comb from a satisfying assignment is insufficient since without the reverse implication, unintended teeth could also produce violations for unsatisfiable formulas. We address this difficulty by classifying every tight cut in the constructed graph and showing that each tooth of a violated comb must lie inside a single ladder gadget. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sections 1.1 and 1.2 introduce the notation and the separation problem. Section 2 presents the main theorem and its corollaries. Section 3 presents the details of the proof with Section 3.1 explaining how half-integrality constrains the teeth of violated combs and Section 3.2 describing the ladder gadget and the Boolean relation it encodes. Section 3.3 shows how to construct a weighted graph from a 3-SAT formula while Section 3.4 proves that each tooth of a violated comb must lie inside a single ladder, shows how the constructed graph can be explicitly made nonplanar and proves both directions of the reduction. Finally, we present our conclusion in Section 4.

Polynomial-time separation algorithms are known for several restricted families of comb inequalities and related classes of inequalities. Padberg and Rao [48] developed a polynomial-time separation algorithm for the 2-matching inequalities, also known as blossom inequalities, which correspond to comb inequalities whose teeth each contain two vertices. Carr [11] showed that comb inequalities with a fixed number of teeth s can be separated by solving O(n2s) maximum-flow problems, where 

n is the number of vertices. Since s may be any odd integer satisfying 3 ≤ s ≤ ⌊ n/ 2⌋, this approach does not yield a polynomial-time algorithm when the number of teeth is unrestricted. Fleischer and Tardos [21] described an O(n2 log n)-time algorithm that finds maximally violated combs when 

x∗ ∈ SEP( n) and the support graph is planar. A comb is maximally violated if its slack is −1 in Eq. (7), the largest possible violation when x∗ ∈ SEP( n). Letchford [37] introduced the larger family of domino-parity inequalities and separated these under the same planarity assumption in O(n3)time. This result was further generalized by Cook, Espinoza and Goycoolea [16] to a larger family for which a subset containing all the clique-tree inequalities can be separated in polynomial time, provided that the support graph is planar and the number of handles is bounded by a fixed constant. Caprara, Fischetti and Letchford [9] demonstrated that {0, 12 }-cuts form a family of inequalities that includes comb inequalities and showed how to detect maximally violated {0, 12 }-cuts in O(n2|E∗|)time. Fleischer, Letchford, and Lodi [22] also established polynomial-time separation for a superclass of simple comb inequalities when the fractional solution is in the subtour elimination polytope. Note that these algorithms for larger families may return violated inequalities that are not comb inequalities. Consequently, the results presented by Caprara, Fischetti and Letchford, for example, do not automatically extend to comb separation under the same assumptions since their algorithm may return a maximally violated {0, 12 }-cut that is not a comb inequality whether a maximally violated comb inequality exists or not. Other heuristic separation approaches were developed by Padberg and Rinaldi [45], Clochard and Naddef [13], and Naddef and Thienel [43]. ## 2.1 NP-completeness and proof outline 

The decision problem corresponding to the separation of comb inequalities asks only whether a violated comb exists. Here the input is a fractional point of SEP( n), represented with finite binary encoding. This promise can be checked in polynomial time by verifying nonnegativity and the degree constraints and applying the separation procedure for subtour elimination constraints as discussed in Section 1.1. Equivalently, one may define a decision language that rejects inputs outside SEP( n). In this work, we use the standard complexity classes P and NP and show a polynomial-time many-one reduction from 3-SAT to deciding if a comb is violated for an input x∗ ∈ SEP( n). A many-one reduction constructs one target instance in polynomial time and preserves both yes and no answers. A problem is NP-hard if every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time, and NP-complete if it is also in NP [24]. NP-completeness of the decision problem for 3-SAT follows from the work of Cook [14] and Karp [35]. The reduction to deciding comb violation, as illustrated in Fig 2, begins by introducing relations of the form R(a, b ; c, d ) : a + b = c + d. These relations encode the constraints of a given 3-SAT formula and are used to show that each tooth of a violated comb must be contained within a certain subset. Each relation is represented by a ladder gadget, with each port assigned a variable of the relation, as shown for R(a, b ; uj , v j ) with vertices a, b, uj and vj in Fig. 2. Each relation variable is encoded by a cubic graph (graph where each vertex has 3 edges) gadget. A graph is constructed by connecting the vertices of each cubic graph gadget to ladder ports corresponding to the same variable. Each edge of the graph is assigned a weight of 0, 12 or 1. We further show that the resulting

As every vertex of the constructed graph has weighted degree two, as required by the degree equations, and each edge has a weight of 0, 12 or 1, the degree of a vertex in the support graph can be at most four. We show that any violated comb in the constructed graph restricts how the handle can cut ladder edges and forces each cubic graph gadget to lie entirely inside or outside the handle. For these combs, we also show that each tooth must lie within a ladder. These restrictions result in having all vertices assigned to a given relation variable being either all inside the handle or all outside it. Coloring vertices inside the handle black (value 1) and those outside white (value 0) therefore assigns a Boolean value to each relation variable. The restrictions on the structure of violated combs ensure that these values satisfy the encoded relations and yield a satisfying assignment for the original 3-SAT formula. Conversely, a satisfying assignment for the formula determines compatible vertex colors, which yields a handle and a choice of teeth that together form a violated comb. Thus, the formula is satisfiable if and only if the constructed instance admits a violated comb, which leads to Theorem 1. 

Theorem 1. Deciding whether a fractional point x∗ ∈ SEP( n) violates a comb inequality is NP-complete, even when every coordinate belongs to {0, 12 , 1} and the support graph is nonpla-nar with maximum degree four. Consequently, the associated separation problem is NP-hard, even under these restrictions. 

Our reduction proves NP-hardness for vectors in SEP( n), have each of their elements in {0, 12 , 1}

and have a nonplanar support graph with maximum degree ∆( G∗) = 4. Allowing arbitrary coordinates in [0 , 1] |E| retains NP-hardness because the constructed instances are still admissible inputs. However, this does not exclude polynomial-time separation for restricted classes of inputs as illustrated in Fig. 3. For instance, when x∗ ∈ SEP( n) and x∗ ∈ { 0, 12 , 1}, violated combs are all maximally violated (see Lemma 7). Therefore, for these vectors, when G∗ is planar, the polynomial-time algorithm of Fleischer and Tardos [21] can be used so the separation problem for these inputs 6is in P. The hardness result neither establishes average-case hardness nor rules out polynomial-time separation for restricted comb families. For example, blossom inequalities admit polynomial-time separation [48].

This corollary distinguishes a bound on tooth size from a bound on the number of teeth. The latter grows with the formula in our construction, so there is no conflict with Carr’s polynomial-time result for fixed numbers of handles and teeth [11].

3.2.3 The rooted ladder 

The following fact will later exclude teeth that extend across several gadgets. 

Lemma 11. The rooted graph L◦ has no violated comb inequality. Proof. Every tooth contains at least two vertices. A comb on seven vertices therefore has exactly three teeth, with sizes (2 , 2, 2) or (2 , 2, 3). If violated, the two halves of each tooth form a tight domino by Lemma 7. A two-vertex tight domino requires its two vertices to be joined by a unit edge, by Eq. (18). The only such edges are the three rungs. In the first case, the teeth are the three rungs. Removing their semicuts leaves both rails joined through the root, so the remaining support graph is connected. Therefore, every nonempty proper handle H cuts at least one remaining edge. Since every edge in the support graph has positive weight, this edge contributes positively to the residual, contradicting the zero-residual condition (Lemma 7). In the second case, two teeth are rungs and the last tooth consists of the root and the remaining rung. This triple is tight only when the remaining rung is an end rung: its complement consists of two consecutive whole rungs, as classified by Lemma 9. The only tight split of the triple is the root versus that end rung. A pair consisting of the root and one endpoint has cut weight three and is not tight. Its semicut is thus the pair of external edges at that end. After deleting this semicut and the other two rung semicuts, the rails and the uncut end rung connect all six ladder vertices, and the root is still attached at the opposite end. The remaining support is again connected, giving the same contradiction. 

## 3.3 From 3-SAT to a weighted graph 

The reduction proceeds in the following stages. We first encode the input 3-SAT formula using Boolean relations of the form R(a, b ; c, d ). This relation is encoded using the ladder gadget introduced in Section 3.2, with its four arguments corresponding to the colors of the four ports. Lemma 10 provides the connection: a port coloring admits the required local tooth configuration exactly when its four values satisfy R, and the parity of the number of selected teeth is determined by these values. 13 As such, Boolean values of this formula will be encoded through handle membership in the resulting graph. Color one means that a vertex belongs to H, while color zero means that it lies outside H. Occurrences of the same logical variable will be connected through a variable gadget (a 

prism graph) designed to enforce a common color for these occurrences in every violated comb. This consistency property, together with the necessary restrictions on tooth structure, will be proved in Section 3.4. This realizes a correspondence between Boolean assignments satisfying the 3-SAT formula and handles equipped with compatible teeth of violated combs. The graph and its edge weights are constructed from the formula alone. No satisfying assignment is needed to build them.

3.3.2 The anchor relation 

We call the logical reference variables T and F the anchors , because they provide a common reference for interpreting truth and falsehood throughout the construction. Their names do not prescribe their handle colors. Instead, we must ensure that they receive opposite colors. In this subsection, we show that we can complement the handle if needed to obtain T = 1 and F = 0. To enforce T̸ = F , take two copies of every relation in Eqs. (26) and (27), and add one copy of 

R(T, F ; T, F ). (30) We call this additional relation the anchor relation . As a Boolean constraint, it is the tautology 

T + F = T + F , so it places no restriction on the anchor values by itself. The purpose of this relation is to control the parity of the number of teeth through its associated ladder. By Lemma 10, that ladder contributes a number of internal teeth whose parity is T ⊕ F .Each duplicated relation has identical arguments in its two copies, so their tooth counts have the same parity and their combined contribution is even. Consequently, the parity of the total tooth count is determined by the anchor relation. Requiring this total to be odd forces T ⊕ F = 1, and therefore T̸ = F .All relations are preserved by complementing every logical bit: a+b = c+d implies (1 −a)+(1 −b) = (1 − c) + (1 − d). Their parities are preserved as well. Once T̸ = F has been established, we may therefore normalize to T = 1 , F = 0 by taking the complementary handle if necessary.

3.4.1 Classification of tight cuts

# 4 Conclusion 

This work resolves a longstanding open question in polyhedral combinatorics: the computational complexity of comb separation. Although comb inequalities have played an important role in exact TSP methods since their introduction in their general form in 1979 by Grötschel and Padberg [27, 28], neither a polynomial-time exact separation algorithm nor an NP-hardness proof was known [3, 15]. Our reduction from 3-SAT establishes that deciding whether a violated comb exists is NP-complete and that exact comb separation is NP-hard. Consequently, unless P = NP , the polynomial-time separation algorithm conjectured by Padberg and Grötschel in 1985 [47] does not exist. The hardness persists under strong restrictions. The constructed vectors belong to the subtour elimination polytope, have coordinates in {0, 12 , 1}, and have support graphs of maximum degree four that can explicitly 23 be made nonplanar. Moreover, the decision problem remains NP-complete when admissible teeth contain only two or four vertices, divided equally between the handle and its complement. The result also has consequences beyond exact separation. Every violated comb at a half-integral point of SEP( n) is maximally violated. Unless P = NP , no polynomial-time algorithm can therefore always estimate the maximum violation with absolute error strictly below one half, or guarantee finding a violated comb whenever one has violation at least a fixed ε ∈ (0 , 1] (using the scaling of Eq. (6)). Furthermore, membership in the comb relaxation CP( n) is coNP-complete and exact optimization of arbitrary linear objectives over this relaxation is NP-hard under a polynomial-time Turing reduction. Several directions merit further investigation. One is to determine whether hardness persists when the input is restricted to an extreme point of SEP( n). Such a result would connect the present construction more directly to the fractional points that can arise as optimal solutions of the subtour relaxation. Another direction is to refine the boundary between tractable and intractable separation. The polynomial-time solvability of the half-integral planar case [21] motivates studying how the complexity changes with structural parameters of the support graph. This question may identify useful classes beyond those covered by existing separation algorithms. The complexity of separating larger inequality families presents a further challenge. Extending the reduction to domino-parity, clique-tree, or path inequalities requires more than containment of the comb family. Unsatisfiable 3-SAT formulas must produce vectors satisfying every inequality in the larger family. Finally, despite the worst-case hardness established here, very large TSP instances have already been solved to optimality [3]. There remains substantial room for further algorithmic advances through more effective heuristics, efficient exact methods for restricted classes, and their combined use within branch-and-cut solvers. We hope that our NP-completeness proof and the gadgets developed for the reduction will inform the design of new heuristics for comb separation.
