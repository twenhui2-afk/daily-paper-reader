# On Bonart’s interpretation of the Square-Root Impact Law 

J.-P. Bouchaud, I. Mastromatteo, B. T´ oth Capital Fund Management ∗

October 8, 2026 

Abstract 

The square-root impact law (SRIL), I ≃ Y σ pQ/V , bundles two facts that a single mech-anism must explain at once: a shape (impact proportional to square-root of traded volume Q)and an amplitude (Y = O(1), independent of the participation rate φ). Fixed-kernel models reproduce the shape but carry a “ √φ wart,” Y ∝ √φ. Bonart has recently proposed an elegant escape [1]: if realized and counterfactual prices are both diffusive, information-neutral impact must have white increments and the SRIL follows without the wart. We reformulate and simplify his argument, and foreground the ingredient he himself regards as essential — that the market whitens the stationary stream of each participant’s metaorders, not any isolated one. A solv-able linear model (Lillo–Mike–Farmer) satisfies his premises, yet gives an isolated metaorder the super-square-root impact φ(1 −γ)/2Q(1+ γ)/2, γ ∈ (0 , 1), because single metaorders (i.e. not part of a stream) are statistically invisible and thus priced mechanically. We then construct an ex-plicit multi-agent propagator that realizes Bonart’s whitening, and show it reproduces the clean SRIL precisely when the market can attribute trades to their issuer. But correct re-attribution of anonymous trades is a formidable task, which requires markets to behave as implausibly ef-ficient signal processing machines. The stream hypothesis makes a sharp, falsifiable prediction — isolated metaorders, well separated in time, should not obey the SRIL — that does not seem agree with empirical data known to us. Independently, a volume-conservation argument singles out the square-root law and lays bare the step the diffusivity route must assume: diffusivity fixes the variance of impact — an additive, stationary, age-blind quantity — whereas the SRIL is a law for the mean impact, which marginally decays as 1 /√age and therefore demands an origin of time. Finally, the pure all-pass phase left free by diffusivity pins the magnitude of permanent impact to that of the peak, instead of the observed decay toward zero. Together, these point away from whitening and toward a mechanical resolution: the square root reflects 

volumes that must balance , not correlations that must vanish .

# 1 Introduction 

The square-root impact law (SRIL hereafter) 

I = Y σ 

r QV (1) relates the price impact I of a metaorder of size Q to the daily traded volume V and the daily volatility σ, with a dimensionless prefactor Y = O(1) [2, 3]. Equation (1) is among the most robust empirical regularities in all of market microstructure: it holds across asset classes, venues, and epochs, and it is remarkably insensitive to the details of how the metaorder is sliced and scheduled, and to the type of trading strategy used [4] — see the extended discussion in [2] and refs. therein. This robustness is itself a clue: a law that survives so much heterogeneity is unlikely to depend on the fine microstructural mechanics that differ from venue to venue. It must reflect something deeply structural. However, the theoretical interpretation of the SRIL is still much open to debate. The latent liquidity model (dubbed LLOB) proposed in [5–7] offers a plausible framework that naturally leads to Equation (1), but only in a high-participation trading regime that does not seem to correspond to the empirical regime where the SRIL is observed, typically Q/V ≲ 0.1. This discrepancy, and possible ways to escape it, were addressed in Refs. [8–10], but nagging doubts on the validity of their escape were expressed in [11], based on the analysis of all transactions on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. We shall return to the latent-book picture, and to whether it can be reconciled with the arguments of the present note, in a companion paper; here we set it aside. More recently, Bonart [1] proposed another, trivial-looking origin for the SRIL, rooted in the diffusive nature of prices. His argument, which we review and discuss at length below, is based on the idea that the impact of an individual participant’s flow is “whitened” by the market, so that prices remain diffusive with and without that participant’s metaorders. With one extra assumption, Bonart derives Eq. (1) exactly, and correspondingly calls it the “inconspicuous law of market impact.” The purpose of this note is to examine this proposal on its own terms: to isolate what it genuinely secures, and what it assumes, either explicitly or tacitly. We shall argue that the missing ingredient is detection and, more pointedly, that the information processing an anonymous market 2would need from the order flow it sees is so formidable that it seems quite implausible, at least to our eyes. It is important to note that (1) bundles together two structural facts that must be explained by the same mechanism. This imposes strong constraints on eligible theories, disqualifying those that account for only one of them. 

Feature (i): the shape. Impact grows as √Q. This is the concave, “diminishing marginal impact” law: the tenth million shares move the price √10 ≈ 3.2 less than the first million. It suggests that liquidity is not a fixed depth to be consumed once, but a renewable resource that the very act of trading calls forth (as assumed in the LLOB picture [2]). 

Feature (ii): the amplitude. The prefactor Y is O(1) and, most tellingly, does not scale with the participation rate 

φ ≡ Qf T , (2) i.e. the rate at which the metaorder consumes volume (where T is the time needed for fully executing the metaorder, and f the volume traded by the market per unit time). Concretely: take two metaorders of the same total size Q, one executed patiently at φ = 0 .1% of market volume, the other more aggressively at φ = 1%. Eq. (1) says that they incur essentially the same impact. Said differently, the SRIL says that impact is only dependent on the traded volume V , not on the time T it took for being executed. It is this participation-independence — on top of the square root per se — that is the deep and counter-intuitive fact. Now, in the regime of small participation φ the LLOB model boils down to a simple linear propagator model [6], in which feature (i) is reproduced but feature (ii) comes out wrong: 

I ∼ pφ Q , (3) i.e. Y ∝ √φ. We call (3) the √φ wart . Another way to write Eq. (3) is in terms of T . Noting that 

Q ∝ φT , we obtain I ∼ Q/ √T . For the two metaorders above, for which T varies by a factor 10 at fixed size Q, this predicts a factor √10 ≈ 3.2 difference in impact — a gross effect that would be impossible to miss, and that is simply not there [11, 12]. In fact the wart is not a defect of one particular model but a near-theorem: for any linear , self-similar kernel G(τ ) ∼ τ −β the exponents of φ and Q are locked by dimensional homogeneity to sum to unity, I ∼ φβ Q1−β , so demanding the observed √Q (β = 12 ) forces the √φ. Escaping the wart therefore requires leaving the linear, self-similar family altogether. Bonart’s proposal elegantly avoids the wart and correctly predicts a φ-independent prefactor, by replacing the fixed mechanical kernel with the requirement that impact accumulate diffusively .In this note we first restate Bonart’s additive variance law without flourishes, and the mean-equals-standard-deviation step through which the square root emerges free of the wart (Sec. 2); we also flag there the detectability problem this step raises. We then confront the argument with a solvable linear model (Sec. 3), which satisfies most premises of Bonart’s neutral-regime argument, yet fails to reproduce the SRIL for individual 

metaorders. This result highlights what Bonart emphasizes as a crucial ingredient in his con-struction: that the market is supposed to detect the statistical signature of a stationary stream of metaorders, not isolated ones. It is only this stream that the market is able to whiten, not any particular chunk. Section 4 then asks whether a phenomenological “perceived-persistence” propagator could sup-ply that detection. We construct an explicit, multi-agent propagator model that generates exactly 3Bonart’s whitening device, but by the same token illustrates how formidable this assumption is. The detection hypothesis is also at the heart of three proposals made in the literature to explain the SRIL of isolated metaorders (Farmer–Gerig et al. [13], Saddier–Marsili [14], Maitrier–Bouchaud [15]). The assumption that a somewhat regular stream of metaorders can be detected and at-tributed to a single market participant would then be a way to justify that single metaorders can be statistically detected on the fly. Section 5 then shows that the decay of impact tells the same story from the phase side: diffusivity fixes only the spectral magnitude of the response, but its pure all-pass phase is empirically wrong, and the same perceived correlator would cure it — at the same cost in detection. Finally, Section 6 reframes the diffusivity argument in terms of a purely macroscopic volume-conservation constraint that singles out, but does not pin down, the SRIL. In particular, it makes a clear distinction between the average impact and its standard deviation, which are conflated in Bonart’s argument. The result is suggestive: impact appears to be set by the volumes a metaorder must transact, not by the correlations it induces. (As noted above, the SRIL only depends on V

and not on T .) In Sec. 7, two short remarks show that mere diffusivity selects no particular impact law, even when detection is granted. 

Notation. We reserve φ for the participation rate. Bonart writes μ for the same quantity, and the literature sometimes uses ν for a cancellation rate; the LLOB reaction–diffusion picture also uses 

ν for cancellation. To avoid future collisions we use φ throughout for participation, as in Ref. [15]. Throughout the main development we take unit-volume child orders, i.e. q = 1, so that “number of trades” n and “executed volume” are often interchangeable. Most of the time, we also interchange real time T and trade time N . We use M to denote the total number of metaorders. 

# 2 The diffusivity argument 

2.1 The counterfactual decomposition and structural diffusivity 

Bonart’s starting point is a counterfactual decomposition of returns . Let xt be the return over a bin of size ∆ t, and let yt be the counterfactual return that would have prevailed had the investor not traded. The investor’s market impact is the difference, 

xt = yt + jt, jt ≡ xt − yt, (4) so that yt is the “market-without-me” and jt is the investor’s own causal footprint. This is a decomposition into background flow and own impact : yt is not an “efficient” or “fundamental” price, it is the entire rest of the market in the investor’s absence. Bonart’s assumption is that both the realized and the counterfactual returns are diffusive. This is not imposed by fiat but adaptively : in the absence of persistent arbitrage, returns must be serially uncorrelated, and arbitrageurs continuously recalibrate to the statistical composition of order flow, restoring diffusivity as participants enter or exit. The counterfactual price — the market without the investor — is therefore also diffusive. 2.3 Variance is stationary, but mean needs a start time 

One should now make an important remark, that has to do with stationarity and when it breaks. Clearly, the variance law is stationary, in the sense that the extra contribution to volatility of each trade of any investor does not depend on the time since the metaorder started. This is a direct 5consequence of the linearity of Eq. (6). But as soon as one takes the square-root of the variance to estimate the mean impact of each metaorder, one encounters a thorny problem that is intrinsic to the SRIL: the marginal impact of an additional trade must depend on the number of trades already executed since the start of the metaorder , as [ n(t)

Hence, in order to justify Bonart’s identification of the mean impact and its standard deviation, one has to come up with a mechanism that allows the market to identify the start of a metaorder, at least to some level of precision. As we will discuss below, this is a problem shared with many of the previously proposed theories of the SRIL (except the LLOB model). 

2.4 A note on interpretation 

Continuing on this detectability issue, Bonart forcefully insists on the fact his argument does not concern the impact of a single, isolated metaorder. Rather, each metaorder is viewed as a “typical signed excursion of stationary activity,” and √Q is the standard deviation of an accumulation of white increments, the mean and the standard deviation being explicitly conflated. If participants continually make round turns in their inventory — an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck-like churn of positions opened and closed — then a buy program is one excursion of an ever-present, sign-balanced-on-average flow. Bonart’s occasional language of a “Brownian excursion” is then meaningful: the stream is genuinely stationary, a metaorder is not a perturbation of it but a typical fluctuation within it, and the excess correlation is a property of an ensemble the market sees every day and has purportedly already estimated. In this limit the start time of a metaorder can be predicted, and the passage from variance Q to mean ε√Q is perhaps not too problematic. Indeed, the average height of a Brownian excursion of length T is √T (this is actually the core of the argument of Capponi & Cont [18], see [12] and footnote 3). However, a metaorder that buys for a few days, does nothing for several weeks, and then sells for a few days — the CFM-style execution, common to many investment strategies, for which metaorders are separated by times much longer than their execution time — is not a stationary stream and cannot be read as an excursion of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck-like process: on the scale of its execution it is a rare, isolated coherent event against an otherwise balanced (but long range correlated) background, and its onset is an actual event to be located, not a recurring phase. For such a metaorder the start time cannot be pre-estimated as an ensemble property but something that would have to be inferred on the fly from a single realisation. It is worth separating two readings of this “stream” hypothesis, because they make different predictions for an isolated metaorder. On the excursion reading — continual round-turn (Ornstein– Uhlenbeck) churn, within which a metaorder is a typical fluctuation — an order that is well sepa-rated in time from its predecessors is not an excursion of anything stationary, and should not obey the SRIL, as explicitly shown in the next section 3. On the attribution reading — the market has learned to assign trades to their issuer — a recognizable repeat participant is whitened whenever identified, regardless of temporal separation, and Bonart’s SRIL may survive. This reading thus yields a clear, testable prediction: A single metaorder starting long after the previous one ended should not obey the SRIL under the excursion reading — a smoking-gun prediction that does not seem to agree with CFM’s empirical data, but we need to revisit this matter in the future precisely because of its sharpness. 63 Isolated metaorders: a linear counterexample 

Bonart’s result may read as though the SRIL followed from diffusivity alone. This section exhibits a fully solvable linear model in which the aggregate price is diffusive, the amplitude law holds, and trades are information neutral — yet the impact of a single metaorder is not the square root. Such a framework makes apparent that an extra ingredient is needed for Bonart’s argument to make sense, which he argues is the statistical detectability of a stream of metaorders from the same issuer. We will come back to this question in the next section 4.

3.2 The undetected metaorder incurs under-concave impact 

Now inject one additional metaorder, drawn from the same run-length distribution, and recompute the price with the very same propagator. Because the metaorders are the very constituents of the flow, adding one more is simply drawing one more sample from the generating distribution: it changes no statistics, alters γ not at all, breaks no diffusivity, and creates no return predictability.

3.3 The missing ingredient is detection 

The gap between Eq. (10) and the SRIL is precisely what detection would have to close. Bonart’s √Q is the impact the run would incur if the market detected it and reshaped its response — steepening the mean-response kernel from the diffusive β = (1 − γ)/2 to β = 12 , and supplying the amplitude that removes the residual √φ (the mechanism we examine in Sec. 4). This is an adaptive response, and it presupposes the metaorder has been somehow noticed — in Bonart’s picture because it belongs to a statistically identifiable stream of similar metaorders. In the LMF market, this means that some additional correlation between the sign of metaorders of a given length would need to be added. A generalisation of the LMF model that would take such correlations into account would be valuable to clarify exactly how Bonart’s scenario would apply in that case. We take a short cut in the next Sect. 4 and posit that the market is indeed able to assign all metaorders in the order flow to individual market participants. We note in passing that the under-concave, participation-dependent regime of the LMF model is not an isolated pathology: it reappears in Sec. 6 as a degenerate corner ( b → 1) of a macroscopic variance budget, the limit in which coherent metaorder impact carries a vanishing ( O(M −1)) share of the daily variance. The SRIL occupies the complementary corner of that same budget, in which the share is O(1) — and, as we shall see there, imposing it derives the very mean-equals-standard-deviation step that the present section shows diffusivity alone cannot supply. 

# 4 An effective multi-agent propagator model

In particular, the way the market is able to find out who does what (even statistically) in an anonymous market is, at this stage, beyond us. A caveat: smart detection. Eq. (18) sets the threshold for a correlation-based estimator working on signed flow alone. A real market is not so impoverished: participation, volume and timestamp regularities, order-size clustering, venue and broker footprints, and a host of other HFT signals might carry information about a metaorder that the sign autocorrelation discards. Any such channel improves detectability — possibly substantially, and this is presumably how execution-detection algorithms operate in practice. But the reduction is bounded by whatever coherence the metaorder actually leaves on the tape, and a well-executed order is engineered precisely to leave as little as possible — whereas the SRIL appears universal and largely independent of the style of execution [4]. 

4.3 Three realizations where detection re-enters 

Three alternative completions of the diffusive picture in the literature also reach the SRIL success-fully, and it is worth seeing precisely how. In all three, detection is not derived — it is built into the setup, through the same assumption we have found to be crucial and difficult to justify: that the metaorder’s onset (and hence its age) is known. 

Farmer, Gerig, Lillo and Waelbroeck: efficiency plus fair pricing. The earliest of these completions [13] is also the closest in spirit to our counterexample of Sec. 3, and pinpointing the difference is instructive. Farmer et al. take the same long-memory order flow and impose two conditions: market efficiency (returns are martingale) and a fair-pricing condition — that the average price paid over the course of a metaorder equals the permanent price it leaves behind, so that liquidity providers break even against it. The efficiency condition alone is precisely what our LMF market embodies: it fixes the participant-blind response that whitens the flow, and on its own it delivers the over-convex mechanical impact of Sec. 3. The fair-pricing condition is the extra ingredient, and it is what bends the impact toward the observed concave law. But fair pricing is imposed order by order: it equates a metaorder’s average execution price with its own terminal impact, which presupposes that the pricing “knows” a metaorder is running, and over what horizon and size — exactly the conditioning on a coherent order that an anonymous tape does not supply. In our language, Farmer et al. obtain the concave law by promoting detection to a no-arbitrage 

axiom (fair pricing) rather than deriving it: the metaorder’s presence and extent are built into the break-even condition from the outset. 

Saddier and Marsili: an explicit Bayesian detector. Saddier and Marsili [14] make the detection explicit. Their market maker does not price through a mechanical kernel at all; it holds a Bayesian posterior over the presence and rate φ of an informed trader, and the price is the posterior mean of fundamental value given the observed flow. Impact is then the revision of that 11 posterior as the order runs. Two consequences matter directly for our argument. First, because a Bayesian estimate of a persistent drift sharpens as ∝ 1/√t,4 the posterior value-revision inherits the square root: the concavity is the shrinking of a belief, not the decay of a kernel, and the residual participation dependence is absorbed into the volatility normalisation σ ∝ √φ (their Eq. 5) — Bonart’s amplitude-law, inherited here from the assumption that informed traders adapt their participation rate to their signal. Crucially, their √Q is a posterior mean , not a root-mean-squared, so their construction is free of the mean-versus-standard-deviation identification we flagged in Sec. 2.4 as Bonart’s weak point — a genuine advantage. The price of it is that detection is assumed : their trader is informed and sets 

φ accordingly, and the onset of the metaorder ( t = 0) is taken to be identifiable. The mechanism does not manufacture detection from an anonymous tape; it posits an informed-trading equilibrium in which detection is automatic — exactly the ingredient we have argued is missing. And, tellingly, the construction inherits the same fragility: fed long-range order flow, it too leaves the clean regime and returns an under-concave impact (their Appendix D.3). The exponent is different from ours but the qualitative lesson is identical: a detection-based square root is fragile precisely when the background is realistically persistent. 

Maitrier and Bouchaud: an age-clock propagator. The two-time propagator of [15] is the phenomenological realization of exactly the object Sec. 4.1 posited. Its kernel depends not only on the lag but on the age of the metaorder — the time t′ elapsed since its first child — decaying more slowly at short age and reverting to the ambient exponent at long age, and it robustly reproduces the √Q peak with a φ-independent prefactor. The age variable t′ is precisely an encoding of detection: to apply an age-dependent kernel the market must somehow know that a metaorder started, and when. An anonymous market reading the tape does not possess t′ — it would have to infer the onset, which is the very detection Sec. 4.2 showed to be unavailable below Q ∼ φ−3

(for γ = 12 ). The two-time propagator is therefore an effective description of a market that has 

somehow detected the order and is tracking its age. Read the other way, its empirical success might be indirect evidence that something in real markets does supply the onset — that detection, by whatever route, has effectively occurred — which is precisely what Bonart’s stream detectability aims at producing.

The very construction that would steepen the build-up to √Q thus also shapes the decay correctly, from a single object. But it inherits the very same liability: the plateau-and-tail must be keyed to the metaorder’s onset and extent, which — as Sec. 4.2 showed — an anonymous market cannot resolve in the low-participation regime where the SRIL holds. The decay problem and the detection problem are, once again, one problem: diffusivity fixes the magnitude of the response and leaves its phase free, and pinning that phase to the empirically correct shape requires exactly the conditioning on a coherent order that we have argued the market cannot supply.

What remains open. The above reasoning seems to us the correct way to frame Bonart’s argument: if the impact of metaorders is assumed to carry an O(1) share of the daily variance, and if one imposes that this share (and hence Y ) does not scale with the number of contemporaneously executed metaorders, then the SRIL is the only possibility compatible with volume conservation. What we still lack is a microscopic reason for the premise itself — for why coherent metaorder impact should retain an O(1), M -independent share of daily variance rather than diluting to the variance-subdominant b → 1 world. But conversely, the fact that the SRIL indeed holds empirically shows that metaorders contribute to O(1) of the volatility, as argued from a different angle in [15]. 

# 7 Two further remarks 

We close with two brief remarks placing the above within the wider scope of the diffusivity con-straint. They confirm that diffusivity alone selects no particular impact law. 

A permanent-impact toy-model. Consider a stationary flow of metaorders starting at un-correlated times with uncorrelated signs, each impacting the price once , permanently, at its first child, with no further impact. The impact-increment series is a train of uncorrelated impulses, cumulative impact is a random walk; returns are diffusive and unpredictable. Yet the metaorder impact is permanent, front-loaded, and independent of Q: no concave build-up, no √Q.A diffusive, information-neutral model can therefore lie arbitrarily far from the SRIL; whether impact scales with Q at all is set by the amplitude law, not by diffusivity. 

The Kyle model. At the opposite pole, an informed trader’s flow is correlated with fundamental value, the market maker prices competitively, and the outcome is a diffusive price with impact that is linear and permanent [28].

# 8 Conclusion 

Bonart’s proposal turns the oldest fact about prices — their diffusivity — into a constraint on market impact, and shows, elegantly, that white impact increments accumulate to a φ-independent square root, free of the √φ wart that afflicts every fixed-kernel model. What it secures is the additive variance law, under the information-neutrality of order flow. What it assumes is the step from that law to the square root of a metaorder — and that step hides an asymmetry worth stating plainly, for it is the root of everything else. Diffusivity is a statement about the variance of returns: an additive, stationary quantity, blind to when any particular metaorder began. The SRIL is a statement about the mean impact, which grows as the square root of the volume executed since the start of the order — an age-dependent object. One cannot pass from a stationary, clockless variance to an age-dependent mean without an origin of time; and that origin, the metaorder’s start, is exactly what an anonymous, aggregate tape does not contain. The Lillo–Mike–Farmer model makes the gap concrete: it satisfies every premise yet, lacking any such clock, prices an isolated metaorder as the under-concave φ(1 −γ)/2Q(1+ γ)/2 rather than √Q.We take Bonart at his word that his object is not the isolated order but the stationary stream 

of a participant’s metaorders, which the market has learned to whiten — but that is indirectly a way of supplying the missing clock. Our multi-agent propagator makes the required ingredient precise, and shows how strong it is. Whitening each participant’s own flow yields the clean law if and only if its perceived persistence is linear , C∗ ∝ φ — and that linearity is exactly what stream attribution buys: the one missing power of φ, relative to the anonymously estimable φ2, is the price of assigning each trade to a coherent, persistent stream and so furnishing it with a start. The clean SRIL thus requires the market to unmix the aggregate flow into per-trader streams and whiten each on its own — a statistical source-separation problem, not a recovery of identities — a formidable task that we doubt markets are able to achieve, in view of the so many other persistent anomalies that they are not able to get rid of (see e.g. the recent discussion in [29]). The stream hypothesis also makes a sharp, falsifiable prediction — an isolated metaorder, well separated in time from its predecessors, should not obey the SRIL — a claim that is at odds with our available CFM data, which we are revisiting because of the importance of that claim. To summarize our message: what is in doubt is that whitening produces the square-root law. The volume-conservation argument of Sec. 6 points the same way: among all exponents, δ = 12

is the unique one for which the daily variance budget closes independently of the metaorder-size distribution and of the number of simultaneous metaorders — pinned by a conservation law, with no reference to whitening or detection. The robustness of the square root, we suspect, is a statement about volumes that must balance , not correlations that must vanish . Its natural home is the latent-order-book picture, in which impact is the displacement required to walk a metaorder’s volume through a book of resting liquidity [2, 6, 7, 30]. The LLOB produces the clean square root natively only at high participation, whereas the SRIL is measured at low; reconciling the two regimes is unfinished business, which we take up in a companion paper.
