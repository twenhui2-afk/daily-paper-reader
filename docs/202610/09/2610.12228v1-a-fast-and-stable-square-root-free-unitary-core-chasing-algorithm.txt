Abstract. Symmetric and unitary matrices are among the most important classes of structured matrices admitting efficient and stable eigenvalue algorithms. Notable examples include structure-preserving variants of the QR algorithm and their square-root-free counterparts. For unitary matri-ces, these algorithms were historically derived from recurrence relations for orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. A matrix-based derivation using core chasing was later given in [3]; this family of algorithms is backward stable, but the underlying symmetries needed to cast out the square roots were not identified, and the question of structured backward stability was left open. In this paper we present a matrix-based derivation of the symmetric unitary core-chasing algorithm and use it to obtain a square-root-free variant. We give a matrix-based proof of backward stability and show that strict structured backward stability is impossible in general. Open-source Fortran code and numerical experiments demonstrate that the combination of exploiting the symmetry and removing the square roots reduces computation time without degrading accuracy. 

Key words. unitary matrix, eigenvalues, square-root-free, Pal-Walker-Kahan, core transforma-tions

1. Introduction. This project began with an observation. Among all the differ-ent types of structured matrices that admit fast algorithms for computing eigenvalues, symmetric tridiagonal matrices and unitary upper Hessenberg matrices possess a num-ber of very similar algorithms. For both classes there exist fast versions of Francis’ implicitly-shifted QR algorithm [7, 12] and fast divide-and-conquer algorithms [6, 15]. There are also Krylov methods based on short recurrences [4, 18], bisection meth-ods [5, 20], and Jacobi methods [10, 17], though, in general, these apply to full matri-ces. In the case of the QR algorithm, the similarities between these two classes of matrices run even deeper. For both classes, the recursions used in each QR iteration are not unique, leading to multiple versions of the “same” algorithm. In the case of unitary matrices, many variants have been proposed, though not all of them are stable [1,3,12–14,21–24]. Additionally, for both classes of matrices there exist square-root-free versions, which avoid computing the square roots needed to normalize the similarity transformations. When done correctly, removing the costly square roots gives an even more efficient algorithm for computing eigenvalues, without sacrificing backward stability. For symmetric tridiagonal matrices, the first square-root-free algorithm was proposed by Ortega and Kaiser in 1963 [19], and later improved by Pal, Walker, and Kahan in 1968 [20, Section 8.15]. Further versions were introduced and their stability analyzed in [8]. Developing square-root-free algorithms for unitary upper Hessenberg matrices is more complicated than for symmetric tridiagonal matrices, since the transforming matrices are necessarily complex. In the original unitary QR algorithm by Gragg [12], cancellation errors due to the necessary complex arithmetic made the algorithm unstable. In [13, 14], Gragg introduced an alternative formulation of his original

In [21–23], Stewart reviewed several variants of the QR algorithm for unitary upper Hessenberg matrices and gave a detailed stability analysis of a stabilized recurrence-based formulation, including square-root-free variants. In [3], the authors unified many of the existing unitary QR algorithms by in-terpreting them as unitary core-chasing algorithms [2]. This point of view allows the same algorithms to be derived from a strictly matrix-based analysis, as opposed to one based on recurrence relations for orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. It also allows one to show that, in exact arithmetic, many of the seemingly different variants of the QR algorithm for unitary upper Hessenberg-like matrices are identical. The matrix-based analysis further aids in simplifying the proof of backward stability. However, the additional symmetries needed to derive a square-root-free unitary core-chasing algorithm were not identified in [3], and the question of structured backward stability was not addressed. This paper addresses these details, starting in Section 2, where we show that the core-chasing formulation of the unitary QR algorithm contains a previously unnoticed symmetry. This symmetry is generated by the first fusion in a single-shift core-chasing step and is preserved, in exact arithmetic, by a symmetric turnover. We also discuss its relation with the QR algorithm for symmetric tridiagonal matrices. In Section 3 we study what happens when the core transformations are only nearly-unitary, as occurs when using floating-point arithmetic. In this setting, the na¨ ıve symmetric turnover formulas can be unstable, and one must use a modified formula analogous to Gragg’s stabilized recurrence. We prove that the resulting sym-metric turnover is nearly exact for nearly-unitary data. In Section 4 we show how the symmetry in the turnover can be used to derive a square-root-free unitary core-chasing algorithm and how to preserve the nearly-unitariness of the core transformations without computing additional square roots. In Section 5 we analyze the floating-point implementation. We prove that both the symmetric and square-root-free turnovers are stable in a forward sense for nearly-unitary floating-point input, and then show how this forward error can be converted into a backward error result for the turnover. We also show that a strict structured backward error result is impossible in general. Finally, in Section 6 we present numerical experiments that demonstrate the prac-tical effects of removing square roots, and compare the symmetric and square-root-free algorithms with LAPACK and the unitary core-chasing algorithm in [3].

3. Nearly-Unitary Matrices. Although the results of Theorem 2.1 allow us to compute the turnover efficiently in exact arithmetic, the current formulas are not stable and do not permit the development of a square-root-free symmetric turnover. The instabilities come from the fact that the floating-point representations of the parameters u and v can no longer be guaranteed to satisfy |u|2 + v2 = 1 exactly, and from the possibility of cancellation when computing the turnover in floating-point arithmetic. Similar numerical instabilities were present in Gragg’s first unitary QR algorithm [12] based on recurrence relations for Szeg˝ o polynomials. In [14], Gragg introduced a new change of variables for these relations that appeared to remove these instabilities and also allowed for a square-root-free implementation, though no proof was given. A proof of stability of these recurrence-based algorithms was given by Stewart in [23]. In this and the following sections, we will show that a similar change of variables to that found in [14] can be used to stabilize the symmetric turnover and to develop a square-root-free implementation. We will also show that these versions of the sym-metric turnover are stable when applied to matrices that are only nearly-unitary in floating-point arithmetic.

This means that the symmetric turnover and the entire core-chasing step are backward stable [2]. While U is a core transformation, 

U ′ is almost surely not, which means the errors in U − U ′ are not confined to the nontrivial block [ U ]. Example 5.4 shows that we cannot expect structured errors for nearly-unitary matrices, so this result is not surprising.

7. Conclusions. In this article we presented a matrix-based derivation of a square-root-free unitary core-chasing algorithm for computing eigenvalues of unitary upper Hessenberg matrices. The derivation is based on a symmetry in the turnover operation used in the unitary core-chasing algorithm from [3], which reduces the arithmetic needed to perform the core-chasing step and exposes the connection with square-root-free QR algorithms for symmetric tridiagonal matrices. We showed that the na¨ ıve symmetric turnover formulas can be unstable when the core transforma-tions are only nearly-unitary, as occurs in floating-point arithmetic, and introduced a change of variables, analogous to Gragg’s stabilized recurrence, that avoids this cancellation and leads to a stable symmetric turnover for nearly-unitary data. The A FAST AND STABLE SQUARE-ROOT-FREE UNITARY CORE-CHASING ALGORITHM 25 same change of variables also makes it possible to derive a square-root-free version of the algorithm. The floating-point analysis shows that both the symmetric and square-root-free turnovers are stable for nearly-unitary input, and that the resulting forward error can be converted into a local backward error result for the turnover, al-though a strict structured backward error result is impossible in general. Finally, the numerical experiments demonstrate that the combination of exploiting the symmetry in the turnover and casting out square roots gives accuracy comparable to LAPACK and to the unitary core-chasing algorithm in [3], while reducing the overall runtime.
