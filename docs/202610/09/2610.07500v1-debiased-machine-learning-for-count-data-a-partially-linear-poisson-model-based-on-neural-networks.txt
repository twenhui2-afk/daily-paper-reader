Abstract 

We develop a novel debiased machine learning (DML) estimator to analyze count data, which are common in clinical or biomedical studies. Specifically, we estimate the effect of a binary treatment on count outcomes using a partially linear Poisson model. A key advantage of this model is that it represents the complex covariate structure through a nuisance function, which is estimated flexibly using neural networks. We use a Neyman-orthogonal score function to construct the estimator, reducing its sensitivity to errors in estimating the nuisance function. Cross-fitting is used to mitigate overfitting bias in neural networks. Under mild regularity conditions, this DML estimator is asymptotically normal with root-n convergence. We derive a closed-form variance estimator and construct a Wald confidence interval for the treatment effect. Extensive simulations demonstrate that the proposed estimator reduces bias and root mean square error compared with the generalized linear model and the augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) estimator in most settings, while achieving a coverage probability near the nominal level. The proposed procedure is implemented in the R package PoissonDML .We apply our approach to a synthetic HIV cohort data to investigate the effect of treatment on AIDS-defining event counts of 7,141 analyzed patients. Our approach provides practical and valid estimation and inference for treatment effects under the partially linear Poisson model while combining modern machine learning methods to estimate complex covariate structures. 

## 1 Introduction 

1.1 Background and motivation 

Count outcomes commonly appear in epidemiological or clinical studies because patients may experience recurrent events, such as infections, hospitalizations, or adverse events. When the outcomes are counts, researchers are often interested in comparing the expected event counts between treatments, which can inform assessments of disease progression and treatment decisions. For example, in a large clinical HIV cohort (McGowan et al., 2007), it is of interest to estimate the effect of dolutegravir (DTG)-based versus non-DTG antiretroviral therapy (ART) on the subsequent number of AIDS-defining events (ADEs), while controlling for demographic and clinical baseline variables. As the presence of multiple confounders may introduce bias into effect estimates, it is essential to develop proper methods to analyze such data. A standard approach to analyzing count outcomes is Poisson regression within the generalized linear model (GLM) framework. However, Poisson regression relies on correct specification of the parametric model of covariates, which can be challenging in observational studies with complex covariate structures. This challenge motivates semiparametric methods that aim to flexibly estimate the relationship between the covariates and the outcome. For example, the partially linear regression 1arXiv:2610.07500v1 [stat.ME] 5 Oct 2026 model proposed by Robinson (1988) allows the covariate effects to be estimated flexibly while preserving an interpretable treatment effect parameter. 

1.2 Literature review 

Modern machine learning methods have increasingly been adopted to model data with high-dimensional and complex covariate structures. Among those approaches, neural networks have received particular attention because of their ability to approximate complex nonlinear functions and complex interactions without requiring explicit model specification (Hornik et al., 1989; Hornik, 1991). For example, several recent studies have adapted neural network architectures for count data by integrating Poisson or related likelihood functions (Montesinos-L´ opez et al., 2020; Montesinos-Lopez et al., 2021; Jose et al., 2024). However, these methods are primarily designed for prediction, not estimation or inference on treatment effect parameters. It is challenging to directly incorporate machine-learned predictions into estimating equations, as these predictions are subject to bias. To address this, semiparametric frameworks have been developed to incorporate flexible machine-learned predictions in nuisance models for the augmented inverse probability weighting estimator (AIPW) (Robins et al., 1994) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) (van der Laan and Rubin, 2006). More recently, Chernozhukov et al. (2018) developed the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework, which combines machine-learned nuisance functions with Neyman-orthogonal scores and cross-fitting. These components reduce the sensitivity of estimating equations to errors in nuisance estimation and overfitting, enabling root-n consistency and asymptotic normality under suitable conditions. Subsequent developments address multiway-clustered data (Chiang et al., 2022), heterogeneous effects and other causal functions (Semenova and Chernozhukov, 2021), mediation and dynamic treatments (Farbmacher et al., 2022; Bodory et al., 2022), multiple and continuous treatments (Xiang et al., 2025; Colangelo and Lee, 2026), and nonlinear semiparametric models (Liu et al., 2021; Nekipelov et al., 2022). Several studies have developed semiparametric estimating equations for Poisson models. Robins and Rotnitzky (2001) described a general class of estimating functions for regression models with a log link. The Stata package xpopoisson implements Poisson regression using lasso-based nuisance estimation (StataCorp, 2025). Covert and Sweeney (2023) derived a cross-fitted pseudo-Poisson estimator for binary treatments using the conditional outcome mean and the propensity score. Kumar et al. (2022) developed an orthogonal procedure for continuous price-sensitivity estimation in the presence of treatment heterogeneity. However, the existing literature has not provided a complete DML framework for this setting, including a practical algorithm for estimation, asymptotic theory for the DML estimator, and a variance estimator for constructing valid Wald confidence intervals. 

1.3 Our contribution 

To our knowledge, this work is the first to develop a practical estimation and inference procedure for the DML estimator under the partially linear Poisson model and to provide a comprehensive theoretical justification for its asymptotic properties. Specifically, our main contributions are that we (1) develop a cross-fitted DML estimator that directly estimates g(X) using a neural network; (2) establish Neyman orthogonality, double robustness, and root-n asymptotic normality of the proposed estimator; (3) develop the R package PoissonDML to implement the proposed estimation and inference procedure, and (4) apply the proposed DML method to a synthetic HIV cohort to evaluate treatment effects on AIDS-defining event counts. The R package PoissonDML can be 2accessed through GitHub ( https://github.com/nkzhou/PoissonDML-R ).

## 4 Theory 

In this section, we establish that the proposed estimating equation is Neyman orthogonal and derive the asymptotic properties of the resulting DML estimator needed for valid statistical inference. First, we introduce some notation. Let o(an), O(an), and Ω( an) represent sequences growing at a smaller, smaller/equal, and greater/equal rate of an, respectively. Also, let op, Op, and Ω p be the corresponding rates with probability approaching 1 as n → ∞ . In addition, let X ⊆ Rp denote the domain of Xi.We state the following regularity conditions for θ and the score function used for the DML estimator, adapted from Assumption REG in Liu et al. (2021). 

Assumption 1 (Regularity conditions) . The following regularity conditions hold: (a) The parameter θ belongs to a compact set Θ ⊆ R and there exists a sequence of positive constants δn = Ω( n−1/2 log n) such that (θ0 − δn, θ 0 + δn) ⊆ Θ.(b) The outcome Yi satisfies finite moment conditions: E[|Yi|q] < ∞ for some q > 2.(c) The nuisance functions g0 and π0 are uniformly bounded. Specifically, there exists a constant 

C such that ∥g0∥∞ = sup X∈X |g0(X)| ≤ C and the propensity score π0(X) is bounded away from 0 and 1. (d) There exist constants 0 < c 0 ≤ c1 < ∞ such that c0 ≤ | J0| ≤ c1 and c0 ≤ E[ψ2(Wi; θ0, η 0)] ≤ c1,where J0 is defined in equation (5).

5.3 Results 

Figure 2 summarizes the empirical bias, RMSE, and CP of the 95% confidence intervals under the two covariate structures for the DML estimators using five-fold cross-fitting and other comparative methods. Complete numerical results, including the DML estimators using two-fold cross-fitting and the true outcome nuisance, are reported in Tables 1 and 2. Under the simple covariate structure g1, the linearly adjusted Poisson GLM generally showed the largest absolute bias, and its bias increased as the treatment effect increased. The naive NN estimator reduced the bias relative to the GLM but generally remained more biased than the DML estimators. This suggests that orthogonalization and cross-fitting in DML helped reduce the remaining bias associated with machine-learning nuisance estimation. Compared with the AIPW estimators, the DML estimators using five-fold cross-fitting had lower RMSE in every setting. This pattern held for both logistic regression and random forest propensity score models. In general, the 11 DML estimators had small bias, RMSE that decreased as the sample size increased, and coverage close to the nominal level. Under the more complex covariate structure g2, the five-fold DML estimators remained nearly unbiased and had lower RMSE than both AIPW estimators in every setting, while maintaining coverage close to the nominal level. Within DML, propensity models using logistic regression and random forest had similar results. Differences between two-fold and five-fold cross-fitting were generally modest, although the latter often produced marginally lower RMSE. Notably, DML using the true outcome nuisance generally achieved the lowest RMSE and small bias (Tables 1 and 2), suggesting the importance of reliable estimation of nuisance functions even when an orthogonal estimating equation is used. 

## 6 Empirical study 

In this section, we apply the proposed DML estimator to the synthetic CCASAnet data and compare the estimated treatment effect with those from the Poisson GLM and AIPW estimators. 

6.1 Data description 

We applied our method to a synthetic dataset from the Caribbean, Central and South America Network for HIV Research (CCASAnet), a collaboration that harmonizes clinical HIV cohort data across sites in Latin America and the Caribbean. The cohort consisted of 49,606 adults living with HIV from six countries as of December 2024 and spanned more than 30 years of follow-up. The synthetic data closely reproduced longitudinal distributions, missingness patterns, time-to-event estimates, and risk-factor relationships in the original CCASAnet cohort without disclosing sensitive patient-level records (Liang et al., 2026). The treatment of interest was dolutegravir-based antiretroviral therapy (ART). Treatment was classified as DTG-based if the initial regimens included DTG and as non-DTG otherwise. The target population comprised ART-naive patients whose first recorded ART had a documented start date on or after January 1, 2013, the year when DTG was approved by the FDA. After excluding patients with incomplete baseline covariates, the analytic sample consisted of 7,141 patients: 2,223 initiated a DTG-based regimen and 4,918 initiated a non-DTG regimen. The primary outcome was the number of ADEs occurring within five years after ART initiation. We retained patients with less than five years of recorded follow-up, with observed follow-up duration as an adjusted covariate in all models to account for the difference in observation time. Finally, our 

target causal estimand is the conditional effect of DTG-based ART versus non-DTG ART on the count of the ADEs within five years. Specifically, it is the ratio of the expected five-year ADE counts under the two treatment regimens, conditional on covariates. The treatment and outcome models used the same set of baseline characteristics and demographic information, including baseline CD4 count, age, school level, sex, country/clinical center, HIV acquisition mode, and time of ART initiation. Descriptive statistics for these baseline covariates are reported in Appendix H, Table 4.

6.3 Results 

Table 3 presents the estimated treatment effects on the log scale and their exponentiated values, interpreted as conditional five-year expected count ratios. The two DML estimates, 1.357 (95% CI: 0.996, 1.848) with the logistic propensity score model and 1.294 (95% CI: 0.955, 1.752) with the RF propensity score model, were similar to each other. These estimates corresponded to 35.7% and 29.4% higher expected five-year ADE counts for DTG-based regimens than for non-DTG regimens. However, both confidence intervals included the null value of 1. Compared with the Poisson GLM estimate of 1.425 (95% CI: 1.038, 1.957), the point estimates of DML methods were closer to the null, while their confidence intervals suggested insufficient evidence that DTG was associated with higher ADE counts than non-DTG regimens, contrary to the Poisson GLM results. The DML estimates had smaller estimated standard errors than the AIPW estimates for both propensity score models. The precision gain was particularly large for the estimates using the logistic propensity model, as the estimated standard error decreased from 0.413 for AIPW to 0.158 for DML. This difference may suggest that the DML estimator is more stable in the presence of extreme propensity scores, because DML does not invert the propensity scores to construct inverse probability weights. The distributions of the raw and truncated inverse probability weights from the logistic regression and the random forest models are presented in Appendix H.4. 13 7 Conclusion 

We presented our DML estimator for the treatment effect coefficient in a partially linear Poisson model. Using simulations, we demonstrated that DML estimators had smaller bias and decreasing RMSE in most settings. More importantly, coverage was near the nominal 95% level, supporting the validity of statistical inference for our estimator. In the synthetic CCASAnet application, DML found insufficient evidence that DTG was associated with higher five-year ADE counts, in contrast to the Poisson GLM. DML also provided more precise estimates across propensity score models and suggested greater robustness to extreme propensity scores than AIPW. Some limitations may affect the interpretation and generalizability of the CCASAnet analysis. First, loss to follow-up may bias the estimated treatment effect if associated with patient charac-teristics or evolving health status. As a result, inverse probability of censoring weighting could be incorporated to address this potential bias. Second, although our model assumes a conditional Pois-son distribution, the unbiasedness and Neyman orthogonality of the proposed score function depend only on the conditional mean, and do not require the mean to equal its variance. In addition, the empirical sandwich variance estimator allows misspecification of the conditional variance while still providing valid variance estimation asymptotically. However, overdispersion may reduce its efficiency relative to estimators that appropriately incorporate the correct conditional variance. Future work could develop an orthogonal estimating equation based on negative binomial regression or other flexible count models. Third, the partially linear model assumes a constant conditional count mean ratio across covariate values. This restriction provides a parsimonious model for treatment effect estimation, but does not allow treatment heterogeneity. Future work could incorporate prespecified treatment-effect modification by allowing a subset of the covariates to interact with the treatment.

## G Simulation study: implementation and hyperparameter selec-tion 

This appendix provides the implementation of nuisance-function estimators, definitions of com-parative methods, and hyperparameter selection procedures used in the simulation study. The data-generating process is specified in Section 5.1, and the numerical results are reported in Tables 1 and 2. The general DML estimation and inference procedure is described in Section 3. 

G.1 Estimator implementation 

We evaluated the proposed DML estimator using either logistic regression or a random forest to estimate the propensity score. For both models, the outcome nuisance function was estimated using the partially linear Poisson neural network architecture introduced in Section 3.1. The covariate branch contained two equal-width hidden layers, with GELU activation and dropout applied after each hidden layer, while treatment entered through a separate linear node. Parameters in the covariate branch and the treatment coefficient were jointly optimized using Adam and the Poisson negative log-likelihood described in Section 3.1. The logistic regression propensity model included the four covariates as linear terms on the logit scale. The random forest propensity model used the same four covariates as predictors. We applied the cross-fitting procedure in Section 3 separately with K = 2 and K = 5. Within each evaluation dataset, the hyperparameters selected from the reserved tuning datasets remained the same across the cross-fitting folds. For numerical stability, the estimated propensity scores were truncated to [10 −6, 1 − 10 −6]. For each propensity score model, we also evaluated a DML estimator in which the known gs(Xi)from the data-generating process replaced the nuisance function estimated by neural networks. The propensity score remained estimated from the observed treatment and covariates. This estimator serves as a benchmark since it isolates the contribution of nuisance estimation error from g0(Xi). The linearly adjusted Poisson GLM included treatment and the four covariates as additive linear predictors. Its fitted treatment coefficient was reported as the estimate of θ0.The naive partially linear Poisson neural network estimator used the same architecture and selected hyperparameters as the DML outcome model but was fitted once to the complete evaluation 31 dataset. The coefficient on its linear treatment node was reported directly as the estimate of θ0,without cross-fitting or the Neyman-orthogonal estimating equation. For AIPW, a full-sample Poisson GLM containing treatment and the four covariates as additive linear predictors was used to estimate the conditional mean count under each treatment level, denoted by ˆ ma(Xi) for a ∈ { 0, 1}. The propensity score was estimated using five-fold cross-fitting with either logistic regression or a random forest. Let ˆ p1(Xi) = ˆ πi and ˆ p0(Xi) = 1 − ˆπi. G.2 Hyperparameter selection 

For each simulation setting, we reserved the first five of the 500 simulated datasets for hyperparameter tuning and excluded these datasets from the Monte Carlo performance summaries. Each candidate configuration was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation within each tuning dataset. The validation losses were averaged across the five folds and five tuning datasets, and the configuration with the lowest average loss was fixed for analyzing the remaining 495 datasets. This cross-validation for hyperparameter selection was separate from the two-fold or five-fold DML cross-fitting subsequently performed within each evaluation dataset. For the neural network, the candidate grid included hidden-layer widths of 8 or 16, dropout probabilities of 0.01 or 0.05, and 500 or 1,000 training epochs. The two hidden layers always had the same width, and the learning rate was fixed at 0.01. Candidates were compared using the held-out Poisson negative log-likelihood for the complete partially linear predictor θA i + g(Xi). The selected configuration was used for the cross-fitted outcome nuisance models in DML and for the corresponding full-sample naive neural network estimator. For the random forest, the candidate grid included 50, 100, or 200 trees; maximum tree depths of 4, 8, or 16; minimum split sizes of 2, 5, or 10; and minimum leaf sizes of 2, 5, or 10. At each split, the number of candidate predictors was determined by the square root of the total number of covariates, and each tree was fitted using a bootstrap sample. The candidates were compared using held-out treatment log loss averaged across the validation folds and the five tuning datasets. The random forest was tuned separately for each simulation setting, and the selected configuration was fixed for analyzing the remaining 495 datasets. 

## H Empirical study: data description, implementation, and diag-nostics 

This appendix provides the baseline descriptive statistics, hyperparameter selection, inference details, and inverse probability weight diagnostics for the CCASAnet analysis. The data were used with provider consent and are available upon request to the authors and CCASAnet, subject to provider approval. 32 H.1 Baseline characteristics 

Table 4 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the 7,141 ART-naive patients in the analytic sample, stratified by the ART regimen that patients started. The CCASAnet cohort underlying the synthetic data includes patients from six countries; however, the synthetic data only consist of five countries. To improve estimation stability, categories with small sample sizes were combined in the models. For country, Argentina and Mexico were combined into a single category. For HIV acquisition mode, the Perinatal, Transfusion nonhemophilia related, and Other categories were combined into a single Other category. 

H.2 Cross-fitting and hyperparameter selection 

We used five outer folds to obtain cross-fitted predictions of the outcome nuisance function g0(X)and the propensity score π0(X). Within each outer training sample, hyperparameters of neural networks and random forests were selected using a single 80%/20% inner training-validation split. For the neural network, the candidate grid included hidden-layer widths of 8, 16, or 32; dropout probabilities of 0.05 or 0.10; learning rates of 10 −3 or 10 −4; 500, 1,000, or 2,000 training epochs; and weight-decay values of 10 −4 or 10 −3. Each candidate was fitted on the inner training sample and evaluated on the inner validation sample using the Poisson negative log-likelihood. After selection, a new neural network was fitted using the complete outer training sample and used to obtain ˆ gi for observations in the held-out outer fold. The same cross-fitted ˆ gi predictions were used for the DML estimators with logistic regression and random forest propensity scores. The logistic regression propensity model required no hyperparameter tuning and was fitted on each complete outer training sample. For the random forest, the candidate grid included 100 or 200 trees; maximum tree depths of 4 or 6; minimum split sizes of 2, 5, or 10; minimum leaf sizes of 2, 5, or 10. At each split, the number of candidate predictors was determined by the square root of the total number of covariates. Each candidate was evaluated using treatment log loss on the inner validation sample. Similar to the outcome model, the selected random forest was refitted on the complete outer training sample and used to predict propensity scores in the held-out outer fold. For numerical stability, the estimated propensity scores used by DML were truncated to [10 −6, 1 − 10 −6]. 

H.3 Inference for comparison methods 

For the AIPW estimators, we used a nonparametric bootstrap for their confidence intervals. The propensity scores for both AIPW estimators were obtained using five-fold cross-fitting. We truncated the propensity scores from the logistic regression to [0 .01 , 0.99] and the RF propensity scores to [10 −6, 1 − 10 −6]. We resampled the analytic data for B = 1 , 000 bootstrap samples and refitted the outcome and propensity score models for each sample. Percentile intervals were constructed for the AIPW estimates. In addition, HC0 robust standard errors were used for the Poisson GLM model.
