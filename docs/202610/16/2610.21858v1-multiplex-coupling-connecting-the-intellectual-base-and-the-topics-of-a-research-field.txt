Abstract 

Two documents can be close because they cite the same works or because they write about the same things, and the two kinds of proximity often disagree. Science map-ping reads them in separate maps or merges them, as hybrid clustering does; either way the disagreement is lost. We introduce multiplex coupling, a framework that couples the same documents by their references and by their terms, keeps the two layers separate and analyses their relation. Similarities are standardised against a null model, and the agreement between the layers is measured on random pairs of documents. Consensus clustering partitions each layer into roots, groups sharing an intellectual base, and themes, groups sharing a topic. A test on the roots × themes table identifies branching roots and convergent themes, and pairs of roots are fol-lowed over time. Every structure is reported with its persistence across numbers of neighbours, which on synthetic data identifies most false branching. Applica-tions to Web of Science, Scopus and OpenAlex collections show that the two layers carry largely complementary information, and reveal substantive and methodolo-gical bases that have grown into several topics. The framework is implemented in 

bibliometrix .

Keywords: bibliographic coupling, science mapping, multiplex networks, research fronts, thematic analysis, bibliometrix 1arXiv:2610.21858v1 [cs.DL] 15 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Two scientific documents can be close in two ways: they can cite the same works, or they can write about the same things. Science mapping has a method for each. Bibliographic coupling (Kessler, 1963) relates documents through their shared references and groups them into research fronts defined by a common intellectual base (Boyack & Klavans, 2010; Persson, 1994); co-word analysis (Callon et al., 1983) and its variants relate them through their shared terms and describe the conceptual structure of a field. In the workflow of Aria and Cuccurullo (2017), the two methods map different knowledge structures of the same collection, and the analyst reads one map at a time. The two kinds of proximity need not coincide, and often they do not. Documents that draw on the same base may apply it to unrelated subjects, as when a methodological literature is cited across a whole discipline; documents that cite different bases may meet on the same subject. Comparisons of citation-based and text-based maps of the same documents find that the two partitions overlap little (Boyack & Klavans, 2020; Xie & Waltman, 2025). A map built on one kind of proximity cannot show where it disagrees with the other. The established answer to this disagreement is hybrid clustering, which combines citation links and lexical similarity into a single measure and clusters the documents once (Janssens, Glänzel, & De Moor, 2008; Liu et al., 2010; Thijs & Glänzel, 2018; Thijs, Schiebel, & Glänzel, 2013). Hybrid measures have been found to give more accurate partitions than either signal alone (Boyack & Klavans, 2020; Liu et al., 2010), which is what matters for delineating topics. For understanding how a field is structured, however, merging the two signals discards the information carried by their disagreement. A hybrid partition can no longer tell whether a group of documents shares references but not topics, or topics but not references. Multiplex coupling combines the two kinds of layer without merging them. Compar-ing two layers on the same documents raises methodological problems that single-layer mapping does not face. Similarities in the two layers are on different scales and have to be standardised before they can be compared. Clusters found by modularity optimisation 2change with the random order of the algorithm and with the density of the graph, so a relation found between the clusters of the two layers may be an artefact of an unstable partition. We address, in particular, three research questions: 

RQ1 – How far do the intellectual base and the topics of a field agree on the same documents, and how can their agreement be measured without the bias introduced by the selection of neighbours? 

RQ2 – Which intellectual roots have grown into several themes, which themes have been reached from different roots, and how can these structures be distinguished from artefacts of the clustering? 

RQ3 – How do the relations between roots, in references and in topics, change over time? We answer these questions with a framework that treats the two kinds of proximity as two layers of a multiplex network on the same documents (Battiston, Nicosia, & Latora, 2014; Kivelä et al., 2014). Each layer is standardised against a null model, so that sim-ilarities in references and in terms become comparable, and the agreement between the layers is measured on random pairs of documents and tested by permutation. Consensus clustering then partitions the references layer into roots , groups of documents that share an intellectual base, and the topic layer into themes , groups that share a topic. A single rule on their cross-tabulation tells which roots have grown into several themes and which themes have been reached from several roots, and pairs of roots are placed on the plane of their proximity in references and in topics and followed over time. Robustness is part of the result rather than a check made afterwards: every link and structure is reported with its persistence across numbers of neighbours, which on synthetic data turns out to be the diagnostic of false branching. We apply the framework to a collection of management studies from Web of Science, report two further collections from Scopus and OpenAlex in the Supplementary Material, assess it on synthetic collections with a planted structure, and make it available in bibliometrix and Biblioshiny. Section 2 reviews the background and Section 3 presents the framework. Section 4 ap-plies it to the management collection and Section 5 assesses it on synthetic data. Section 6 3discusses results and limitations, and Section 7 concludes. 

# 2 Background 

2.1 Citation-based and word-based science mapping 

Science mapping represents the relational structure of a research field from bibliographic data (Börner, Chen, & Boyack, 2003; Cobo et al., 2011; Zupic & Čater, 2015). Two of its oldest methods use the references of documents. In bibliographic coupling (Kessler, 1963) two documents are related when they cite the same works; in co-citation analysis (Small, 1973) two works are related when they are cited together. Persson (1994) gave the two methods distinct objects: the co-cited works form the intellectual base of a field, and the citing documents grouped by coupling form its research fronts . Among the citation approaches, bibliographic coupling has been found to represent the research front of a set of documents more accurately than co-citation (Boyack & Klavans, 2010), while direct citation gives the most accurate taxonomies of large literatures (Klavans & Boyack, 2017). Co-word analysis (Callon, Courtial, & Laville, 1991; Callon et al., 1983) relates terms that occur in the same documents, and the clusters of the resulting network are read as the themes of a field, its conceptual structure. With a Document × Attribute matrix A,coupling is AA ⊤, whereas co-citation and co-word analysis are A⊤A (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017). Coupling can be applied to attributes other than references: author keyword coupling relates authors through the keywords they use (Yang et al., 2016), and in the same way documents can be coupled through their terms. The two layers of the present framework are of this kind, two couplings of the same documents, one by references and one by terms. Science mapping software computes these structures separately (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Cobo et al., 2011), and their comparison is left to the analyst. The relation between citation-based and word-based structures has been studied since Braam, Moed, and van Raan (1991), who characterised co-citation clusters by the words of the documents citing them. They found that co-citation analysis does identify subject-matter specialties, but that different clusters may identify the same specialty. Later 4work moved from comparing the two structures to combining them. Hybrid clustering combines citation links and lexical similarity into a single measure, either by a weighted combination of the two similarity matrices or by integrating the partitions obtained from each (Janssens, Glänzel, & De Moor, 2008; Liu et al., 2010). The approach has been refined with second-order similarities (Thijs, Schiebel, & Glänzel, 2013), used to detect emerging topics (Glänzel & Thijs, 2012), and assessed for the contribution of its lexical component (Thijs & Glänzel, 2018). Citation-based, text-based and combined relatedness measures have been compared experimentally (Ahlgren & Colliander, 2009), and then on large collections against principled accuracy criteria (Ahlgren et al., 2020; Waltman et al., 2020). On nine million biomedical documents, Boyack and Klavans (2020) found that a hybrid of direct citation and textual relatedness gives the most accurate clusters, and that the clusters of the citation-based and text-based models differ in content more than in accuracy: the two logics are complementary. A parallel line of work compares the two kinds of structure without merging them. At the level of institutions, Yan and Ding (2012) found that coupling, citation and co-citation networks resemble each other, as do topical and co-word networks, and that the main dimension separating the networks is citation-based versus non-citation-based; they recommended hybrid or heterogeneous networks for the study of scholarly communication. Bascur et al. (2025) found that the same topic categories are well or poorly represented in maps built from citation and from text similarity, with citation networks performing better for some categories and for small clusters. Xie and Waltman (2025) compared citation-based clustering with topic modelling on cardiovascular research and found the relations between clusters and topics generally weak: citation clusters reflect the intellec-tual structure and its micro-communities, while topics better capture the societal needs related to the disease. These comparisons establish that citation-based and word-based partitions of the same documents differ, and differ systematically. They measure the difference for the map as a whole or for categories of topics. They do not identify which clusters of one partition are related to which clusters of the other beyond chance, nor which citation clusters are 5spread over several topics. 

2.2 Multiplex and multilayer networks 

A multiplex network is a set of nodes connected by several types of edges, one layer for each type; it is the special case of a multilayer network in which every layer contains the same nodes (Kivelä et al., 2014). Several measures describe how a node or an edge is distributed across layers. The participation coefficient, used in Section 3.2, measures how evenly the edges of a node are spread over the layers, and edge overlap measures how many pairs of nodes are connected in more than one layer (Battiston, Nicosia, & Latora, 2014). At the level of the whole network, De Domenico et al. (2015) asked how many layers are needed to represent a system and proposed to aggregate layers that carry redundant information. Community detection in multiplex networks usually looks for communities across lay-ers. In the multislice modularity of Mucha et al. (2010), each node is connected to its copies in the other layers, and a coupling parameter sets how strongly the partition of one layer is tied to that of the others. This is the multilayer counterpart of hybrid clustering: the layers inform a partition, and their differences are a nuisance to be controlled. Aria, Cuccurullo, et al. (2024) compared the conceptual structures of different units of obser-vation, such as institutions, in a joint representation; the comparison is between groups of documents on the same layer, not between layers on the same documents. Existing approaches either merge the citation and the lexical signal into one partition, as hybrid clustering and multislice community detection do, or compare the two signals globally, as the comparisons of networks and maps do. What is missing is a method that partitions the two layers of the same documents separately, tests which clusters of one are related to which clusters of the other, classifies the resulting structures, and reports how robust they are and how they change over time. # 3 The multiplex coupling framework 

Let D = {d1, . . . , d N } denote a collection of documents, each described by the references it cites and by the terms it uses. The framework represents D as a multiplex network with two layers on the same nodes. In the references layer two documents are close when they cite the same works; in the topic layer, when they use the same terms. Both layers are products AA ⊤ of a Document × Attribute matrix (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017, 2026). The references layer is bibliographic coupling itself (Kessler, 1963); the topic layer couples documents through their terms. The analytical goal is threefold. First, to measure how far the two layers agree on the same documents. Second, to partition each layer into clusters, the roots of the references layer and the themes of the topic layer, and to identify which roots feed which themes. Third, to compare the roots with each other and to follow how their relations change over time. Why “roots” The clusters of the references layer are called roots , for intellectual roots. In the classical reading of citation analysis the works cited by a group of documents are the roots on which it builds: the shared exemplars through which a community recognises its problems and methods (Kuhn, 1970), the intellectual base that Persson (1994) distinguishes from the research front citing it. Havemann (2021) delimits topics by clustering the citation links to highly cited sources; a root is instead a group of citing documents, a research front (Boyack & Klavans, 2010) named after the works it cites in common. It is not a research school, a group held together by training, institutions and a leader (Geison, 1981; Morrell, 1972): coupling data observe none of these ties. 

Why “themes” The clusters of the topic layer are called themes , for conceptual themes. In co-word analysis the clusters of the network of terms are read as the themes of a field, which together form its conceptual structure (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Callon, Courtial, & Laville, 1991). A theme of the framework is a group of documents rather than of terms: the documents that use the same concepts, whatever they cite. Roots and themes are thus two readings of the same documents, by the base they draw on and by the concepts they use. Each root is named by the titles of its strong references. 3.6 Pairs of roots, static and over time 

The links relate roots to themes; the framework also relates roots to each other. Two roots are distinct groups in the references layer by construction, but they may be more or less distant in it, and they may or may not write about the same subjects. Reading every pair of roots in both layers at once separates four relations between intellectual bases. Two roots close in both layers share part of their base and apply it to the same subjects. Two 15 roots close in references only share part of their base but apply it to different subjects. Two roots close in topics only are the cluster-level counterpart of convergence: different bases that have reached the same subjects. Two roots close in neither are unrelated. The lifts of Eq. (7) measure the two proximities on the same scale, so each pair of roots is described by ( λref , λ top ), and the value 1, the proximity of two random documents, separates close from not close in each layer. When two roots close in topics are both linked to the same theme, that theme names what brings them together. These relations are not fixed. Bases that start apart may come to write about the same subjects, and bases that once shared their topics may specialise. The evolutionary view follows the same two lifts over time. Longitudinal science mapping usually clusters each period separately and then links the clusters of successive periods (Aria, D’Aniello, Misuraca, & Spano, 2026). Here documents belong to one period only, so clusters found separately in each period could not be matched through their members. The roots are therefore defined once, on the whole collection, and in each period the lifts are computed on the documents of that period, relative to two random documents of the same period. A root is followed in the periods in which it has enough documents. For each pair of roots seen in at least two periods, the slopes of log 2 λref and log 2 λtop on the period index summarise the trend. With a threshold m on the slopes, a pair of roots is converging 

when it gets closer in topics but not in references, diverging when it gets farther in topics but not in references, consolidating when it gets closer in both and drifting apart when it gets farther in both; when its topic proximity does not change, it is closer in references 

or farther in references if its reference proximity does, and stable otherwise. Pairs that move from one of the four relations to another are the ones that change relation. Two displays are proposed for this reasoning. 3.7 Robustness to the number of neighbours 

The number of neighbours k sets how dense the two graphs are, and so how fine roots and themes are. As k grows, clusters tend to merge rather than reshuffle, so that k acts largely as the resolution does in publication-level classifications, where the granularity has to be chosen against an external criterion (Sjögårde & Ahlgren, 2020); partitions found at different k are nonetheless different. Rather than fixing a single value, the framework reports which of its conclusions persist. Only the neighbour graphs depend on k; the layers, the weights and the documents do not. Roots and themes are therefore found again at other values of k, below and above the one chosen, from the same layers, with all other choices unchanged; the partitions are those of a new analysis with that k, at the cost of the clustering only. A link between Ra

and Tb persists at another k when most of its nab documents fall in a single cell of the new 17 roots × themes table that is itself a link; a root or a theme keeps its structure when most of its documents fall in a new root or theme with the same structure. The persistence of a link, root or theme is the share of the other values of k at which this happens. The interpretation rests on the links and the structures that persist at every k.

# 4 Empirical application 

The framework was applied to three collections from three bibliographic sources, chosen to differ in field, in size and in the way cited references are recorded. The manage-ment collection is the one used throughout Aria and Cuccurullo (2026): 2,063 original research articles in English, indexed by Web of Science between 1985 and 2025, that use bibliometric or scientometric methods in management and business. The explainable AI collection contains 3,628 articles from Scopus whose titles mention “explainable artificial intelligence”, “explainable AI” or “XAI” (2017–2025). The disinformation collection con-tains 2,272 journal articles from OpenAlex (Priem, Piwowar, & Orr, 2022) with the term in the title (2000–2025, duplicates removed). The OpenAlex data, the identifiers of the Scopus records and the scripts of all analyses are deposited on Zenodo (Aria, Cuccurullo, & Spano, 2026). This section presents the management collection, a field whose literature is familiar to most readers and easy to interpret. The other two collections are reported in full in the Supplementary Material (Section S5), with their data, analysis, results and interpretation; the findings they share with the management collection are taken up in the conclusions. 

Analytical choices A document enters the analysis when it has at least one cited reference and at least one term. The topic layer is built from the author keywords and the Keywords Plus, since these are available for almost all documents; titles and abstracts would be used instead if keywords were missing for a large share of the collection. Terms used by a single document are removed, and so are the placeholders that Web of Science writes for unreadable references (“NO TITLE CAPTURED”, “ANONYMOUS”), which would couple unrelated documents. The keywords were cleaned with lists of synonyms and 18 terms to remove: spelling variants, singular and plural forms and acronyms were merged into one term, and the terms of the search query, generic words and indexing boilerplate were removed; the lists are available with the replication material. The analysis uses 

k = 10 nearest neighbours, B = 99 randomised matrices and α = 0 .05 for the null model, a random sample of 10 5 pairs for the association, consensus partitions of 20 runs, and the link rule with z = 2 and nmin = 5. Clusters of fewer than five documents are not kept, and their documents are reported as being in no root or in no theme. Persistence is measured at k = 5 and k = 20. In the evolution, a root is followed in a period when it has at least five documents in it, and the threshold on the slopes is m = 0 .1. These are the choices of a first analysis, not part of the method; Section 4.3 varies them. All the analyses were run with the development version of bibliometrix (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017), available on GitHub, in which the framework is implemented (Supplementary Material, Section S7).

4.3 Sensitivity and stability 

Table 5 varies one choice at a time on the management collection. The number of neigh-bours is the parameter with the largest effect: the adjusted Rand index with the baseline partition is 0 .54 for the roots and 0 .28 for the themes at k = 5, and 0 .74 and 0 .39 at 

k = 20. Most of this effect is a change of granularity: as k grows, clusters mostly merge rather than reshuffle (Supplementary Material, Section S4, which also compares altern-ative constructions of the graph). Restricting the analysis to a subset of the most cited documents (i.e., 1,000 documents) changes the roots substantially (0 .44), because it re-moves the recent documents, which are the least cited; so does weighting references by idf (0 .35). Titles and abstracts give themes that agree little with those from keywords (0 .12), which is why the source of topics is reported with the results. Association strength makes the topic layer degenerate (9 themes, NMI 0 .14). Without keyword cleaning the themes change too (0 .28). The threshold on the documents of a link and the minimum size of a cluster change only the links and the smallest clusters. Consensus clustering is what makes the partitions reproducible. Across five seeds far apart, single runs of Louvain agree with each other at 0 .47–0 .76 for the roots and 0 .43–0 .44 for the themes (mean adjusted Rand index, three collections; Supplementary Material, Section S5); consensus partitions agree at 0 .82–0 .94 and 0 .76–0 .80.

# 6 Discussion 

The framework rests on one premise: the references and the topics of the same documents describe a field in two ways that do not coincide, and the points where they disagree are worth studying. The three collections, the management collection of Section 4 and the two reported in the Supplementary Material (Section S5), confirm the premise: in all of them the correlation between the two similarities on random pairs is positive but low, from 0 .04 to 0 .17, and the two layers carry largely complementary information. 

6.1 What the second layer adds 

Taken alone, the references layer gives clusters close to those of ordinary bibliographic coupling (Supplementary Material, Section S6). What the framework adds is the relation 30 between two partitions. A hybrid clustering (Boyack & Klavans, 2020; Janssens, Glänzel, & De Moor, 2008; Thijs & Glänzel, 2018) resolves the disagreement between references and terms inside a combined similarity, where it is no longer visible. Comparisons of citation-based and text-based maps also report limited overlap between them (Boyack & Klavans, 2020; Xie & Waltman, 2025): low agreement between the layers appears to be common, and the question is what it means. The structures of Section 3.4 give three answers. A branching root is an intellectual base whose documents write about different things. In the management collection, the largest root (R1) has this form because its documents share the methodological literature of bibliometric reviews and apply it to unrelated subjects; a hybrid clustering would split it among the subjects, or keep it and blur them. A convergent theme is a subject reached from different bases, as patents and technology indicators are reached from the economics of public science and from technology forecasting. Braam, Moed, and van Raan (1991) had observed that different co-citation clusters may identify the same specialty; convergent themes turn that observation into a structure, defined by a test and checked for persistence. Finally, the plane of the pairs of roots shows bases that remain distinct in what they cite while coming to write about the same subjects. In the terms of Persson (1994), the roots are research fronts identified through their intellectual base, and the framework connects them to the topics of the same documents. 

6.2 Robustness as part of the result 

Clusters found by modularity optimisation depend on chance and on choices that the data do not fix. Chance is addressed by consensus clustering, which raises the agreement between seeds from 0 .43–0 .44 to 0 .76–0 .80 for the themes (Section 4.3). The number of neighbours cannot be addressed in the same way, because different values of k give partitions of different granularity, and the appropriate granularity can only be chosen against an external criterion (Sjögårde & Ahlgren, 2020). The framework therefore treats the dependence on k as information: a link, a root or a theme that persists at k = 5, 10 and 20 does not depend on the granularity chosen. This is also the most effective protection 31 against false branching, which comes from themes that split: among the branching roots that persist at every k only 2.4% are false, against 35% among the others (Section 5.2). We recommend reading the persistent structures first and treating the others as hypotheses. Further recommendations for use are given in the Supplementary Material. How the association between layers is measured matters beyond this framework. Schol-arly networks are often compared after sparsification, by thresholds or by nearest neigh-bours (e.g., Yan & Ding, 2012). The agreement between two layers should be measured on pairs selected on neither. 

6.3 Limitations 

First, themes are less stable than roots. With a few keywords per document the topic layer is sparser and noisier, and single runs of the clustering agree at 0 .45 for the themes of the management collection against 0 .74 for the roots. The errors of the framework originate in the themes: the false branching of the synthetic collections and the convergence that does not persist in the disinformation collection (Supplementary Material, Section S5). Second, persistence identifies the conclusions that do not depend on k but does not remove the dependence. Alternative constructions of the graphs, based on shared neigh-bours, on the significance of similarities or on several values of k at once, do not remove it either (Supplementary Material, Section S4). Third, the topic layer depends on its source: keywords and the terms of titles and abstracts give themes that agree little (0 .12), and the framework cannot say which is right. Keyword cleaning relies on lists prepared by the analyst. Fourth, the framework requires cited references in a usable form. Scopus needs refer-ence matching (Aria, D’Aniello, & Spano, 2026). In OpenAlex the coverage of references is comparable to that of Web of Science and Scopus on recent publications indexed by all three (Culbert et al., 2025), but references are missing for many records, more often for lower-impact journals and less prestigious institutions (Zhou, Liang, & Acuna, 2026), and for publications not indexed in the other two sources, as shown for African research (Alonso-Álvarez & van Eck, 2025). Only 1,521 of the 2,272 documents of the disinforma-32 tion collection entered the analysis, and the roots describe the documents whose references are recorded. Sources that do not export cited references cannot be analysed. Fifth, roots are defined once on the whole collection. This makes them comparable across periods, but a root that splits over time remains one root, and in growing fields most pairs of roots are followed in two periods only (15 of 78 in the management collection are followed in three or more). Sixth, the names of the roots are an aid to interpretation, not a result. They rest on the strong references whose title can be found in the collection, in the reference strings or in OpenAlex, and on their sources when fewer than five titles are found. As with any algorithmically constructed topical structure, whose meaning has to be checked against the substance of the field (Held & Velden, 2022), the analyst should read the strong references of a root before interpreting it. Seventh, the synthetic collections plant a few structures with fixed pools of references and terms and no change over time, so the evolution of pairs of roots has been assessed on real data only. 

# 7 Conclusions 

This paper has introduced multiplex coupling, a framework that couples the same docu-ments by their references and by their topics, keeps the two layers separate and studies how they relate. The agreement between the layers (RQ1) is measured on random pairs of documents and tested by permutation. In the three collections analysed it is low, and this is the main rationale for a multilayer analysis: the two layers carry complementary information, and reading them together enriches what coupling alone tells about a field. Roots that have grown into several themes and themes reached from several roots (RQ2) are identified by a single rule on the roots × themes table; on synthetic data the planted structures are recovered, and the false branching that unstable themes produce is recog-nised by its lack of persistence across numbers of neighbours. The relations between roots (RQ3) are placed on the plane of their lifts in references and in topics and followed over periods, which shows pairs of bases that remain distinct in what they cite while converging 33 on the same subjects. 

Future developments Three developments follow from the limitations of Section 6.3. The first is to add further layers on the same documents, such as the cited sources or direct citation, which would turn the comparison of two partitions into the comparison of several. The second is to let roots change over time, finding them in each period and connecting them by measures of lineage between periods, so that a root that forms or splits can be observed. The third is to strengthen the topic layer, where most of the instability lies. The similarity between documents in this layer could be measured on embeddings of their titles, abstracts, keywords or full texts, which represent content densely rather than through a few shared terms: two documents that share no keyword could still be found close, and themes should become as stable as roots are. Embeddings learnt from text alone would be needed, since models trained on citation links would bring the references back into the topic layer.

S1 Implementation details and recommendations for use 

Nearest neighbours For each document, the candidate neighbours are the documents with a strictly positive similarity. When there are more than k, the k most similar are kept, ties being broken by the order of the documents. The neighbour relation is made symmetric by union, not by mutual selection: a pair is kept when either document is among the k nearest neighbours of the other, so a document may have more than k neighbours. 

Null model Each randomisation applies 5N curveball trades (Strona et al., 2014) to the observed binary matrix of a layer; each of the B randomisations starts from the observed matrix. The weights of a document (binary, or 1 + ln tf for titles and abstracts) are permuted among its new features, and the idf weights are those of the observed matrix, which the randomisation preserves. The p-value of Eq. (2) of the main text is one-sided, and no correction for multiple testing is applied: the four situations of the pairs are descriptive, and the framework draws no inference from single pairs. 

Association between the layers The random pairs are drawn uniformly with replacement and deduplicated, so their number can be slightly lower than requested. In the quadratic assignment test (Hubert & Schultz, 1976; Krackhardt, 1988) only the documents of the topic layer are permuted, with the same number of permutations B as the null model; the p-value is two-sided, on the absolute value of Spearman’s correlation. 

Consensus clustering The consensus, which follows Lancichinetti and Fortunato (2012), is com-puted on the edges of the neighbour graph: at each iteration every edge is weighted by the share of the nruns runs that put its two ends in the same cluster, edges with a share below one half are dropped, and no edge is added. The graph therefore stays sparse, and two documents that are not neighbours are never joined. The runs use the seeds s, s + 1 , . . . , s + nruns − 1. Iterations stop when every edge has a share of 0 or 1, or after ten iterations; the partition returned is the first run of the last iteration, and the output reports the number of iterations and whether the runs agreed. The agreement between runs (mean adjusted Rand index) is measured at the first iteration, on the original graph. 

Cohesion and lift Cohesion and lift are always mean cosines over the full rows of the two layers, whatever similarity is used for the neighbours, and the mean cosine of two random documents includes the documents that are in no root or in no theme. 

Links The link rule ( rab > z and nab ≥ nmin ) is applied cell by cell without correction for multiple testing; the classification of roots and themes reads the same set of links by rows and by columns. 1Evolution A cutting year belongs to the earlier period. Lifts are floored at 1/16 before the logarithm, and slopes are estimated on the period index, not on the years. 

Recommendations for use Scopus data should be processed with reference matching, since without it the roots change almost completely (adjusted Rand index 0.09 ). The source of the topic layer should be stated and, when both are available, the analysis repeated with the other source. Keyword cleaning has a visible effect on the themes and should be reported with the lists used. Trends of pairs of roots are more reliable when the pair is followed in three or more periods, which in a growing field means choosing periods with enough documents in each. 

S2 Diagnosis of false branching 

Section 5.2 of the main text shows that false branching roots originate in unstable themes and that persistence across numbers of neighbours recognises them. This section reports the indicators that were assessed before reaching that conclusion. 

Indicators For every root classified as branching, four indicators can be computed on real data: • persistence : the persistence of its structure at k = 5 and k = 20 (Section 3.7 of the main text); • imbalance : the documents of its weakest link over those of its strongest; • separation : the mean cosine in the topic layer between the two most similar themes linked to the root, over the geometric mean of their internal mean cosines (close to 1 when the two themes are fragments of one); • noise : the agreement between single runs of the themes. On the development runs (the 54 collections of Table 6 of the main text), a root is labelled false when its documents come from a root planted with a single theme ( R4 or R5). Agreement between runs pointed the opposite way (AUC 0.15 : the false branching roots occurred in runs with higher agreement of the themes); being a property of the whole collection rather than of a root, it was set aside. 

A rule fixed in advance On the development runs, 4 of the 189 branching roots are false, and the rule “persistence below 1 and imbalance below 0.3” flags all of them and one true branching root. Since the thresholds had been chosen on four cases, the rule was fixed before generating new data and validated on 72 collections with new seeds (noise 0.5, 0.6, 0.7; balanced and unbalanced groups; m = 40 , 100, 250; four replicates). Out of sample it flags 11 of the 20 false branching roots and 7 true ones (precision 0.61 ). The validation runs contain a form of false branching absent from the development runs: at noise 0.7 the planted theme splits into two parts that receive links of comparable size from the same root, so that the imbalance is not low. Adding the separation of the themes catches more false branching roots but flags more true ones as well (Table S1): branching roots linked to themes with similar terms are common among true branching too. 

Conclusion Persistence alone has the highest discrimination out of sample (AUC 0.81 , against 

0.80 for separation and 0.69 for imbalance), and it is the only indicator that follows from the design of the framework rather than from thresholds tuned on the simulation. S5.1 Explainable AI (Scopus) 

Data The collection was exported from Scopus with the query 

TITLE("explainable artificial intelligence" OR "explainable AI" OR "XAI") AND DOCTYPE(ar) AND LANGUAGE(english) AND PUBYEAR > 2014 AND PUBYEAR < 2026 ,

which returned 3,629 articles, published from 2017 to 2025. Duplicate records of one paper, under two spellings of the journal name, were reduced to the record with more citations, leaving 3,628 documents. The topic layer is built from the author keywords and the indexed keywords, which cover 99% of the documents with references. The keywords were cleaned with 1,851 groups of synonyms (spelling variants, and acronyms such as SHAP, LIME and CNN merged with their full forms) and 32 terms to remove: the terms of the query, the check tags of medical indexing (human, female, adult, controlled study, and the like) and generic terms of the field (performance, accuracy, learning systems); the distinct keywords fall from 21,309 to 19,227. The cited references were processed with the reference matching of Aria, D’Aniello, and Spano (2026). Of the 3,628 documents, 13 have no cited references and 43 no keywords, and 84 lose all their references or all their keywords when the features used by a single document are removed; 3,488 documents enter the analysis. 8Analysis The references layer has 16,270 references cited by at least two documents and the topic layer 5,384 terms. Only 2.3% of the neighbour pairs are neighbours in both layers, and on random pairs the correlation between the two similarities is 0.04 : different from zero (QAP p = 0 .01 , the smallest value attainable with B = 99 ), but close to it. Two documents that cite the same works are hardly more likely than two random ones to share keywords. Single runs of the clustering agree at 0.55 for the roots and 0.45 for the themes (mean adjusted Rand index); the consensus partitions are reported. 

Effect of the reference matching Scopus writes a cited work in several forms, which coupling counts as different works. The matching reduces the distinct reference strings from 177,225 to 147,228, but raises the references cited by at least two documents from 8,694 to 16,270: variants of one work, each cited once and therefore discarded, become one reference cited many times. More documents then share a reference with another (3,488 instead of 3,248), and the neighbour pairs of the references layer rise from 21,212 to 26,950. The roots fall from 30 to 22, the branching roots rise from 10 to 16, and the links from 36 to 50. On the documents analysed in both runs, the roots before and after the matching agree poorly (adjusted Rand index 0.09 ). The themes agree at 

0.55 : they do not depend on the references and change only because the set of documents analysed changes, so that their agreement is of the order of that between single runs of the themes. Without the matching, the variants of a cited work split its base into several roots; the results below use the matched references. 

Results The references layer gives 22 roots (16 branching, 2 consolidated and 4 dispersed) and the topic layer 29 themes (13 convergent, 13 consolidated and 3 dispersed), connected by 50 links (Tables S8 and S9, Figs. S2 and S3). Of the links, 20 persist at every k and 13 at neither; 8 of the branching roots and 6 of the convergent themes persist. On the plane of the pairs of roots (Fig. S4), 122 of the 231 pairs are close in neither layer, 94 in topics only, 6 in references only and 9 in both. That so many pairs are close in topics only reflects a vocabulary shared across the field: machine learning, artificial intelligence and deep learning are among the characteristic keywords of many roots, which nevertheless cite different works. 

Interpretation The roots separate the field by the family of models and the explanation technique its documents rely on. R2 is the base of visual explanations for deep networks: its strongest references are the papers that introduced Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) and class activation mapping and the deep convolutional architectures they are applied to. R3 is the base of tree ensembles: random forests (Breiman, 2001), gradient boosting, support-vector networks and XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016). R4 is the base of model-agnostic explanation, LIME (Ribeiro, Singh, & Guestrin, 2016) and SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), and R1, the largest root, that of the surveys and conceptual papers on explainability, black-box models and the social sciences of explanation. The persistent branching roots are the technical bases. R2 feeds the theme on Grad-CAM and transfer learning (T4), magnetic resonance imaging and brain tumours (T6) and plant diseases (T23), with all three links persisting; R3 feeds remote sensing and spatial analysis (T7), XGBoost and Shapley additive explanations (T9), and Shapley explanations in financial fraud detection (T11), again with persistent links. A visual explanation method for convolutional networks thus travels from one base into medical imaging and agriculture, and tree ensembles with their explanations into environmental, spatial and financial applications. Smaller roots branch from an application domain rather than from a technique: R9, whose strong references join explanations for tree models with the surgical literature on ovarian cancer, feeds the themes on random forests and receiver operating 9characteristic analysis (T1) and on XGBoost (T9); R19, on chest X-ray images and COVID-19, feeds the themes on COVID-19 (T14) and on Grad-CAM (T4). For R10 and R13 the branching persists although none of their links does: these roots keep feeding several themes, but not the same ones at every k.The persistent convergent themes are application domains in which several bases meet. Remote sensing (T7) draws on tree ensembles (R3), on the base that joins layer-wise relevance propagation with landslide susceptibility (R6) and on the hedonic models of housing prices (R16); the theme on random forests and receiver operating characteristic analysis (T1) draws on the toolbox of scikit-learn, resampling and heart disease prediction (R7) and on the ovarian cancer base (R9). The conceptual base R1 does not branch at every k: its persistent link is to the theme on explainability itself (T2). In this field, explanation methods move from a few technical bases into application domains, while the conceptual literature on explainability keeps its own topics. The whole analysis, from the two layers to roots, themes, links and persistence, takes 29 seconds on 2,000 documents, 62 on 5,000 and 130 on 10,000 (random subsets of a larger OpenAlex collection on disinformation and misinformation, downloaded with openalexR (Aria et al., 2024); Apple M4 Pro 14 cores, three replicates). Cohesion and lift need no enumeration of pairs (Proposition 1 of the main text), and persistence reuses the layers. The roots are close to the clusters of the single-layer coupling of bibliometrix (couplingMap() 

on references; normalised mutual information 0.28 –0.38 across the three collections), whereas the themes are not close to the clusters of the coupling by keywords ( 0.10 –0.18 ). Part of the difference may come from the graphs, since the themes are communities of the nearest-neighbour graph. What the framework adds to single-layer coupling is in any case not a different partition but the relation between two partitions. 

S7 Implementation in bibliometrix 

The framework is implemented in the R package bibliometrix (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017). All the analyses of the paper were run with its development version, which can be installed from the 

feature/multiplex branch of its GitHub repository: 

install.packages("remotes") remotes::install_github("massimoaria/bibliometrix", ref = "feature/multiplex") 

The analysis is organised in five functions, which follow the three phases of Fig. 1 of the main text, and one plotting function. Table S12 lists them, and Table S13 gives the arguments that correspond to the notation of the main text, with the values used in the paper, which are also the defaults. Function What it does 

multiplexCoupling() builds the two layers and their weights, the nearest-neighbour graphs, the null model, the association between the layers with its QAP test, and the node indices (Phase I) 

multiplexClusters() finds roots and themes by consensus clustering, names them, and computes the roots × themes table, the residuals, the links, the structures, cohesion and lift (Phase II) 

multiplexRobustness() clusters the same layers again at other values of k and computes the persistence of links, roots and themes 

multiplexEvolution() computes the lifts of the pairs of roots in each period, their slopes and trends (Phase III) 

multiplexPairs() lists the pairs of roots followed over time, with the areas of the plane they cross and their trend

The keyword cleaning is passed to multiplexCoupling() through synonyms , a vector whose elements list synonyms separated by “;” and merged into the first of them, and remove.terms , a vector of terms to remove; the lists used in the paper are in the replication material. Each function returns the object it received with its results added, so the analysis can be resumed at any step: 

multiplexRobustness() , for instance, reuses the layers and reruns only the clustering. Every randomisation (null model, QAP test, consensus clustering) is controlled by the argument seed ,and the random number state of the session is restored on exit, so that two runs with the same arguments give the same results. 

Results The object returned by multiplexClusters() holds, in $clusters , the roots and the themes with their size, name, structure and cohesion in both layers ( roots , themes ), the links with their documents, residual and persistence ( links ), the roots × themes table and its residuals, the lifts of every pair of roots ( plane ), and the normalised mutual information and adjusted Rand index between the two partitions. The membership of every document in a root and a theme, and its 22 node indices, are in $nodes ; the association between the layers and the documents left out, with the reason, in $info . The plots are ggplot2 objects, or interactive plotly widgets with interactive = TRUE ; with greyscale = TRUE the static plots tell structures and trends apart by shapes and line types as well as by colour, as in the figures of the paper. 

Names of the roots Roots are named after the titles of their strong references (Section 3.3 of the main text). The titles come from the collection when a cited work is itself one of its documents, from the reference string when the source writes the cited title, as Scopus does, and otherwise from OpenAlex, by DOI or OpenAlex identifier. OpenAlex is queried only when an API key and an e-mail address are configured ( multiplexClusters(..., email, api.key) ), with a time limit (openalex.timeout ); the titles are cached for the R session, and openalex = FALSE names the roots from the local information only. 

Biblioshiny In Biblioshiny, the web interface of bibliometrix , the analysis is available under Coup-ling > Multiplex Coupling. The parameters of Table S13 are set in the panel of the analysis, the keyword cleaning uses the lists of synonyms and terms to remove of the other text analyses, and the plots are interactive. In the SAAS workflow of Aria, Cuccurullo, D’Aniello, and Spano (2026) the analysis belongs to the Synthesis phase, where the structures of a field are interpreted.
