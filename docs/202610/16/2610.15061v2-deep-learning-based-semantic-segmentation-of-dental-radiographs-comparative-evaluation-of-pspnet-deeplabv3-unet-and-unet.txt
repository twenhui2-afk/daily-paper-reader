## ABSTRACT 

Accurate and efficient analysis of dental radiographs has become increasingly important for disease diagnosis and clinical decision-making. In particular, dental radiographs segmentation plays a critical role as it is challenging due to several reasons, such as low contrast, noise in the images, variations in the image quality, etc. Previous studies have primarily addressed these challenges using convolutional neural networks (CNN)-based models. However, direct comparison of these segmentation models is still challenging due to the variations among the experimental settings. Therefore, we mainly compared four segmentation models, including Pyramid Scene Parsing Network (PSPNet), DeepLabV3, UNet, and UNet++, through a fair, controlled experimentation setting under varying conditions such as image sizes and batch sizes. For this, we used 1269 dental radiography images, and prior to training, we implemented a series of preprocessing techniques. To assess the models, we used several metrics, including Dice coefficient (DICE), Intersection over Union (IoU), accuracy, precision, training and validation losses. According to our results, UNet++ demonstrated the highest IoU score of 0.6719, and a DICE value of 0.7869, showing superior results in terms of segmentation, specifically when configured with an image size of 300×300 and a batch size of 16. In addition, we also explored the effect of image resolutions and batch sizes on model performance and accuracy under different architectures. Therefore, this study provides insights into the strengths of different segmentation models and the potential of deep learning–based semantic segmentation methods for improving automated dental radiograph analysis.

## 1. INTRODUCTION 

Teeth segmentation and orientation are fundamental in modern oral healthcare, enabling precise diagnosis, treatment planning, and dental implant design (Dhar et al. , 2023). Dental radiography serves as a primary medium for diagnosis, treatment planning, and the longitudinal monitoring of oral health based applications (Rad et al. , 2013; Brahmi, Jdey and Drira, 2024). For dental practitioners, the ability to separate individual teeth and structures is essential for automatic dental chart filing, which reduces administrative burdens, enables the identification of specific pathologies or anatomical anomalies with high precision, and creates a structured dataset for further diagnostic analysis. 

In the past, dental structures were identified and labelled manually which is a time-consuming process that is prone to inter-observer variability. To overcome these inefficiencies researchers have shifted towards automated segmentation frameworks (Brahmi, Jdey and Drira, 2024; Zhou et al. , 2025). Recent advances in deep learning (DL), especially convolutional neural networks and UNet architectures, have set a new standard for accuracy. DL frameworks are capable of processing dental images with the high complexity and noise that traditional methods cannot handle, offering high-precision tooth instance segmentation (Fındık et al. , 2025) and transparent, verifiable results to the clinicians. This also enables the creation of diagnostic reports by automatically filling in dental charts (Muramatsu et al. , 2021) transforming pixels directly into clinical documentation. Thus, it is evident that automated segmentation methods, particularly those based on deep learning and artificial intelligence, offer several transformative clinical advantages for contemporary dental diagnostic workflows. Instead of manual identification, dental practitioners can shift to automated identification with high precision, which helps them provide more accurate diagnosis and manage their administrative activities. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore and compare four deep learning-based semantic segmentation models using dental radiographs. 

This paper is structured as follows: In Section II we discuss the previous literature related to the topic of this study. We also describe the methodology that we followed in Section III while we explain the results that we obtained in detail in Section IV. Finally, we conclude the study in Section V. 

## 2. RELATED WORK 

Dental image analysis has evolved significantly with advances in image segmentation, classification, and explainability using machine learning and deep learning techniques. This section reviews key methodologies and contributions in tooth segmentation, classification, and the integration of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). 

Early research in dental radiography segmentation has explored traditional image processing methods. For instance, Rad et al. (2013) discussed existing methods of segmentation that have been applied to dental X-ray images, including thresholding and integral projection, active contour without edge, region growing, and level-set methods (Rad et al. , 2013). 

With the rise of deep learning, more robust and automated solutions have emerged. Muramatsu et al. (2020) investigated the feasibility of automatic filing of the dental chart using CNNs. The proposed method plays an important role in the automatic filing of dental charts for forensic identification and the preprocessing of dental images for prescreening purposes. In addition, the comparison of single and multi-sized input models in their paper highlighted the importance of neighboring information in tooth type classification.

This motivates the present work, which aims to use Deep Learning to perform dental radiography segmentation efficiently.

We selected these four representative segmentation architectures mainly due to their complementary strengths with PSPNet and DeepLabV3 offering strong multi-scale context modelling capabilities, and UNet, together with its advanced variant UNet++, demonstrating their proven effectiveness in biomedical segmentation through the use of skip connections and deep supervision. This selection allows us a balanced comparison between context-aggregation and precise localization strategies while using the same ResNet50 encoder across all models. 

Alongside these four models, we tested several combinations while training by varying parameters, such as image size and batch size, along with the encoder (ResNet50). The models were trained with an Adam optimizer with a learning rate and weight decay parameter of 0.0001. 

In addition, we used augmentation techniques during the training process to enhance the robustness of the results. The augmentation pipeline consisted of several metrics, including horizontal flipping (p=0.5), vertical flipping (p=0.3), random 90 degrees rotation (p=0.5), shift scale rotation (shift limit=0.1, scale limit=0.2, rotate limit =15, p=0.5), random brightness contrast adjustments (brightness limit=0.3, contrast limit=0.3, p=0.7), Gaussian noise injection (var limit=(10.0, 50.0), p=0.5), grid distortion (p=0.3), and elastic transformation (alpha = 1, sigma =50, alpha_affine = 50, p=0.3). 

In addition, input resolutions were carefully selected to align with the architectural characteristics of each model family while enabling a fair comparison of segmentation performance. Specifically, we chose image sizes as multiples (or near-multiples) of 32, corresponding to the effective down-sampling stride of the ResNet50 encoder used across all models. Accordingly, PSPNet and DeepLabV3 were trained using image sizes of 256, 304, and 384, while UNet and UNet++ were trained using input sizes of 224, 256, and 300. 

Moreover, batch sizes of 4, 8, and 16 were systematically evaluated to investigate the effect of batch size on training stability, generalization performance, and segmentation accuracy. 3.2 Evaluation Metrics 

To test the efficiency and select the best model, we mainly used three popular metrics: DICE, IoU, and accuracy. 

1. DICE 

The Dice coefficient (or Sørensen–Dice index) compares two sets (like pixel masks) to see how similar they are. After a model segments an image, the Dice score assesses how well the predicted mask matches the known correct mask. It can be calculated as in the following Equation (1). 

DICE = 2|A∩B|/(|A|+|B|) (1), 

where |A∩B| is the overlap (intersection) and |A|,|B| are the sizes of the predicted and ground truth masks, respectively. 

2. Intersection over Union (IoU) 

IoU is a crucial metric for assessing segmentation models, commonly referred to as Jaccard's Index, as it quantifies how well the model can distinguish objects from their backgrounds in an image. IoU is a popular metric to measure localization accuracy and compute localization errors in object detection models (Rezatofighi et al. , 2019). It calculates the amount of overlap between two bounding boxes: a predicted bounding box and a ground truth bounding box [16], as depicted in Equation (2). 

IoU = |A∩B|/(|A ∪B|) = (|I|)/(|U|) (2), 

Where A and B are the prediction and ground truth bounding boxes. 

3. Accuracy 

Accuracy is also used as an additional evaluation metric in this study to measure the proportion of correctly classified pixels. It can be calculated as shown in Equation (3). 

Accuracy = Number of correctly classified pixels/ Total number of pixels (3). 

## 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

In this study, we used different image sizes, including 256, 304, and 384 for PSPNet and DeepLabV3, and 224, 256, and 300 for UNet and UNet++ models, for the training purposes. Tables 1, 2, and 3 present the quantitative segmentation performance of four deep learning architectures, evaluated on the dental radiography dataset under varying image resolutions and batch sizes. We assessed the performance mainly by the DICE value, IoU value and pixel accuracy. In addition, we also considered the training and validation losses. 

Among encoder-decoder models, UNet demonstrated strong performance stability across different image resolutions, with peak performance observed at 256×256 resolution, achieving an IoU of 0.6336 and a Dice score of 0.7575 using a batch size of 8. Increasing the image resolution beyond this point resulted in marginal gains or slight degradation, suggesting diminishing returns due to increased spatial redundancy. 

On the other hand, the advantage of higher image resolution was more obvious in the case of PSPNet and DeepLabV3, specially at 384×384, with the IoU values of 0.6157 and 0.6338 , respectively. 

This improvement highlights the effectiveness of pyramid pooling and atrous convolution in capturing multi-scale contextual information in higher-resolution dental radiographs. Pixel accuracy remained consistently high (>97%) across all models and combinations. However, given the strong class imbalance between background and dental regions, accuracy alone was found to be insufficient for reliable performance assessment, reinforcing the importance of IoU and Dice metrics for semantic segmentation. 4.1 Effect of Image Resolution 

We can observe that the image resolution has ameasurable impact on segmentation quality across all models. Lower resolutions (ex, 224×224 or 256×256) enabled faster convergence but often resulted in reduced boundary precision, particularly for fine-grained dental structures such as roots and interproximal regions. 

Furthermore, higher resolutions (300×300 and 384×384) improved spatial detail representation, leading to increased IoU and Dice scores for context-aware architectures such as PSPNet and DeepLabV3. However, excessively larger input sizes introduced higher computational cost and increased susceptibility to overfitting, especially when batch sizes were constrained by GPU memory.

4.2 Effect of Batch Size 

In particular, batch size played a critical role in optimization stability and generalization. Smaller batch sizes, for example, 4, resulted in noisier gradient updates and less consistent validation performance. Conversely, batch sizes of 8 and 16 consistently produced higher IoU and Dice scores, particularly for UNet and UNet++ architectures. 

Notably, UNet++ demonstrated improved performance at larger batch sizes, likely due to its dense skip connections facilitating more stable gradient propagation during training. This trend was less pronounced in PSPNet, indicating architectural sensitivity to batch size variations. 

These results suggest that moderate-to-high resolutions (256–300) offer the best trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency for dental radiography tasks. Therefore, UNet++ was selected as the best-performing model for dental radiography image segmentation, particularly with an image size of 300×300 and a batch size of 16. From a clinical perspective, accurate segmentation of dental structures is essential for applications such as automated diagnosis, treatment planning, and longitudinal monitoring. Our results indicate that UNet++ based architectures provide a reliable and computationally efficient solution, making them suitable for real-time or resource-constrained clinical environments. While PSPNet and DeepLabV3 demonstrated competitive performance at higher resolutions, their increased computational overhead may limit practical deployment in routine dental workflows. 

## 5. CONCLUSION 

This study evaluated and compared the performance of four state-of-the-art deep learning architectures: PSPNet, DeepLabV3, UNet and UNet++ in dental radiography image segmentation. The experimentation with different image sizes and batch sizes revealed that UNet++ yields the best segmentation accuracy, specifically when using an image size of 300×300 and a batch size of 16, achieving a maximum IoU score of 0.6719. The results highlight the critical importance of hyperparameter tuning in optimizing segmentation models for dental imaging. Dental practitioners can improve diagnostic accuracy, optimize clinical workflows and deliver better patient outcomes by replacing manual techniques with fully automated, high-precision systems that enable more reliable treatment planning and monitoring. 

However, this study relies on a single publicly available dataset and may have limited generalizability. Future studies will validate these models on larger multi-institutional datasets with diverse imaging modalities like cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and intraoral scans. Explainable AI (XAI) techniques can also be incorporated to provide interpretable segmentation insights, possibly boosting clinical trust and adoption. In conclusion, the use of such automated systems in clinical practice has a great potential to develop digital dentistry and personalized oral healthcare.
