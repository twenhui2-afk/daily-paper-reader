Abstract 

The intermittent and variable nature of precipitation makes its accurate esti-mation over extended domains difficult, yet its spatiotemporal structure sug-gests that a low-rank representation may be possible. This work represents daily precipitation over the contiguous United States (CONUS) as spatiotem-poral tensors and applies CANDECOMP/PARAFAC factorization, showing that preserving the native spatial and temporal modes yields more accurate reconstruction than factorizing independent daily fields or unfolded space– time matrices. Building on this finding, this work presents TMerge, a tensor-based framework that integrates satellite precipitation with sparse reference observations through shared low-rank spatial and temporal factors. TMerge was applied to correct the IMERG Final Run product with climate prediction center reference observations over CONUS. During 2019–2022, TMerge in-creased correlation from 0.53 to 0.85 and reduced root-mean-square error and mean absolute error by 48.2% and 29.3%, respectively. TMerge consistently outperformed linear bias correction, quantile mapping, and neural networks across seasons, precipitation-intensity regimes, and regions. Improvements were spatially coherent and largest in coastal regions where IMERG errors were greatest. These results demonstrate that low-rank tensor structure par-simoniously approximates the dominant spatiotemporal variability of precip-itation and provides a practical mechanism for improving satellite estimates under limited reference observations over extended domains.

1. Introduction 

As the primary mechanism that delivers atmospheric water to the Earth’s surface, precipitation underpins water resources, food production, flood and drought risks, and economic activity worldwide (Kotz et al., 2022). Reliable precipitation estimates are therefore required for environmental assessment and decision-making (Portier et al., 2023). However, precipitation has asym-metric and intermittent spatiotemporal patterns, making its accurate estima-tion difficult, particularly over extended spatial and temporal domains (Sun et al., 2006; Pendergrass and Knutti, 2018; Lovejoy and Schertzer, 2013). The highly variable nature of precipitation is shaped by complex cloud mi-crophysical and convective processes that remain challenging to represent ex-plicitly in global models (Arakawa, 2004; Stevens and Feingold, 2009; Prein et al., 2023), and parameterized representations of these processes are associ-ated with substantial uncertainty (Bauer et al., 2015; Yuval and O’Gorman, 2020). Therefore, direct and indirect precipitation observations, together with the gridded products derived from them, remain central to character-izing precipitation variability, evaluating models, and supporting hydrologic and environmental applications (Sun et al., 2018). Multi-source merging is often used to develop precipitation products by integrating complementary observations (Kidd and Levizzani, 2011; Sun et al., 2026), as ground-based measurements are relatively accurate but spatially constrained, while satellite-based estimates, with broader coverage, vary in temporal sampling and retain retrieval uncertainties (Kidd and Huffman, 2011). Among various satellite precipitation products (Hsu et al., 1997; Joyce et al., 2004), the Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals for GPM (IMERG) is a leading near-global precipitation product developed within NASA’s Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) mission and succeeding the earlier TRMM multisatellite precipitation analysis (Huffman et al., 2007). IMERG com-bines passive microwave and infrared observations with monthly gauge ad-justment in the Final Run, providing precipitation estimates at 0.1 ◦ spatial resolution (Huffman et al., 2020). Nevertheless, errors remain substantial and heterogeneous; regional evaluations of the IMERG Final Run have re-ported daily root-mean-square error (RMSE) values of approximately 4.5–4.7 2mm day −1 over Europe and the contiguous United States (CONUS) (Massari et al., 2020). These errors are particularly pronounced in coastal zones, where sharp land–ocean emissivity gradients and coastal precipitation regimes, such as orographic enhancement, make satellite precipitation detection and esti-mation especially difficult, as evidenced by IMERG evaluations over coastal regions of the United States (Derin et al., 2021, 2022). These persistent errors have led to various correction and merging approaches to improve satellite precipitation products, from bias correction and distribution matching to ma-chine learning methods using auxiliary predictors (Manz et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2021; Dao et al., 2025; Baig et al., 2025). However, achieving consistent improvements across broad domains with diverse ter-rain, coastlines, and precipitation regimes remains a challenge and motivates a closer examination of the underlying structure of precipitation fields. Low-rank methods have been applied across several hydrologic applica-tions, including streamflow prediction (Zhang et al., 2023), soil-moisture re-construction (Beale et al., 2025), hydrogeologic parameter estimation (Zhuang et al., 2025), computationally efficient inversion (Zha et al., 2018), data as-similation (Ghorbanidehno et al., 2015), precipitation-monitoring network design (Çelik and Altunkaynak, 2024), and hydroclimatic variability char-acterization (Zhang et al., 2021). Empirical orthogonal function analysis, a common geophysical form of principal component analysis, has also been widely used to study precipitation variability through covariance decomposi-tion and low-rank analysis (Rao and Hsieh, 1991; Baldwin et al., 2009; Ben-estad et al., 2023). Precipitation exhibits a well-established spatiotemporal structure, as reflected in studies of multifractal scale invariance, stochas-tic rainfall modeling, and spatiotemporal modeling (Veneziano et al., 2006; Paschalis et al., 2013; Banerjee et al., 2023; Northrop, 2024). Precipitation’s inherently multidimensional spatiotemporal structure motivates moving be-yond conventional low-rank analysis. Tensor factorization extends low-rank modeling to multiway data, allowing spatial, temporal, and data-source di-mensions to be represented jointly (Liu et al., 2022). Tensor decomposi-tion methods have been widely used in remote sensing for image inpainting, hyperspectral restoration, and cloud removal (Ng et al., 2017; Zou et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Recently, spatiotemporal tensor approaches have also been applied to the reconstruction of environmental and geophysical data (Bai et al., 2022; Chu et al., 2022; Solgi et al., 2025). In hydrology, however, tensor factorization remains underexplored particularly for precip-itation estimation over extended regions, where spatial variability, temporal 3evolution, and multiple data sources must be modeled jointly. This work represents daily precipitation over extended spatiotemporal domains as tensors and applies CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) factoriza-tion (Kolda and Bader, 2009), allowing spatial variability and temporal evo-lution to be modeled jointly while preserving the original multidimensional structure of the data. Using daily precipitation data over CONUS during 2018–2022, with the climate prediction center (CPC) unified gauge-based analysis as the reference, we show that tensor factorization reveals a rela-tively strong low-rank signal. This allows a compact set of low-rank factors to parsimoniously represent precipitation variability across space and time, a representation not achieved by factorizing precipitation fields as independent daily snapshots or unfolded space–time matrices. To demonstrate the prac-tical utility of low-rank tensor structure, this study adopts a tensor-based satellite–reference merging framework, TMerge, which couples the IMERG Final Product and CPC precipitation through shared low-rank factors along spatial, temporal, and source modes. TMerge improves the IMERG Fi-nal Product over CONUS, a domain characterized by diverse hydroclimatic regimes, complex terrain, and heterogeneous precipitation processes. This work also evaluates TMerge against widely used correction baselines, in-cluding linear bias correction, neural networks, and distribution-matching methods, across regions, seasons, precipitation-intensity regimes, and vary-ing reference-data availability. This work’s results demonstrate that low-rank tensor structure provides a compact multilinear representation of precipita-tion variability, enabling improved satellite–reference precipitation merging over extended spatiotemporal domains under sparse reference observations. 

2. Methods 

Low-rank analysis in hydrology has remained matrix based, with spa-tiotemporal data commonly reshaped into space–time matrices. This unfold-ing does not preserve the original multidimensional structure and therefore may limit the effectiveness of low-rank representations. If tensor factor-ization proves effective in improving the fidelity with which the dominant variability of precipitation is captured in low-rank form it could further sup-port hydrologic applications that rely on low-rank structure. This would produce efficient data representation, reliable reconstruction, scalable mod-eling, and systematic monitoring. This work first examines the hypothesis that continental-scale precipitation fields exhibit a low-rank structure that 4is most effectively revealed when the data are represented and factorized as spatiotemporal tensors, rather than as independent spatial snapshots or as space–time matrices. The next step presents a tensor-based merging frame-work that exploits shared low-rank factors between satellite precipitation products and sparse reference observations, thereby providing more accu-rate, high-resolution precipitation estimates. 

2.1. Tensor and low-rank representation 

This work represents precipitation fields as tensors (multidimensional ar-rays) to preserve their spatial, temporal, and multi-source structure. A daily gridded precipitation field can be represented as a second-order tensor (ma-trix), whose two modes correspond to latitude and longitude. When daily gridded precipitation fields are stacked over time, the resulting dataset forms a third-order tensor, with modes corresponding to latitude, longitude, and time. When multiple precipitation products or data sources are represented jointly, an additional source mode can be introduced, yielding a fourth-order tensor. More generally, a tensor X ∈ RI1×I2×···× IN is a real-valued, multidi-mensional, array whose entries represent precipitation estimates or observa-tions indexed across N modes. This work adopts CP factorization to examine and leverage the low-rank structure of spatiotemporal precipitation data. A rank-R CP decomposition represents X as a sum of separable rank-one components (Kolda and Bader, 2009):

2.3. Satellite–reference precipitation merging 

To leverage the low-rank tensor structure of precipitation, this work in-troduces a tensor-based merging framework, referred to as TMerge, for im-proving satellite precipitation estimates using sparse reference observations. The rationale behind TMerge is that the dominant latent low-rank structure underlying the spatiotemporal variability of precipitation should be at least partly shared between satellite and reference products, even though the two sources differ in accuracy, coverage, and error structure. TMerge exploits this latent structure by factorizing a joint satellite–reference precipitation tensor into shared low-rank spatial and temporal factors. In this formulation, the satellite product provides high resolution spatial coverage of precipitation variability, while the sparse reference observations provide more reliable con-straints. By combining these complementary strengths, the joint low-rank representation produces a high-resolution precipitation product corrected by the reference observations. 

2.3.1. TMerge: Low-rank tensor merging 

TMerge extends the spatiotemporal low-rank formulation from a single precipitation tensor to a joint satellite–reference representation. 2.4. Evaluation metrics 

Model performance was evaluated across the experiments using comple-mentary metrics as relevant to each experiment, including the Pearson cor-relation coefficient ( r), which measures the linear association between esti-mated and observed precipitation (Legates and McCabe, 1999); the root-mean-square error (RMSE), which measures error magnitude while placing 9greater emphasis on large deviations (Chai and Draxler, 2014); the mean ab-solute error (MAE), which measures the average absolute difference between estimated and observed precipitation values (Willmott and Matsuura, 2005); the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE), which evaluates predictive performance relative to the mean of the observations (Nash and Sutcliffe, 1970); and the Kling–Gupta efficiency (KGE), which jointly accounts for correlation, bias, and variability (Gupta et al., 2009). 

3. Results 

This work organizes results according to two questions: whether daily precipitation fields admit a compact spatiotemporal low-rank representation, and whether that structure can be exploited to merge sparse gauge-based references with satellite estimates. To address the first question, STT is applied to CPC daily precipitation fields within CONUS and demonstrate that tensor factorization yields a more parsimonious low-rank representation than matrix-based formulations. To address the second question, TMerge is applied to merge sparse CPC references with the IMERG Final daily product within CONUS. Across all experiments, fixed-location sampling is applied such that the same subset of spatial locations is used for model fitting across all time steps, while the remaining locations are reserved for evaluation. This choice reflects the fixed spatial coverage of ground-based reference networks such as rain-gauge stations.

Satellite precipitation errors are known to be spatially heterogeneous. In our experiment, this heterogeneity manifested as elevated IMERG RMSE, particularly at three primary marine coastlines of the continental United States: the West Coast, the Gulf Coast, and the East Coast. These coastal zones span diverse precipitation regimes, including orographic precipitation along the mountainous West Coast, trop-ical and convective systems along the Gulf Coast, and complex coastal and frontal processes along the East Coast (Derin et al., 2022). Precipitation estimation in these coastal zones is challenging for satellite retrievals such as IMERG because the underlying precipitation processes in these regions are governed by terrain forcing, land–ocean gradients, and mesoscale storm dy-namics that are not fully resolved by remotely sensed cloud and ice signatures. TMerge consistently improved precipitation estimation across CONUS, with the strongest error reductions in coastal regions where IMERG errors were highest. This spatial correspondence suggests that regional-scale low-rank spatiotemporal structure provides a basis for mitigating systematic compo-nents of IMERG error, and TMerge exploits this structure by propagating sparse CPC reference information through a joint tensor factorization of CPC and IMERG, thereby substantially reducing IMERG error. The improvement was consistent across both time and precipitation regimes. As shown in Figures 4a and 4b across the 20 three-month periods from 2018 to 2022, TMerge achieved the highest correlations and lowest RMSE in ev-ery period, outperforming MLP, QM, Linear correction, and the IMERG Final product. Compared to IMERG, TMerge improved RMSE in all peri-ods, with improvements ranging from 1.798 to 3.520 mm/day and a mean improvement of 2.595 mm/day. These gains were substantially larger than those of the baselines: the smallest RMSE improvement achieved by TMerge (1.798 mm/day) exceeded the largest improvements obtained by MLP (1.480 mm/day), QM (0.740 mm/day), and Linear correction (0.404 mm/day). As shown in Figure 4c, TMerge achieved lower RMSE than IMERG in every precipitation bin, outperforming the MLP, QM, and Linear correction baselines in all regimes. TMerge RMSE improvement relative to IMERG ranged from 27% in the >50 mm/day bin to 54% in the 1– 5 mm/day bin, with improvements of about 39–54% across the 0–20 mm/day bins and a still substantial 36% improvement in the 20–50 mm/day bin. This consistency is important because higher precipitation intensities are both less frequent and associated with larger IMERG errors, making these conditions progressively more difficult to correct. The robustness of TMerge was most evident in the > 50 mm/day bin. The > 50 mm/day bin represents a rare high-precipitation regime, accounting for only 0.28% of all samples. In this regime, QM reduced RMSE by only about 4%, while MLP and Linear cor-rection degraded IMERG performance; in contrast, TMerge reduced RMSE by 27%. 4. Discussion 

By preserving the native spatial and temporal organization of daily pre-cipitation, tensor factorization captures its dominant variability with greater accuracy and parsimony than matrix-based factorization. This property is 28 important for hydrology because it enables high-dimensional precipitation fields to be represented compactly without disrupting their underlying spa-tiotemporal structure. This has important implications for a broad range of hydrologic and Earth system applications in which low-rank structure sup-ports efficient data representation, reliable reconstruction, scalable modeling, and systematic monitoring. TMerge exemplifies one such application allow-ing IMERG and CPC to share low-rank spatial and temporal factors while retaining source-specific information. TMerge’s superior performance over simpler linear and distribution match-ing methods, as well as the more complex nonlinear MLP, shows that the multilinear structure imposed by tensor factorization can capture rich spa-tiotemporal relationships with a compact parameterization. This compact-ness reduces reliance on the large reference datasets typically required by data-intensive neural networks with millions of parameters and can improve generalization to unseen locations. The contrast between the two resolution settings further highlights the parameter efficiency of TMerge relative to the MLP. The MLP performed substantially better applying the native IMERG resolution than using the coarser CPC resolution, where fewer training sam-ples constrained the useful network capacity. TMerge, however, remained effective with a compact parameterization in both settings, indicating that the low-rank formulation is less affected by spatial resolution as long as the dominant spatiotemporal structure can be captured. These characteristics make TMerge particularly well suited for improving satellite precipitation estimates in regions with limited reference-observation coverage. The stronger performance obtained with CONUS-wide spatial coverage and three-month periods suggests that low-rank modeling is more effective when precipitation is represented as a single tensor over an extended spa-tiotemporal domain. Although IMERG provides products at subdaily res-olutions, this work used the daily IMERG precipitation product because it matches the temporal resolution of the CPC gauge-based reference, is directly relevant to many hydrologic applications, and reduces the computational demands of multi-year, CONUS-wide experiments conducted in this study. Nevertheless, this work’s findings motivate extending the analysis to subdaily precipitation, where similar low-rank structure may also be present. Future studies can also extend TMerge to incorporate auxiliary variables, such as topography, as additional tensor modes. 29 5. Conclusion 

This study demonstrates that daily precipitation contains an exploitable low-rank structure that is represented more accurately and parsimoniously when its native spatial and temporal dimensions are preserved in tensor form. Building on this property, TMerge integrates dense satellite estimates with sparse reference observations through shared low-rank spatial and temporal factors, substantially improving IMERG precipitation estimates while retain-ing their native spatial resolution. Across CONUS, TMerge consistently out-performed linear correction, quantile mapping, and a nonlinear MLP across seasons, precipitation-intensity regimes, and levels of reference availability, with particularly strong improvements in coastal regions where satellite er-rors were greatest. These findings establish low-rank tensor structure as an organizing property of precipitation variability with potential benefits for several hydrologic and Earth system applications, and demonstrate its effec-tiveness in improving high-resolution precipitation estimates over extended regions with limited reference-observation coverage.
