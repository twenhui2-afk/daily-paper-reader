# ABSTRACT 

Contact angles are central descriptors of wetting and capillary behavior at solid–fluid interfaces. Dynamic contact angles in molecular simulations are commonly extracted from two-dimensional liquid–vapor contours and near-wall tangent fits. We introduce PANDA-D, an extension of Predicting Angle from Nanoscale Density Analysis (PANDA) that determines both contact angles of a driven liquid roll without extracting an interface. The density profile measures occupied layer width but does not identify which meniscus is advancing. The method resolves this ambiguity by jointly fitting density and the layerwise center-of-mass displacement relative to the roll. A circular-meniscus model maps these profiles to receding and advancing angles and an effective near-wall offset. On synthetic rolls with prescribed angles, the reconstruction error is 0.29 ◦ root-mean-square. We then analyze force-driven Lennard–Jones rolls between crystalline walls. Joint fits reproduce both measured profiles, whereas density-only fits can match density while assigning incorrect asymmetry. The resulting angles agree with independently determined circle-fit angles to 0.84 ◦ root-mean-square. Across four wall–fluid attractions, the receding angle decreases and the advancing angle increases with force, approximately linearly over the studied ranges. By eliminating contour thresholds and near-wall tangent extrapolation from the primary estimate, the two-profile approach enables reproducible, automated angle analysis across molecular trajectories.

# 1 Introduction 

Wetting governs how a liquid occupies a surface or pore, and the contact angle is its most compact geometric descriptor. The equilibrium angle is tied to the interfacial free-energy balance, whereas a moving contact line generally exhibits distinct advancing and receding apparent angles [de Gennes, 1985, Bonn et al., 2009, Snoeijer and Andreotti, 2013]. Those angles enter discussions of spreading, forced displacement, and capillary transport, but their value depends on the length scale and protocol at which a curved interface is observed. At the pore scale, even a small angular difference can alter the interpretation of multiphase invasion and wettability heterogeneity [Blunt et al., 2013, Gerke et al., 2021, Ipatova et al., 2026]. A reproducible molecular-scale measurement is consequently useful both as an observable in its own right and as an input to coarse-grained models. Dynamic wetting is difficult partly because a moving three-phase contact line couples processes across scales. The no-slip continuum construction has a local stress singularity [Huh and Scriven, 1971]; slip, contact-line kinetics, and viscous bending offer different descriptions of its regularization and the resulting apparent angle [Blake and Haynes, 1969, Voinov, 1976, Cox, 1986, Thompson and Troian, 1997, Qian et al., 2006, Ren and E, 2007]. and hydrodynamic accounts need not identify the same local angle, and a measured angle should therefore be reported with its observation scale and wall convention [Blake and De Coninck, 2002, 2011, Keeler et al., 2022]. The present paper does not choose among these constitutive theories. Its narrower aim is to measure two angles consistently from particle trajectories before asking which dynamical law, if any, describes them. Molecular dynamics (MD) can resolve the fluid, solid, and their fluctuating interface simultaneously. Early contact-line simulations established the importance of molecular slip and the sensitivity of dynamic angles to solid–liquid interactions [Thompson and Robbins, 1989, Bertrand et al., 2009]. Later simulations examined microscopic versus apparent angles and detailed molecular motion near the contact line [Fernández-Toledano et al., 2020, 2021]. In a confined bridge the two menisci can respond differently to forcing, while the liquid layers next to the walls need not resemble a sharp continuum interface [Wang et al., 2019, L¯ acis et al., 2022, Camargo et al., 2024]. Temperature-control algorithms may also change nonequilibrium observables, so a comparison of angles from different thermostat protocols requires care [Basconi and Shirts, 2013, Ruiz-Franco et al., 2018, Heinz et al., 2026]. The usual MD angle estimator first constructs a time-averaged two-dimensional density map, chooses an interface contour, fits a circle or another curve, and extrapolates its tangent to a specified wall plane. Such an estimator is physically intelligible and remains an important independent check. Yet the result can vary with the density threshold, the points excluded near the layered wall, the fit interval, and the location of the wall plane. These choices are especially consequential for nanodroplets and nanobridges, for which a few particle diameters are a substantial fraction of the meniscus height [Peng et al., 2012, Becker et al., 2014, Ravipati et al., 2018, Polovinkin et al., 2024, Camargo et al., 2024]. Thus agreement between two contour protocols is not automatically a unique “true” angle. Large simulation campaigns add a practical difficulty: robust extraction must be repeated without manually repairing each imperfect interface. An alternative is to infer geometry from integrated one-dimensional observables. Our original Predicting Angle from Nanoscale Density Analysis (PANDA) recovered an equilibrium angle from the density profile of a liquid bridge in a slit, without selecting a two-dimensional liquid–vapor contour [Semenchuk et al., 2025a]. A subsequent automated implementation improved reproducibility across configurations [Semenchuk et al., 2025b]. Related work extended interface-shape inference to liquid–liquid systems in cylindrical pores [Vandyshev et al., 2026]; simulations combining nanoscale wetting and roughness further motivate automated, clearly defined observables [Guskov et al., 2025]. These equilibrium approaches do not directly solve the driven case: a horizontal density profile integrated across the roll tells us its layer width but not whether the rear or front meniscus produced a given part of that width. This is an identifiability problem rather than merely a noisy-fit problem. If the two menisci are exchanged, the one-dimensional density is unchanged. For small opposite angular shifts about equilibrium, their first-order effects on density cancel. A second observable must record the orientation of the occupied layer. We use its horizontal center of mass relative to the center of mass of the complete roll, retaining the favorable averaging of a profile while encoding left–right asymmetry. The pair of observables is linked to two circular apparent menisci by a geometric forward model; a common near-wall offset acknowledges that a sharp idealized boundary cannot coincide exactly with the molecular wall. The underlying concept is geometric, not a particular thermostat-dependent force balance. We introduce this two-profile extension as PANDA-D and test it in three stages. First, synthetic rolls with known angles probe recovery over several equilibrium angles, filling fractions, and splittings. Second, equilibrium and force-driven Lennard–Jones rolls test whether the model follows actual molecular profiles and how its angles compare with independently fitted circles. Third, angle–force plots summarize the measured response at several solid–fluid attractions, without assigning a fitted friction or surface tension. The tests distinguish algorithmic recovery from MD-specific model discrepancy. PANDA-D’s practical claim is an angle estimate that does not require contour extraction; close agreement with a circle fit is a consistency check, whereas proving universal superiority would require a separate matched benchmark with known truth.

# 3 Results and discussion 

3.1 PANDA-D profiles and geometric model 

The equilibrium reference for an ideal, homogeneous roll can be motivated by minimizing interfacial free energy at fixed liquid volume. The liquid–vapor area and the liquid- and vapor-covered wall areas all contribute; their relative surface energies determine the equilibrium contact angle, while the stationary free interface has approximately constant curvature [de Gennes, 1985, Bonn et al., 2009, Semenchuk et al., 2025a]. For an extended roll in a slit, circular arcs are therefore a transparent low-dimensional geometric approximation to the two menisci. The simplification to liquid–vapor area alone would require a special wetting limit and is not invoked for our partially wetting states. Nor do we assume that a driven steady state minimizes the equilibrium free energy: the model maps measured shape information to apparent angles, without deriving their force dependence. Before profile construction, each saved frame is recentered on the roll with the periodic streamwise coordinate unwrapped consistently. In the geometric analysis all coordinates and distances are normalized by the physical pore height H; the resulting coordinates are denoted directly by x, y, and z. 3.2 Synthetic validation and analysis pipeline 

To test recovery against known angles, fluid coordinates were sampled inside prescribed circular-roll geometries. The test combines θ0 = 110 ◦, 130 ◦, and 150 ◦; filling fractions ϕ = 0 .3, 0.5, and 0.7; and half-splittings ∆θ = 0 ◦, 2◦, 5◦,

10 ◦, and 20 ◦. Each synthetic geometry uses θrec = θ0 − ∆θ and θadv = θ0 + ∆ θ, yielding 45 geometries and 90 individual true angles. Synthetic positions use the same order of particle number as the MD roll. This test checks the inverse problem under its assumed geometry; it does not include molecular layering or a diffuse molecular interface. The analysis pipeline is fixed across synthetic and MD cases: periodically recenter every frame; accumulate ρk and qk

In the synthetic test, the prescribed geometry supplies the true reference and makes any inversion error directly observable. In MD, an independent two-dimensional circle analysis is reserved for validation, not fed into the PANDA-D objective. Conventional contour approaches extract the missing orientation directly from a two-dimensional boundary [Peng et al., 2012, Camargo et al., 2024]; PANDA-D instead recovers it from an integrated particle observable. For an independent geometric comparator, the time-averaged xz density map is recentered and an interface is extracted at half the liquid-core density. Contour points within a normalized distance of approximately 0.094 from either wall (three fluid-particle diameters) are excluded; separate circles with vertical centers fixed at z = 0 are fitted to the two remaining contours. Their tangents are evaluated at the same wall planes, z = ±1/2, as the PANDA-D angles. This comparator does not enter the one-dimensional fit, but it is sensitive to contour, exclusion, and wall-position choices [Peng et al., 2012, Ravipati et al., 2018, Camargo et al., 2024]. Agreement with it tests consistency; the circle fit is not ground truth. Across the refreshed campaign, the PANDA-D joint-fit estimates lie close to independent circle-fit angles over a broad range of advancing and receding states (Fig. 5b). For the 167 quality-accepted individual angles in this comparison, the root-mean-square PANDA-D–circle difference is 0.84 ◦. PANDA-D gains information by compressing three-dimensional particle coordinates into two complementary one-dimensional profiles. This makes it attractive for automated trajectory analysis, but the circular-meniscus and uniform-core assumptions remain explicit. A noncircular meniscus, strong wall layering, substantial vapor populations, varying local density, or a contact line too close to 180 ◦ can bias the geometric interpretation. Likewise, the apparent contact angle depends on the chosen physical wall plane even when the interface itself is not extracted. The fitted near-wall offset partly addresses this issue but cannot eliminate it. Block scatter omits model discrepancy, contour-systematic uncertainty, and uncertainty in the equilibrium reference. The method should therefore report profile residuals, valid-bin support, convergence information, and an independent geometric comparison when one is available. The selected cases demonstrate feasibility for a simple roll, not universal performance for arbitrary contact-line topologies. A next step is to transfer the analysis to GROMACS for chemically realistic fluids and substrates and validate it for more complex meniscus shapes. 

# 4 Conclusions 

PANDA-D turns the asymmetric geometry of a moving liquid roll into a two-profile inverse problem. The density profile alone cannot distinguish the advancing from the receding meniscus, whereas the layerwise center-of-mass profile supplies the missing orientation information. Joint analysis therefore yields both dynamic contact angles without selecting a liquid–vapor contour for the primary estimate. 9PANDA-D for dynamic contact angles A P REPRINT 

On synthetic configurations, PANDA-D recovered prescribed angles with a root-mean-square error of 0.29 ◦. For force-driven Lennard–Jones rolls, the joint model reproduced both measured profiles, while density-only fits could match density but miss the observed asymmetry. PANDA-D angles agreed with independent circle fits to 0.84 ◦

root-mean-square. Across four wall–fluid attractions, the receding angle decreased and the advancing angle increased with force, approximately linearly over the investigated ranges. These results establish PANDA-D as a practical, reproducible method for extracting dynamic contact angles from molecular trajectories with less dependence on interface detection. Future work will test more complex meniscus shapes and transfer the analysis to GROMACS simulations of chemically realistic fluids and substrates.
