# ABSTRACT 

Column generation folklore warns that initial columns hurt: a warm start may bias the dual and slow the root of branch-price-and-cut. We show the opposite once the columns are imperfect in the right way. Preloading the support of the optimal root LP, the best columns one could know in advance, fails to finish on half of our hardest instances; a pool of ordinary routes that need not belong to any optimal solution, held outside the master and priced at the current smoothed dual, speeds the root up. Because the columns need not be optimal, producing them becomes a learning task: a neural edge scorer guides a constructive generator that builds the pool in seconds. A second network predicts the initial center of Wentges smooth-ing from the instance file, and with it the direction of the smoothed pricing queries; the solver’s own updates run unchanged. With prefill and center in place, exact pricing dominates root pricing time, so we rebuild bucket-graph labeling around its dominance test for the GPU and certify on the CPU. Inside RouteOpt, the state-of-the-art exact solver for capacitated vehicle routing, the pipeline reaches the same certified root bound in 13.2 % of vanilla RouteOpt’s root pricing time over 95 instances with 300 to 1000 customers, prefill alone in 26.1 %, and 17.5 times faster in the best case. In an exact branch-price-and-cut solver for unre-lated parallel machine scheduling, prefill with smoothing and the learned center certifies all 25 benchmark instances, 12 times faster than the solver’s own column generation, which certifies 20 of them. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Branch-price-and-cut (BPC) is the leading exact approach for large-scale NP-hard optimization problems, such as vehicle routing, crew and machine scheduling, and cutting stock (Barnhart et al., 1998; L¨ ubbecke & Desrosiers, 2005; van den Akker et al., 1999; Costa et al., 2019). Within branch-and-bound, column generation (CG) solves the Dantzig–Wolfe relaxation by generating necessary variables on the fly through solving a pricing subproblem, and cutting planes are typically applied to strengthen the bound. Work on accelerating BPC has therefore concentrated on branching, pricing, and cutting (Fukasawa et al., 2006; Jepsen et al., 2008; Baldacci et al., 2011; Pecin et al., 2017a; Pes-soa et al., 2020; Sadykov et al., 2021), with arc elimination (Irnich et al., 2010), route enumeration (Baldacci et al., 2008) and dual stabilization (du Merle et al., 1999; Wentges, 1997; Pessoa et al., 2018) as complementary accelerators. All of these techniques apply across problems, but pricing is where the problem’s structure enters. Our main test bed is RouteOpt (You & Yang, 2026), an exact solver for the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) with a state-of-the-art integrated implementation of all of the above. With the CPU-side toolbox this mature, learning and GPU computing offer two further directions. Learning has been applied to branching (Khalil et al., 2016; Gasse et al., 2019; Bengio et al., 2021; You et al., 2026), to cutting plane generation and selection (Tang et al., 2020; Paulus et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Deza & Khalil, 2023), to column generation and selection (Morabit et al., 2021; Chi et al., 2022; Morabit et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025; Kouteck´ a et al., 2025; V´ aclav´ ık et al., 2018) and to dual stabilization (Kraul et al., 2023; Babaki et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024; Sarkar et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025; Wang & Khir, 2026). On the GPU side, first-order 1arXiv:2610.21001v1 [cs.LG] 14 Oct 2026 methods have moved the linear-programming solve onto the GPU (Lu & Yang, 2025), and GPU labeling exists for constrained shortest paths outside BPC (Lu et al., 2020), but the exact pricer of a state-of-the-art BPC solver has stayed on the CPU. It is worth noting that gains beyond a modest research baseline can shrink or reverse inside a state-of-the-art solver, so a method of practical value has to be demonstrated against an efficient implementation. Our target is root pricing : the column generation that solves the root relaxation before any cutting plane is added. Its wall-clock time is our outcome, and every configuration we compare that com-pletes ends at the same certified root bound, so root pricing is a clean test bed and delivers a certified lower bound of practical value on its own. Our method is a combination of three moves: a prefilled archive supplies candidate columns, a learned initial center shapes the pricing queries, and a GPU accelerates the exact pricing level. We started from a piece of folklore. Textbooks warn that a warm start, even from an excellent primal solution, can hinder column generation (L¨ ubbecke & Desrosiers, 2005; Vanderbeck, 2005): the initial columns pull the dual toward the solution they encode, and in the root of a modern BPC, heuristic routes are elementary and need not suffice to attain or certify the ng-route bound that pricing works over (Baldacci et al., 2011), while a large pool adds to the complexity of solving the restricted master problem (RMP). Our measurements confirmed the warning on deep instances: preloading a heuristic pool into the master slowed the root down there, and preloading the support of the optimal root LP, the best columns one could know in advance, was the worst choice of all (Section 3.1, Appendix D.1). What did help was the opposite of what the folklore protects against. A diverse pool of ordinary, non-optimal routes, held outside the master as a lazy archive and queried at the current smoothed dual, shortens root pricing by a large factor on every benchmark set and route-size class we test (Section 4.2). Learning for column generation has so far concentrated on the pricing loop itself, on selecting, ranking, or generating columns during the iterations. We take a different perspective: leave the pricer untouched and learn the prefilling, with a constructive generator guided by a neural edge scorer, whose pool need not be optimal. Prefilling alone is not the whole story. In an earlier development experiment, dumping a metaheuris-tic pool into the initial master slowed the root down, as the folklore predicts (Appendix D.1): the pricer spent many rounds searching for the direction of the dual and accumulated a bloated LP. The classical remedy is dual smoothing (du Merle et al., 1999; Wentges, 1997; Pessoa et al., 2018), and the learned variants predict the optimal dual and install it as the reference point (Kraul et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024). Where those methods learn the values of the dual, we learn its direction: the RMP has many optimal duals, so what matters is the direction from which the query approaches the optimum, not its distance to one optimal point. We therefore learn the initial center of the standard Wentges smoothing from the instance file, calibrate its scale, and let the solver’s own rule move the center and adapt the weight from there. On the hardest instances in particular, the learned center becomes decisive, especially in combination with prefill (Section 4.2, Appendix D.2). Prefill and center cut the run time of heuristic pricing sharply, so exact pricing, the level that certifies optimality, becomes the dominant share of root time. Bucket-graph labeling is the exact pricer of modern routing solvers (Sadykov et al., 2021), and its dominance test is the cost that structure exists to contain; parallel CPU labeling has been developed for it (Petersen & Spoorendonk, 2025), while GPU work on BPC has addressed the linear-programming solve (Lu & Yang, 2025). We rebuild exact bucket-graph labeling around the dominance test for the GPU, with a final CPU certificate (Sections 3.3 and 4.3). The construction applies to any bucket-graph-based pricer once the resource extension, the dominance rule, and the join are adapted to the problem. Our contributions are summarized as follows. • A general learn-to-prefill framework: a neural edge scorer and a constructive generator whose pool is served to root pricing as a lazy archive. This opens a new direction for learning in column generation, one that is both highly effective and broadly applicable (Section 3.1; Appendix D.1). • A learned dual direction under prefilled columns: the initial center of Wentges smoothing, pre-dicted by a neural network, together with a calibration that emphasizes the direction and relaxes its absolute magnitude to better steer the prefilled columns (Section 3.2, Appendix D.2). • A GPU backend for exact bucket-graph labeling with CPU certification, which applies to any BPC solver with bucket-graph pricing (Sections 3.3 and 4.3). 2• A general plug-in pipeline for problems solved by BPC, which delivers strong performance inside optimized exact BPC solvers: the state-of-the-art CVRP solver RouteOpt and an exact solver for unrelated parallel machine scheduling (Sections 4.2 to 4.4).

Heuristic and exact pricing. Pricing is a shortest-path problem with resource constraints, solved by bucket-graph labeling (Sadykov et al., 2021) at three levels, as in generic BPC solvers (Pessoa et al., 2020): two heuristic levels (LIGHT and HEAVY) drop part of the label state, are cheap, may miss columns and account for most of the iterations; the exact level keeps every state the dominance rule needs, is expensive, and alone certifies that no column prices out. Root pricing stops when an exact pricing call at the raw dual u that explores its whole label space, a complete call, returns no route with rc r (u) < −ϵ, where ϵ > 0 is the solver’s pricing tolerance; its wall clock is what we measure (Section 4.1). This pricer is highly efficient, a bar that learned column generators rarely clear, which is why our two learned components leave its algorithm untouched; the GPU component of Section 3.3 reimplements its exact level without changing what it returns.

Once the deployed endgame switch fires (Appendix B.2.2), α is set to 0 and adaptation stops. 3.3 GPU EXACT PRICING 

The first two components change where root pricing spends its time. Prefill and a learned center re-duce the work at the heuristic levels, and in our development runs this left exact pricing as the largest stage of the run and the next target for acceleration. The CPU profile of Appendix C.2 identifies the dominance test as the hotspot, and its per-level counters show that an exact call evaluates orders of magnitude more dominance predicates than a heuristic call. Appendix C.1 reviews bucket-graph pricing: labels are extended bucket by bucket, each arrival is checked against the current contents of its target bucket, and the resulting insertions and deletions create a sequential dependence between the arrivals to a bucket. Our GPU exact pricing removes this dependence in three steps. 1. Batched dominance. Labels enter a bucket in waves. Each wave passes three dominance stages: stored labels against candidates, candidates against earlier candidates in a canonical order, and the surviving candidates against the stored labels. The stages run in sequence and the comparisons within a stage in parallel; every deletion keeps a direct dominator that survives the wave, with conservative floating-point guards and a repair pass that restores any candidate left without a surviving direct dominator. 2. Labeling redesigned around the test. RouteOpt’s push of one label at a time into the next bucket becomes a level-synchronous, wave-based expansion in which the arrivals to a bucket are batched instead of chained, the form parallel CPU labeling also takes (Petersen & Spoorendonk, 2025): resource levels are processed in order, waves repeat within a level until its frontier is empty, and all label state is in structure-of-arrays form. Label extension, dominance testing, and the join of half-routes run on the GPU; route reconstruction, deduplication, and the final column selection 6run on the CPU, the heuristic levels stay on the CPU, and dispatch sits inside the exact level, so the CG loop is unchanged. 3. Memory and filtering. Shared-memory tiles reuse candidate prefixes across comparisons, and the cross-bucket filter runs one warp per frontier label and stops at a reduced-cost prefix bound. Bucket-graph labeling is the exact pricer of state-of-the-art vehicle-routing solvers, among them VRPSolver and RouteOpt (Sadykov et al., 2021; Pessoa et al., 2020; You & Yang, 2026). They share the label-comparison structure the three steps act on, whose dominance test is the cost the bucket graph exists to contain (Sadykov et al., 2021), so the steps carry over once the resource extension, the dominance rule, and the join are adapted. Appendices C.3 and C.4 give the implementation details and the safeguards behind the optimality guarantee. 

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We organize the empirical evaluation around three questions: (i) How the pipeline works. Which component carries the gain, how the components interact, and where the prefilled columns go during column generation. (ii) What GPU pricing can add. How much a batched exact pricer buys per call, on which GPU, and how that converts into root time once the cost has shifted to exact pricing. (iii) How general the recipe is. Whether prefill and the learned center carry over to a different problem inside a different solver, as a case study on parallel-machine scheduling. Training, model selection and the constants of the pipeline are in Appendix B, the scheduling vari-ants in Appendix A.5; the ablations that isolate the two learned components are in Appendix D. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

All experiments ran on one workstation with an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU and an AMD EPYC 9555 processor; only the per-call device comparison of Table 2 and Appendix C.5 adds an L4 and a B200. For CVRP the baseline is RouteOpt (You & Yang, 2026), the state-of-the-art exact solver, whose reach marks the frontier of instances solved to optimality; for scheduling it is the BPC algorithm of Hijazi et al. (2026), which accelerates pricing with a pointer transformer. On CVRP we focus on the region exact solvers have not yet conquered, instances with 300 customers and more. Route size and instance size are the two factors that decide difficulty, so the panel is stratified by both: on Vrp-Set-X (Uchoa et al., 2017) we group instances by route size with the XML classification (Table 13) and sample five instances with 300 to 1000 customers from each of the short, medium, long and very-long classes; on XML (Queiroga et al., 2022) we sample five instances per class from short to ultra-long at 300 , 400 and 500 customers; 95 instances in all. For scheduling we use the standard benchmark distribution of Hijazi et al. (2026). The measured quantity is the wall clock of root pricing; every completed arm reaches the same certified root bound, each configuration is normalized to the mean of two runs of RouteOpt ( vanilla ) on the same instance, and ratios are aggregated as instance-equal geometric means. 4.2 THE PIPELINE TAKEN APART

Prefill is the central idea and the largest lever. On its own it brings root pricing to 26.1 % of vanilla’s, faster on all instances, and does so on every benchmark set and every route-size class of the panel. In instrumented runs the archive supplied columns in 24 to 52 % of the LIGHT rounds, bypassing labeling in those rounds, so it acts as a fast alternative to the LIGHT level; when it supplies no admissible column, the solver resumes labeling (Appendix B.1.2). The scheduling oracle is a dense dynamic program, so this case study evaluates prefill and dual smoothing without porting the routing GPU backend. We run inside the BPC solver of Hijazi et al. Table 3 reports geometric-mean wall time with the number of instances certified within the time limit. Prefill alone is already strong on this problem: it certifies the same instances as the baseline column generation in a small fraction of the time. Wentges smoothing helps on its own, smoothing with prefill certifies all but one instance, and adding the learned center certifies all 25 instances, where the learned pricer certifies 20; the whole configuration is 12 times faster in geometric mean than the baseline column generation. Changing how column generation is started and steered is therefore a lever of great power on this problem, one that acts on the initialization and the duals rather than on the pricer. 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

This paper breaks with the folklore that initial columns harm column generation and turns prefilling into a general plug-in accelerator for BPC: learning to prefill the columns. The idea at its center is that imperfect columns, routes that need not belong to any optimal solution but together cover the 9customers well, are what speeds the root up, provided they are held outside the master as a lazy archive and served at the current smoothed dual. We built a learn-to-prefill pipeline on this idea and obtained large speed-ups on two problems inside two exact solvers, CVRP in RouteOpt and parallel-machine scheduling in the code of Hijazi et al. (2026). On the hardest instances prefill also showed its weakness: the dual it produces is close to the optimal dual in value but deviates from it in direction, so the query needs a correction of direction rather than of distance, and we designed a learned dual center for that purpose. Once archive and center are in place, the remaining bottleneck of the pipeline is exact pricing, and we rebuilt it around its dominance test for the GPU. On the state-of-the-art CVRP solver the complete pipeline reaches the certified root bound with a 7.6-fold geometric-mean speed-up of root pricing over vanilla RouteOpt, and in the best case in 5.7 % of vanilla RouteOpt’s time. The main limitation is that the effect on the full BPC tree is unknown: at the scale we test, the instances have no known exact solution time, and root pricing is the part we can measure and cer-tify; extending the measurement to the tree is one direction of future work. A second direction is the co-design of the two learned components, which we designed independently, either as a joint configuration or as a single model that predicts pool and center together.

Heuristic and exact pricing. Pricing is solved by dynamic programming over labels, and labels are discarded under a dominance relation ⪯ (Appendix A.4). The exact level uses a relation ⪯E

under which every completion of a discarded label is matched by a completion of its dominator with no larger reduced cost, so that it returns a minimizer of (A.5); the heuristic levels use coarser relations ⪯E⊆⪯ H⊆⪯ L that compare fewer label components, discard more labels and may therefore miss the minimizer. Root pricing , the quantity measured in this paper, is the first column generation from the initial columns to the exact certificate, before any cut is separated.

A.3 TWO TYPICAL TASKS : ROUTING AND SCHEDULING 

In both families a column is the plan of one resource, a vehicle’s route or a machine’s schedule, and the master covers a set of demands, customers or jobs. The pricing problems differ in kind: routing prices by a resource-constrained shortest path over a graph, relaxed from elementarity by ng-routes and solved by bucket-graph labeling with a dominance relation (Baldacci et al., 2011; Sadykov et al., 2021; Pessoa et al., 2020); the scheduling formulation used here prices by a subset-selection dynamic program over jobs whose state is the accumulated processing time (van den Akker et al., 1999; Yu et al., 2026). The learned components of this paper touch neither pricing problem; they generate column pools and initialize stabilization centers. # C GPU EXACT PRICING IN DETAIL 

C.1 BUCKET -GRAPH LABELING AND THE DOMINANCE TEST 

RouteOpt prices by bucket-graph labeling (Sadykov et al., 2021). A label is a partial route leaving the depot together with its reduced cost, its resource consumption, its ng memory and, in the tree, its cut states. The labels at a customer are kept in buckets indexed by a resource interval, and the algorithm processes buckets in resource order, extending each label along the bucket arcs and the jump arcs into the buckets of its successors. In RouteOpt’s own implementation this is a push : an extended label arrives at its target bucket and is checked there against the bucket’s move-to-front list of stored labels, with early exit once a dominator is found; before a stored label is extended, a cross-bucket check compares it with the earlier buckets of the same customer through reduced-cost prefix bounds; buckets are sorted by reduced cost when their level is done. The predicate compares resources, tests ng-set inclusion and, with cuts, checks a reduced-cost gap corrected by the cut duals; the LIGHT and HEAVY levels use weaker predicates, which is why they keep few labels and run quickly. Because every arrival is checked against the bucket as it stands at that moment and changes it, the arrivals to a bucket form a chain; this is the sequential dependence the backend removes. Under symmetry RouteOpt labels forward to the meet point and joins two forward half-routes. C.2 WHERE THE TIME GOES

C.3 REMOVING THE SEQUENTIAL DEPENDENCE 

The backend keeps the contract of the CPU pricer and changes the shape of the computation. At each resource level it expands the current frontier and groups the candidates by target bucket; same-level candidates form the next wave of that level, and candidates for later levels wait for theirs. The 23 candidates of one bucket in a wave form a segment, tested in three stages. Stored labels against can-didates: each candidate independently, stopping at its first dominator, whose stored label is marked as a witness. Candidates against candidates in the canonical order (reduced cost, resources, ng car-dinality, generation index): the serial reference accepts candidates in that order, and the exact GPU mode compares each candidate in parallel against all earlier candidates that survived the first stage. Surviving candidates against the stored labels, with marked witnesses exempt from deletion. A re-pair pass then restores any deleted candidate without a surviving direct dominator. Every deleted label therefore has a dominator alive at the end of the wave, an invariant maintained by retaining the stored labels that serve as deletion witnesses and stronger than what RouteOpt’s own implemen-tation offers under tolerance. Label state is held in structure-of-arrays form; shared-memory tiles reuse candidate prefixes across the comparisons of a block; an optional warp-per-segment path for small segments exists and is disabled in the deployed configuration; the cross-bucket filter runs one warp per frontier label and uses the same reduced-cost prefix bound as the CPU. Label extension, dominance testing and the forward–forward join run on the GPU; route reconstruction, deduplica-tion by canonical route identity and the final selection of returned columns run on the CPU, and with stable numbering, canonical sorting and fixed scan rules the output does not depend on scheduling. The heuristic levels, the master and the cut loop stay on the CPU, and dispatch sits inside the exact level, so the column-generation loop is unchanged. C.4 CERTIFICATION AND SAFEGUARDS 

The backend runs only where its preconditions hold, namely symmetric instances and an ng bit-set width within the compiled bound, with automatic CPU fallback otherwise. Every returned column is re-priced on the CPU at the query qt actually used, since the solver prices at qt and admits at 

ut, and mismatches are counted rather than silently accepted. An empty backend answer is not accepted as a certificate: before column generation terminates, one CPU EXACT call re-proves the same dual, so the root bound of a GPU run is certified by the CPU pricer. The backend’s output policy is aligned with the CPU truncation rule, since a different rule changes how many columns a call returns and hence how many calls the loop makes. Correctness is checked by comparing final root bounds across arms and, in a diagnostic configuration, the returned column sets call by call, and the completed arms of every comparison in this paper must report the same root bound. D.1 SUPPLYING THE COLUMNS : WHICH POOL , AND HOW 

The folklore and its best evidence. Practitioners of column generation know the advice that pre-filling the master with heuristic columns “does not help”: the LP starts wide, the first solve is slow, and the columns are thrown away again. The two oracle arms of Table 11 show where this experience comes from, and why it is only half right. P SUPP preloads exactly the support of the converged root LP—the routes with positive value in an optimal root solution, the best columns anyone could know in advance—and it is the worst arm of the table: 5 of the 10 runs time out, and the five that finish are 0.732 × vanilla but still 2.478 × slower than the deployed prefill on those instances (P SUPP /P N,[1.620, 3.790]). A few perfect columns give the master a degenerate vertex to stand on and nothing to move with. PCONV , which preloads the complete column set of the converged master, is the best arm at 0.183 ×: what a pool has to supply is not the optimum but a rich neighborhood of near-optimal routes. This is the sense in which imperfect is perfect. Neither oracle is a method—both need a completed solve—but they mark two contrasting extremes of what a pool can supply, and on the deep XML instances of a preliminary experiment preloading a heuristic pool (1.209 ×, n = 16 )or the converged basis (1.223 ×) confirmed the folklore rather than beating it (Section 1). 

Learning is necessary. PN is the deployed prefill: the learned pool (Appendix B) admitted lazily, the mechanism of the next paragraph. P H keeps that mechanism and replaces the learned pool by an ordinary heuristic pool, one HGS run with one seed. On the same instances and the same node the learned pool is 1.233 × faster (P H/P N, 90 % interval [1.121, 1.356]), and is the faster of the two on 9 of the 10 instances. The scorer lets the constructor propose an order of magnitude more feasible routes than one heuristic run (326470 against a few thousand on X-n1001-k43), and the archive is selective enough to take only the ones that matter. 

The steps we take are necessary. The deployed archive keeps the pool outside the model: the master starts from the singleton initial columns; after every LP re-solve the pool is priced under the current smoothed dual, the 300 most negative routes are handed to the same admission check the pricers use, the labeling call of that iteration is skipped when at least one route is admitted, and the LP is re-optimized from the previous basis; a route is offered at most once. One scan of the 326470-25 route pool of X-n1001-k43 costs 18 ms; the pool served the first 29 iterations exclusively and 2.7% of it ever entered the model. P DUMP drops this discipline and places the whole learned pool in the initial master. On these instances no difference is detected between the lazy archive and the dump (P DUMP /P N 1.044 ×, interval [0.960, 1.135]). The gap is modest on these instances because the solver’s column cleaner caps the live master at ten thousand columns and repairs a wide start within a few iterations; it is the deep XML instances, where the first barrier solve lands on a bad vertex, on which preloading costs 1.209 ×. The archive costs nothing when preloading would have been harmless and saves the run when it would not, which is why it is the deployed choice. D.2 CHOOSING THE CENTER : THE LEARNED DIRECTION 

The center model predicts the direction of the converged root dual; the vector is then given a mag-nitude and projected onto the structural box B = {c : 0 ≤ ci ≤ max(2 d0i − cveh , 0) } of (8) (Appendix B), and installed as the iteration-0 center of Wentges smoothing. Table 12 tests this on the same instances with the center alone, no pool, as in the Center column of Table 1. 

Learning is necessary. S is the standard Wentges rule: no learned center, the solver seeds its own center at the end of the first heuristic phase. The learned center C N, the deployed model of Appendix B.2.1, is 1.369 × faster (S/C N, interval [1.257, 1.492]), consistent with the panel-wide Center column of Table 1; standard smoothing alone recovers only 0.837 × of vanilla here. 

The steps we take are necessary. CRAND keeps the deployed magnitude, vehicle row and box projection but randomly permutes the customer entries of the learned center—the direction is de-stroyed and nothing else. It costs 1.176 × (C RAND /C N, [1.094, 1.264]): the direction is what the smoothed pricing queries inherit, and it is what the network learns. CRAND still beats standard smoothing (0.719 × against 0.837 × of vanilla), so a calibrated center installed at iteration 0 is worth part of the gain by itself, and the learned direction adds the rest on top.
