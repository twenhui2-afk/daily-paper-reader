GAPS BETWEEN ROOTS IN BALLISTIC DEPOSITION ON A STRIP: LINEAR VARIANCE, GAUSSIAN LIMITS AND A POISSON REGIME 

MATEO MATIJASEVICK 

Abstract. Mansour, Rastegar and Roitershtein studied the roots of ballistic deposition on a cyclic strip of K sites, namely the sites that receive a particle at height one, and the number 

Di,K of pairs of consecutive roots at distance i + 1. They computed the first two moments of 

Di,K for i ≤ 7. They conjectured that for every i the mean and the variance are exactly linear in K, that Di,K satisfies a central limit theorem, and that the standardized vector ( Di,K )i≥1

converges to a Gaussian process. We prove these conjectures. The key observation is that the roots are exactly the valleys of a uniformly random circular arrangement, so that Di,K 

counts occurrences of a family of consecutive patterns. We determine sizes from which the moments are exactly linear, which cannot be lowered in any case we checked, and give closed forms for the mean and variance constants and for the limiting covariance. We also prove a Wasserstein bound of order ( EDi,K )−1/2 that is uniform in i, and a local limit theorem. Finally we describe the limit completely when i grows with K: it is degenerate, Poisson or Gaussian according as EDi,K tends to zero, to a positive constant, or to infinity.

The proofs rest on a simple observation (Lemma 2.1). A site becomes a root exactly when it is visited before both of its neighbours. If each site carries an independent rate-one Poisson clock, the order of first visits is a uniformly random circular arrangement, and the roots are its valleys , the strict local minima. After this reduction the growth process plays no further role. The statistic R[K] becomes the number of valleys of a random circular permutation, so the results of [11] on R[K] are the circular form of classical facts on peaks of random permutations (see, e.g., [16]). Similarly, Di,K counts occurrences of the consecutive patterns of length i + 4 whose up/down word is D U aDb U with 

a, b ≥ 1 and a + b = i + 1. Here the up/down word of a sequence of distinct numbers records U for each ascent and D for each descent between consecutive entries. Thus a gap of length i + 1 is an ascending run of length a followed by a descending run of length b. The joint behaviour of runs up and down is a classical subject [10]. Central limit theorems for such local statistics GAPS BETWEEN ROOTS IN BALLISTIC DEPOSITION 3

follow from the theory of m-dependent sequences [8]; see [6] for consecutive patterns and [7] for Berry–Esseen bounds for pattern counts. The formula for ai is equivalent to the enumeration of permutations with peak set {2, n − 1} due to Billey, Burdzy and Sagan [3] (Remark 4.4). We have not found this reduction in [11, 12], nor in the works citing [11]. We could not find the closed forms for bi and Σ below in the literature on runs and patterns either. What the reduction does not give automatically is the following, and it constitutes the substance of this paper: 

• the non-degeneracy bi > 0 for every i;

• the exact covariance structure, with size thresholds that cannot be lowered in any case we checked; 

• closed forms for bi and Σ; 

• normal approximation bounds that are uniform in i;

• the Poisson regime. 1.4. Organization. Section 2 proves the reduction. Section 3 computes the moments exactly, and Section 4 evaluates the constants. Lemma 2.1 (root criterion) . Let K ≥ 3 and let x1, . . . , x n be any finite sequence of sites, deposited in this order on the flat profile according to (1) . For a site y let ι(y) be the index of its first occurrence, with ι(y) = ∞ if y does not occur. Then x receives a particle at height one if and only if ι(x) < ι (x − 1) and ι(x) < ι (x + 1) .Proof. Heights never decrease, and a deposit raises the height of its site to at least one. Hence at every step H(y) = 0 exactly for the sites y not yet visited. A particle deposited at x lands at height one if and only if H(x − 1) = H(x) = H(x + 1) = 0, that is, if and only if none of x − 1, x, x + 1 has been visited before. This can only happen at the first visit of x, and it happens then if and only if neither neighbour was visited earlier. □
