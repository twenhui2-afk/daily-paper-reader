# A Computational Framework for the Acoustic Characterization of Suikinkutsu (Water Harp Cave) 

Haimes, Paul Ritsumeikan University haimes@fc.ritsumei.ac.jp Uhde, Alarith Ritsumeikan University uhde@fc.ritsumei.ac.jp Marutschke, Daniel Moritz Ritsumeikan University moritz@fc.ritsumei.ac.jp 

Abstract 

Suikinkutsu are partially concealed acoustic devices traditionally installed adjacent to stone water basins ( tsukubai ) in Japanese temple gardens. While previous research has examined their physical acoustics and cultural significance, com-putational methods for documenting and comparing their acoustic characteristics remain underexplored. This exploratory study introduces a computational framework for analyzing suikinkutsu using spectral and temporal audio features extracted from field recordings. Rather than estimating the physical geometry of individual suikinkutsu , the framework characterizes their acoustic profiles using a multidimensional set of computational descriptors, enabling systematic comparisons between recordings across different sites and installations. Audio recordings from nine suikinkutsu located at temples, shrines, and gardens in the Kansai region of Japan were analyzed using a feature-extraction pipeline that incorporates Fast Fourier Trans-form (FFT), Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), Root Mean Square (RMS) energy, spectral centroid, spectral bandwidth, and spectral contrast. The extracted features indicate that, although the suikinkutsu share broadly similar spec-tral and timbral characteristics, each exhibits a measurably distinct acoustic profile; a supplementary analysis of within-site and between-site variability suggests these profiles reflect a bounded range of characteristic resonant behaviour rather than a fixed acoustic signature. The proposed framework provides one of the first computational approaches for systematically characterizing and comparing suikinkutsu , contributing a practical foundation for future research and the digital preservation of this distinctive form of Japanese environmental design. 

Keywords: suikinkutsu, acoustic characterization, digital heritage

# 1 Introduction 

This paper presents a computational framework for analyzing 

suikinkutsu – a term commonly translated as “water harp cave” – a partially hidden feature found adjacent to stone water basins (tsukubai ) in several temple gardens throughout Japan. The device is, more rarely, also referred to as mizugoto , meaning “water harp”. As described in previous research, a suikinkutsu 

consists of “a buried ceramic or metal pot with a small opening at the top... Water drips into the pot, producing soft, resonant echoes, creating a soundscape that blurs the lines between the 

biophonic (biological sounds) and anthrophonic (human-made sounds)” [1]. A piece of bamboo often protrudes from these devices, inviting visitors to listen carefully (Figures 1 and 2). While it is estimated that Japan has around 77,000 temples [2], the national suikinkutsu database website ( Zenkoku Suikinkutsu D¯ etab¯ esu )1, though seemingly no longer updated, lists just 487 

suikinkutsu sites throughout the country.

Despite this history, relatively little research has examined 

suikinkutsu in detail. By the time of Hirayama’s 1958 survey, the device had fallen into such decline that it stood “on the verge of near-total destruction, such that even its structure was about to be forgotten” [3]. It was mainly through the efforts of Hirayama – who “documented surviving examples and provided details of their structure” [1] – that they gained renewed attention during the latter half of the twentieth century. Nevertheless, they re-main an underinvestigated phenomenon within Japanese aesthet-ics, and, in the authors’ experience conducting fieldwork for this study, their scarcity relative to the number of temples in Japan may partly explain why relatively few Japanese people are even aware of their existence. Early acoustic investigations by Kishizuka employed frequency spectrum (FFT) analysis to compare original and reconstructed 

suikinkutsu , demonstrating that the characteristic sound is strongly influenced by the resonance of the vessel [4]. Similarly, Iwase compared the FFT spectra of suikinkutsu with other tra-ditional Japanese sound-producing devices – including the koto ,

shishi-odoshi (deer scarer), and uguisubari (nightingale floor) – and, through simulation, modelled the suikinkutsu ’s distinctive tone as arising from a broadband water-drop excitation governed by the resonant modes of the vessel’s internal cavity, also noting a comparatively long reverberation time of approximately 1.5–2 seconds in the dominant 1–5 kHz range [5]. ¯Oizumi et al. later provided a foundational account of the physical acoustics of the 

suikinkutsu [6]. Their work demonstrated that the characteristic sound arises from two related phenomena: 1. Bubble vibration at the moment of impact of a water drop, which determines the frequency. 2. Amplification by the natural resonance of the inverted ves-sel’s air cavity. Building upon these findings, Watanabe investigated the com-bined effects of bubble excitation, vessel vibration and cavity resonance through theoretical and experimental modelling, fur-ther clarifying the acoustic mechanisms responsible for the dis-tinctive sound of the suikinkutsu [7]. More recently, Fujita et al. used numerical simulation of the air––water interaction to model sound radiation from the water surface itself, providing support for the resonance mechanisms identified in the preceding studies [8]. Similarly, Oku et al. used a computational fluid dynamics simulation to reproduce the sound pressure waveform of a single bubble, offering further support for the bubble resonance mecha-nism described above [9]. Recent work has also examined the cultural and aesthetic sig-nificance of the suikinkutsu within Japanese gardens, exploring its role in environmental sound appreciation and drawing paral-lels between the subtle presence of the suikinkutsu and the subtle, sustained listening required to appreciate Japanese ambient mu-sic ( kanky¯ o ongaku ) [1]. Complementing this, Cerwén examined 

suikinkutsu as one of several sound-producing garden features within a broader soundscape design framework for Japanese gar-dens [10]. Cerwén’s study noted that most of the Japanese gar-dens that contained soundscape elements were located in urban areas. This reality meant that “noise from surrounding activi-ties may be disturbing, but escaping from such noise into a quiet garden can make that garden seem all the more tranquil” [10]. 

Suikinkutsu , along with other phenomena found in Japanese gar-dens, align with a broader tendency in Japanese aesthetics toward understated, easily overlooked forms of beauty [11].

suikinkutsu , this research examines which acoustic features re-main relatively consistent across recorded audio signals and can thus be used to characterize specific examples. This study’s objective is specifically to characterize the 

suikinkutsu as a sound-producing structure – that is, to describe acoustic properties attributable to the vessel’s resonance and con-struction – rather than to document the broader in situ soundscape experience of the garden settings in which suikinkutsu are found, a topic addressed more directly by prior soundscape-oriented re-search [10]. Ambient environmental sound is nonetheless present in all field recordings. Because no objective method was available in the field to measure or separate ambient noise from vessel res-onance, this paper does not treat environmental sound as an ob-ject of study in its own right, or claim to isolate it with certainty; instead, likely instances of environmental noise are flagged and discussed through informed interpretation where evident in the recordings (Sections 3.2, 3.3, and 3.4), rather than through a rig-orously validated threshold (Section 4.1). Estimating the geometry of suikinkutsu is challenging because reliable information about their dimensions is rarely available. Temple records are often unavailable or inaccessible, and knowl-edgeable staff are difficult to locate. Additionally, suikinkutsu are relatively uncommon and are not present at all temples in Japan, which limits the number of sites available for study. As a result, this research is situated at the intersection of acoustic analysis 

and digital heritage , illustrating how computational audio anal-ysis can document and compare suikinkutsu when physical mea-surements are inaccessible. In this context, acoustic characteri-zation denotes the representation of individual suikinkutsu using a multidimensional set of computational audio descriptors that enable comparison between recordings. This framing situates the present study within a broader recogni-tion that the acoustic properties of a heritage site or object con-stitute a legitimate, and often overlooked, dimension of cultural heritage in their own right, distinct from – though complementary to – the physical structure that produces them [12]. Where dig-ital heritage practice has more commonly relied on visual or ar-chitectural documentation, such as photogrammetry or 3D scan-ning, computational audio descriptors of the kind employed here offer a comparable systematic approach for documenting sound-producing heritage objects, aligning with recent proposals to use structured sound classification as a practical tool for heritage management [13], and with recent experimental and numerical work characterizing the acoustics of individual heritage structures as a form of intangible cultural heritage in its own right [15]. This approach is particularly well suited to suikinkutsu : Japan has an existing precedent of formally recognizing everyday and environ-mental sounds as heritage worth conserving, exemplified by the Ministry of the Environment’s 100 Soundscapes of Japan initia-tive [14], which itself includes two named suikinkutsu – one in Gunma Prefecture and one in Gifu Prefecture – among its selec-tions, out of the several hundred known suikinkutsu sites nation-wide (Section 1). The computational framework proposed here offers one practical means of extending this kind of documenta-tion to the many suikinkutsu that have not individually received such recognition. 

# 2 Method 

2.1 Data Collection 

Field recordings were made using either a Teenage Engineering TP-7 2 portable recorder or an iPhone SE (2nd gen) across nine temple gardens, shrine gardens, and other gardens in the Kansai region of central Japan, all open to the public, summarized in Table 1. The device microphones were placed close to the mouth of the bamboo, with the exception of Eikand¯ o, Saij¯ uji, Taima-dera (1), and Taima-dera (2), which lacked bamboo poles, and Kyoto Botanical Gardens, where a bamboo pole was present but the microphone was instead positioned close to the exposed water entrance. At Saij¯ uji, water was poured manually, rather than via a steady, elevated drip as at the other sites (see Figure 2). At Miwa, the suikinkutsu was noticeably quiet at the source despite the presence of a dedicated bamboo pole, and, as a busy, fre-quently visited shrine, background noise was more difficult to avoid than at the other sites. At each site, three usable takes, each approximately 30 seconds in duration, were recorded within a single visit lasting approx-

At a few sites, additional takes were recorded because an in-progress take was interrupted (e.g. by a passing visitor) and subsequently discarded, so that three complete, uninterrupted takes could be retained per site. Microphone placement was kept as consistent as possible across takes at a given site; because sites were sometimes vis-ited by other members of the public concurrently, however, ex-act placement could not always be replicated between takes. As described in Section 2.2, each 30-second take was subsequently trimmed to a 10-second segment for analysis, yielding 30 sec-onds of analyzed audio per site (270 seconds in total across all twenty-seven takes). For the site-level comparisons reported in Section 3.2, the clearest of the three retained takes at each site – judged by the lowest level of extraneous ambient noise – was manually selected as that site’s representative recording. 

2.2 Audio Pre-Processing 

Each audio file was manually processed as follows in RX Audio Editor 3 software: • Each 30-second take was trimmed to a 10-second segment; the segment containing the least background noise was man-ually selected. • Normalized to target peak levels of 0.0 dBFS to ensure con-sistent volume across all audio files. • Exported as a 16-bit 44.1 kHz mono WAV file. • Files were checked to confirm they did not contain any clipping (distortion caused by signal levels exceeding the recording system’s maximum amplitude).

2.4 Within-Site and Between-Site Variability 

To assess whether the acoustic differences reported between sites (Section 3.2) reflect stable characteristics of individual 

suikinkutsu rather than variation attributable to recording con-ditions or the manual selection of a representative take (Sec-tion 2.1), pairwise cosine similarity was additionally computed across all three retained takes per site ( N = 27 recordings), rather than the nine manually-selected representative recordings used elsewhere in this paper. Each pairwise comparison was classi-fied as within-site (both takes from the same site; n = 27 pairs) or 

between-site (takes from different sites; n = 324 pairs). Because pairs sharing a recording are not independent of one another, sta-tistical significance was assessed using a permutation test: site labels were randomly reassigned across the 27 recordings 10,000 times, and the observed difference between mean within-site and mean between-site similarity was compared against the resulting null distribution. 3.3 Site-Specific Acoustic Divergences 

Although the recordings are broadly similar overall, several sites exhibit somewhat low MFCC similarity scores that merit closer examination. One such example is Saij¯ uji, which shares relatively low timbral similarity with Taiz¯ oin (0.890). Saij¯ uji differs from most other sites in two important respects: it lacks the bamboo pole used to channel sound toward the microphone, and water is introduced by manual pouring rather than a steady, elevated drip. These differences likely influence the timing, amplitude, and spectral envelope of the recorded water impacts, contributing to its dis-tinctive descriptor profile. The absence of a bamboo pole alone is unlikely to explain this divergence, however, as Eikand¯ o – also lacking bamboo – occupies a more central position within the descriptor space. These observations therefore suggest that the manually poured water source is likely a contributing factor. This interpretation is further supported by Saij¯ uji’s relatively elevated spectral activity in the 300–600 Hz range (Figure 7), consistent with the broader-band, less periodic impacts expected from man-ual pouring. In the absence of verified physical measurements, however, these explanations remain interpretive. The Miwa recording forms the least similar pair in the dataset with Taiz¯ oin (0.850), and is also among Miwa’s lower pairings with Eikand¯ o (0.907). Unlike the example discussed above, this recording was made using the standard bamboo-mouth config-uration, making recording geometry an unlikely explanation for its distinctiveness. Instead, the source itself was noticeably quiet, while the shrine’s high visitor traffic made background noise dif-ficult to avoid. Because all recordings were normalized to a com-mon peak level, the quieter source retained proportionally more ambient noise after normalization than recordings made under quieter conditions. This offers a plausible explanation for Miwa’s comparatively distinct acoustic profile more broadly, not only its divergence from Taiz¯ oin, although confirmation would require formal noise-floor analysis or verified physical measurements. The Kyoto Botanical Gardens recording also exhibits compara-tively low similarity with several other sites, including Taiz¯ oin (0.932). Unlike Saij¯ uji, the site includes a bamboo pole, but the recording was made adjacent to the water entrance rather than at the bamboo opening. Despite this microphone placement, it remains highly similar to conventionally recorded sites such as Enk¯ oji (0.979) and Unry¯ uin (0.980). It also shares a distinctly high similarity with Saij¯ uji (0.957), despite the latter lacking a bamboo pole and relying on manual water pouring, suggesting that recording position and water-delivery method do not neces-sarily drive large timbral divergence on their own. The recording also contains pronounced energy below approximately 100 Hz (Figure 7), corresponding to traffic noise from a nearby road that was directly observed during field recording and likely con-tributes to this site’s markedly low similarity with Taiz¯ oin, in-dependently of the suikinkutsu . Nevertheless, the low spectral centroid and bandwidth values for the Kyoto Botanical Gardens in Figure 5 suggest that at least some of this divergence from sev-eral sites is due to the characteristics of the suikinkutsu itself. Collectively, these observations suggest that the proposed de-scriptor set captures genuine acoustic differences between indi-vidual suikinkutsu while reflecting the actual recording condi-tions. Rather than representing a limitation alone, this sensitivity reflects the realities of documenting heritage soundscapes in situ ,where environmental factors inevitably contribute to the recorded acoustic signature.

Bearing this in mind, it is clear that suikinkutsu 

are generally active within a broadly consistent frequency range across sites, with the low-frequency activity observed at these four recordings being most plausibly attributable to environmen-tal factors rather than genuine divergence in resonant behaviour. These observations are consistent with previous studies identi-fying cavity resonance as the principal mechanism governing sound production. RMS energy measurements indicate variation in resonance persistence between sites, suggesting differences in damping behaviour (i.e., how quickly the resonance decays over time; see the RMS axis in Figure 4). Longer resonance decay times may reflect differences in cavity geometry, material prop-erties, or other acoustic factors, although the absence of physi-cal measurements prevents attribution to any single cause. It is worth noting that the boundary between genuine low-frequency resonance and ambient noise cannot be established with certainty. Therefore, the frequency ranges discussed above should be read as indicative rather than definitive. Taken together, these results suggest that suikinkutsu exhibit broadly similar acoustic charac-teristics across different sites regardless of differences in vessel dimensions, materials, and construction, while still exhibiting measurable, recording-level differences that the proposed frame-work is able to capture. Whether these differences correspond to stable, reproducible properties of each suikinkutsu or are partly attributable to recording conditions is considered further in Sec-tion 2.4 and Section 4. 

# 4 Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work 

This exploratory study introduced a computational framework for the acoustic characterization of suikinkutsu through audio fea-ture extraction. Building on previous research into the physical acoustics of suikinkutsu , a set of descriptors was extracted from recordings collected at nine sites in the Kansai region of Japan. Rather than possessing a fixed or clearly delineated acoustic sig-nature – an expectation difficult to sustain given that all record-ings were captured in situ under variable environmental condi-tions – individual suikinkutsu appear to exhibit a bounded range of characteristic resonant behaviour, arising from fixed physical constraints such as vessel geometry, cavity volume, and construc-tion materials, even as moment-to-moment excitation (e.g., drip timing, manual pouring, ambient noise) remains inherently vari-able (Section 3.2). The proposed framework provides a practical method for documenting and comparing these culturally signifi-cant sound-producing structures, supporting their preservation as part of Japan’s intangible sonic heritage. 

4.1 Limitations 

Consistent with the exploratory framing adopted in this study (Section 1), the primary limitation is the limited number of recordings and the lack of verified physical measurements for the individual suikinkutsu . As a result, the research emphasizes acoustic characterization rather than precise geometric estima-tion. Furthermore, as all recordings were captured on-site rather than under controlled acoustic conditions, several were subject to uncontrollable environmental variables – including ambient traf-fic, conversation, and wind – which likely contributed to some of the low-frequency divergence observed between sites (Sec-tion 3.2). Peak normalization (Section 2.2), while necessary to ensure a consistent overall signal level across recordings for the purpose of comparison, also proportionally raises the relative contribution of background noise in comparatively quiet source recordings, as observed for Miwa (Section 3.3); this interaction was not independently controlled for or corrected. Relatedly, al-though the within-site and between-site variability analysis (Sec-tion 2.4) indicates that acoustic differences between sites exceed the variation observed across takes recorded during a single visit, this analysis has two further limits worth stating explicitly. First, it cannot rule out the possibility that some between-site differ-ences are also attributable to each site having been visited on a different day, and therefore under somewhat different ambient conditions; disentangling site-level acoustic identity from such day-to-day environmental variation would require repeated vis-its to each site across a range of conditions, which we iden-tify as a direction for future work. Second, because the three takes retained at a given site were recorded with the same device and water-delivery method, and the same or similar microphone placement, low within-site variance is consistent both with a sta-ble acoustic identity for the vessel and with the same recording equipment and procedure simply having been used repeatedly at that site; the present design does not fully separate these possi-bilities, and doing so would require varying recording conditions independently of site. Separately, all comparisons in this study are among suikinkutsu recordings; no non-suikinkutsu recordings were collected for comparison. We do not treat this as a limita-tion of the argument advanced here: the claims made through-out this paper are comparative and within-category, character-izing and comparing suikinkutsu recordings to one another for the purpose of digital heritage documentation, and do not assert that suikinkutsu are always acoustically distinguishable from all other resonant-vessel or dripping-water sources in general; es-tablishing that broader, between-category claim is not something this analysis needs to support. Given the nature of the sites at which suikinkutsu are installed — fixed, embedded garden fea-tures rather than portable objects — recording them under a con-trolled acoustic setting is inherently difficult, and this study was accordingly conducted in situ . In any case, a dedicated control comparison is of limited practical value here: an acoustically un-related submission to the crowd-sourced platform proposed as fu-ture work would be expected to score poorly in the initial MFCC analysis and reveal itself automatically (Section 4.2). Varying recording conditions independently of site, discussed above, re-mains the genuine direction for future work arising from this analysis. Notably, this also meant that a precise boundary be-tween genuine low-frequency vessel resonance and ambient envi-ronmental noise could not be established; the attributions offered in Section 3.2 therefore reflect informed interpretation rather than a rigorously validated threshold. Future work may benefit from supplementary noise-floor measurements or recording protocols better able to isolate the suikinkutsu from surrounding environ-mental sound. However, it is worth bearing in mind that ambient background noise is an inseparable part of the suikinkutsu expe-rience. 

4.2 Future Work 

Onset-based descriptors for quantifying drip timing were also ex-plored but were not incorporated into the present framework, as the distinction between individual impacts and sustained reso-nance proved unreliable, particularly across recordings exhibiting both discrete dripping and continuous water flow. Future work may investigate more robust onset-detection methods validated against manually annotated recordings. Future work could also investigate the relationship between acoustic signatures and physical geometry, should architectural measurements become available. A further direction for future work is the development of a dedicated, modern platform for crowd-sourced documentation of suikinkutsu . This could lead to larger datasets and the incorporation of additional descriptors. Data-driven clustering methods may facilitate the automatic clas-sification of suikinkutsu based on acoustic similarity. As noted in Section 1, the existing national database appears not to have been updated in over two decades; a bilingual website and mo-bile application could allow visitors to submit photographs and audio recordings of suikinkutsu they encounter, along with loca-tion and accessibility information, helping to verify which sites remain active. Submitted recordings would need to be manu-ally reviewed to ensure sufficient audio quality before inclusion in any dataset. In receiving publicly sourced recordings, which are likely to vary considerably in quality and recording condi-tions, a minimum acceptable cosine similarity to existing verified recordings – derived from the pairwise comparisons reported in this study (Section 3.2) – could additionally serve as a useful au-tomated screening threshold, helping to ensure that submitted au-dio remains recognisably of a suikinkutsu prior to inclusion in any dataset. As stated previously, beyond manual review, an acous-tically unrelated recording submitted to such a platform would quickly reveal itself through a low MFCC similarity score below this threshold (see also Section 4.1). Such a platform could sub-stantially expand the scale of data available for analysis, enabling more robust machine learning approaches than the comparative framework presented here, which was necessarily constrained by the small number of recordings collected through manual field-work. This would, however, constitute a separate undertaking beyond the scope of the present study.
