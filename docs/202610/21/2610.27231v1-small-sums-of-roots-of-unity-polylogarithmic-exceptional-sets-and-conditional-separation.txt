# Small sums of roots of unity: polylogarithmic exceptional sets and conditional separation ∗Qi Cheng †

Abstract 

We prove a polylogarithmic exceptional-set estimate for symmetric five-term sums of roots of unity of arbitrary order. More precisely, at most O((log(2 N )) 11 ) points of the N -grid satisfy 

|1 + 2 cos(2 πx ) + 2 cos(2 πy )| < exp( −C(log(2 N )) 13 ). This implies the corresponding bound for small conjugates at the reduced conductor: outside polylogarithmically many real embeddings, every such sum is at least quasipolynomially large. The proof is unconditional and combines a polynomial-vector-field multiplicity estimate, arithmetic jet determinants, analytic propagation, and interpolation determinants for nearby grid points. As a companion result, assuming a uniform number-field abc conjecture, we prove that every sum of a fixed number of distinct prime-order roots of unity has modulus at least exp( −εp ) for every ε > 0 and all sufficiently large primes p, uniformly in the exponents. That proof uses simultaneous compression of the exponents, discriminant estimates, and cyclic resultants.

Such comparisons arise when comparing lengths of polygonal paths with integer coordinates: each Euclidean segment length is a square root of an integer. Numerical approximation can certify the sign of a nonzero S once the approximation error is smaller than |S|, so the required precision is governed by a separation bound. The elementary algebraic estimate can be very weak. The field generated by the square roots has degree at most 2 s, and every conjugate of S has absolute value at most s√H. If S̸ = 0, integrality of its norm therefore gives 

|S| ≥ (s√H)−(2 s−1) .

The resulting precision bound is exponential in the number of terms, despite the short description of the input. Improving this dependence is a central issue in the sum-of-square-roots problem. Cheng [17] obtains sharper separation estimates when the radicands are small relative to the number of terms, leading to a subexponential comparison algorithm in that regime. Short sums of roots of unity exhibit the same tension between a succinct expression and a large ambient field. Here the growing parameter is the order N , even when the number of summands is fixed. A lower bound exp {− C(log N )B } would permit certification of nonvanishing with polyno-mially many bits in log N for a fixed-length sum. This is why the distinction between exponential, subexponential, and quasipolynomial separation is computationally meaningful. The comparison with square roots is motivational; the arithmetic structure of the two families is different. A second motivation is polynomial identity testing by approximate algebraic evaluation. Chen– Kao [18] evaluate integer polynomials at suitable algebraic points constructed from signed sums of square roots of distinct primes. Degree restrictions ensure that an identically nonzero polynomial remains nonzero at the exact algebraic point. Their randomized choice of conjugates, together with a bound on exceptionally small values, allows the error probability to be decreased by increasing numerical precision without increasing the number of random bits. A cyclotomic version of this approach evaluates polynomials at roots of unity and asks for analogous control of small conjugates. For the algorithmic problem of deciding exact vanishing at roots of unity, see also Balaji–Perifel– Shirmohammadi–Worrell [19]. The role of an exceptional-set bound in removing randomness is particularly transparent. Sup-pose that, for a prescribed class of P ∈ Z[X], one knows uniformly that #{t ∈ (Z/N Z)× : |P (ζtN )| < δ N } ≤ BN whenever P (ζN )̸ = 0 .

If φ(N ) > B N , any ⌊BN ⌋ + 1 distinct admissible indices contain a value of modulus at least δN ;there is no need to identify the exceptional indices or to choose an index randomly. Approximating each value with certified absolute error at most δN /4 and testing whether its computed modulus exceeds δN /2 distinguishes the nonzero case from the case in which all exact values vanish. When 

BN and log(1 /δ N ) are polynomial in log N , this requires only polylogarithmically many evaluations and polynomial output precision. For this to be an identity test rather than merely a test modulo the cyclotomic polynomial, one also needs P̸ ≡ 0 ⇒ P (ζN )̸ = 0; the condition deg P < φ (N ) is one sufficient guarantee. 2An algorithmic conclusion further requires effective bounds and efficient construction and certi-fied evaluation of the chosen points in the input representation. Theorem D below supplies the exceptional-set phenomenon for the specific symmetric five-term family (with indices taken modulo sign). Extending it to suitable polynomial classes would provide a route to deterministic numerical identity testing in the spirit of Chen–Kao; the theorem does not by itself give a general derandom-ization of polynomial identity testing. Finally, small root-of-unity sums also arise in counting solutions of linear equations whose variables lie in a fixed subset of a finite field. Zhuang–Cheng–Wen [16] relate these counts to Fourier coefficients given by short sums of roots of unity, making separation bounds relevant to the resulting counting errors. 

1.2 Previous work and the five-term case 

Konyagin–Lev [15] used the distribution of exponential sums to strengthen lower bounds for sums of distinct roots of prime order. Their work adds Fourier information to the norm argument, while leaving the problem of uniform subexponential separation. Habegger [3, Theorem 7] obtained polynomial lower bounds for fixed-length sums simultaneously outside a sparse set of prime orders. This is a strong almost-all-primes result; the problem considered here also includes the exceptional primes. Constructions of small sums address the opposite side of the question. The cases with at most four terms admit explicit geometric analysis, whereas five terms already exhibit more complicated cancellation. Barber [6] proved upper bounds of order N −4/3 for five-term sums, with an improve-ment to N −7/3 for infinitely many orders. Kalogirou [7] gives further constructions for general numbers of terms. These results indicate the richness of near-cancellation configurations; they do not supply uniform lower bounds. A particularly natural five-term family is 1 + ζaN + ζ−aN + ζbN + ζ−bN = 1 + 2 cos(2 πa/N ) + 2 cos(2 πb/N ).

The sum is real, and its small values correspond to rational grid points close to a fixed analytic curve. This geometric description suggests counting the exceptionally small conjugates even when a lower bound at every conjugate is unavailable. In a complementary direction, cyclic resultants and Mahler measure relate products over conjugates to arithmetic growth. Stewart’s work on exceptional units and cyclic resultants [5], and Dimitrov–Habegger’s logarithmic equidistribution results for torsion points near atoral sets [4], supply relevant arithmetic context. The present paper develops two conclusions, at different levels of certainty. Unconditionally, Theorem D gives quasipolynomial separation outside polylogarithmically many embeddings for the symmetric five-term family at arbitrary reduced conductor. As a companion, under the uniform number-field abc conjecture, formulated precisely in Section 3, simultaneous compression and a cyclic-resultant estimate give subexponential separation for every fixed-length sum of distinct prime-order roots. The first conclusion bounds an exceptional set unconditionally; the second controls every conjugate under an arithmetic hypothesis.

# 9 Limitations and future work 

What is and is not proved. Theorem D bounds the number of exceptional embeddings at the quasipolynomial scale, unconditionally, but leaves those embeddings uncontrolled. Theorem 1.1 controls every embedding, but only at the subexponential scale and only under an arithmetic hypothesis. As noted in Section 1.3, neither Conjecture A nor Conjecture B follows unconditionally. An individual lower bound of the form exp {− C(log p)B } at every embedding in the symmetric five-term case remains unresolved by these arguments. 

Scope and possible extensions. The polynomial estimates of Sections 3–8 hold for any monic integer polynomial with constant term ±1, bounded Mahler measure, and nonzero cyclic resul-tant. The compression lemma explains why the distinct prime-order sums fit these hypotheses. Repeated terms can change the extreme coefficients, and general coefficients can produce nonunit roots. Composite orders also remove the automatic invertibility of the pigeonhole dilation. Those 14 extensions of the conditional individual estimate require additional arguments and are not claimed. The unconditional exceptional-set estimate of Theorem D uses a different argument and does allow composite conductors; the specific five-term symmetric family enters through the fixed curve E

of Section 2, and the method should apply to other fixed real-analytic curves cut out by Laurent polynomials with nonvanishing real gradient, although we do not pursue this here.
