LEAST-SQUARES FINITE ELEMENT METHODS WITH WEAKLY IMPOSED BOUNDARY CONDITIONS: NORM AND ERROR EQUIVALENCES, OVER-PENALIZATION, AND FULLY DISCONTINUOUS DISCRETIZATIONS 

SHUN ZHANG 

Department of Mathematics, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong, China 

Abstract. Weakly imposed boundary conditions in first-order system least-squares methods go back to Jespersen (1977), but two questions have remained open for decades: the right weights, especially for the Neumann residual, whose natural norm H−1/2 suggests hF , and norm and error equivalences, especially for indefinite equations. We answer both for general, possibly indefinite, second-order elliptic equations with non-homogeneous mixed boundary conditions. First, for the weights h−1 

> F

on the Dirichlet and 1 on the Neumann residual, we prove norm and error equivalences with mesh-independent constants for every discrete pair: the error in a mesh-dependent norm is equivalent to the square root of the functional plus a Dirichlet data term. The least-squares solution converges optimally in H(div; Ω) × H1(Ω), and the functional is a reliable and efficient error estimator, with a recovered flux also for approximations from other methods, even ones violating the boundary conditions. The key idea is to apply the stability of the boundary value problem to pairs that are discrete only in their Dirichlet traces, whose H1/2 norm an inverse inequality bounds by the weighted penalty. Second, these weights are the right ones: weaker powers, including hF on the Neumann boundary, destroy the norm equivalence, and stronger weights are admissible, since the penalty multiplies zero at a comparison function meeting the projected data. Third, for the fully discontinuous counterpart, a pure penalty method with both jumps weighted by h−1 

> F

, the error equivalence holds for every discrete pair, also for exact fluxes with normal traces not square integrable on interior faces. 

1. Introduction 

First-order system least-squares methods recast a second-order elliptic equation as a first-order system and minimize a sum of squared residuals [10]. In the resulting least-squares finite element method (LSFEM) the value of the functional is a built-in a posteriori error estimator, not only for the least-squares solution but for every conforming approximation that satisfies the boundary conditions. This rests on the coercivity of the functional. For general, possibly indefinite, equations it was obtained through compactness arguments [3, 8, 9], through stability estimates of the boundary value problem [19], and directly from the inf–sup stability of the variational problem under the single assumption that the homogeneous problem has only the trivial solution [30], where right-hand sides in H−1(Ω) are absorbed into the flux. This paper concerns the non-homogeneous mixed boundary conditions u = ϕ on Γ D and σ · n = ψ on Γ N , with σ the flux. Imposing the boundary conditions weakly is long known. Least-squares methods whose trial functions need not satisfy the boundary conditions go back to Bramble and Schatz [4]. For the first-order system, Jespersen [16] added the Dirichlet residual with the weight h−1 for the Poisson equation and analyzed the method in the mesh-dependent norm induced by the functional, and Bensow and Larson [2] used the weights h−1 

> F

on Γ D and 1 on Γ N , together with h−1 

> F

on the interior jumps, for the diffusion equation. Two questions have nevertheless remained open for decades. The first is which weights are right. The natural norms of the boundary residuals are those of H1/2(Γ D ) and H−1/200 (Γ N ), and their scaling suggests the weights h−1 

> F

and hF ; for the Neumann residual in particular, the right weight has been unclear. The second is a norm equivalence, and with it an error equivalence, in particular for indefinite equations. The argument of [2] relies on the coercivity of the weak form and does not extend to indefinite equations, it assumes that the boundary data are finite element functions, and it gives coercivity only on the finite element space; in [2] the functional serves as a refinement indicator without a proof of reliability, and the fully discontinuous variant [2, Sect. 3.3.1] is stated without analysis. Much of the literature has instead imposed the boundary conditions in the natural norms: through multilevel boundary functionals [26], through additional equations measured in the natural norms [27], or through dual norms that lead to saddle-point and minimax problems [23]. For ϕ ∈ H1/2(Γ D ) and ψ ∈ L2(Γ N ) none of these constructions is needed: the weights h−1 

> F

on Γ D and 1 on Γ N are the right ones, and they give the norm equivalence for general, possibly indefinite, equations, an optimal a priori estimate, and a reliable and efficient error estimator. The paper makes three contributions. The first and main one is the norm equivalence and the error equivalence, with mesh-independent constants, for general, possibly indefinite, equations; these are the first such equivalences for boundary conditions weakly imposed in weighted L2 norms. They hold for every discrete pair, whatever method produced it, and not only for the least-squares solution. Since the functional depends on the mesh, a compactness argument does not give mesh-independent constants; following [30], we derive the lower bound from the stability of the boundary value problem and bound the natural norm of each boundary residual by its weighted L2 norm. On Γ N this is the embedding L2(Γ N ) ,→ H−1/200 (Γ N ). The key idea concerns ΓD : we prove the norm equivalence for all pairs whose Dirichlet traces lie in the space W Dh of Dirichlet traces of the discrete scalars, where an inverse inequality bounds the H1/2(Γ D ) norm by the weighted L2 norm. This space is discrete only in the Dirichlet trace and contains every discrete pair. Because the lower bound rests on the stability of the boundary value problem, the same argument applies to the error. The Dirichlet trace ϕ − v of the error is not discrete; it is split into ϕ − Qhϕ, which depends only on the datum, and the discrete trace Qhϕ − v, to which the inverse inequality applies. This gives the error equivalence: for every pair in this space, the error in the norm induced by the functional is equivalent to the square root of the functional plus the best approximation error of ϕ in H1/2(Γ D ) by W Dh , a term that depends only on the Dirichlet datum. Hence the functional is a reliable and efficient error estimator for the least-squares solution and equally for finite element approximations computed by other methods, such as the Galerkin method with interpolated Dirichlet data; for these, a flux is recovered by minimizing the functional with the scalar fixed, as in the non-intrusive estimator of [20]. In particular, the a posteriori estimator no longer requires the approximation to satisfy the boundary conditions, and the Dirichlet term of the functional is the computable violation of the Dirichlet condition. For the least-squares solution, the norm equivalence and a Raviart–Thomas comparison flux that meets the projected Neumann data exactly give an optimal a priori estimate in H(div; Ω) × H1(Ω). The second contribution settles the weights: they are sharp, and any larger weights are admissible. For the weights h−αF on Γ D and h−βF on Γ N , the norm equivalence holds with the same constants for α ≥ 1 and 

β ≥ 0 and fails for α < 1 or β < 0, already on quasi-uniform meshes; in particular, it fails for the weight hF

on Γ N suggested by the scaling of H−1/200 (Γ N ). Larger weights leave the a priori estimate unchanged up to the approximation of ϕ in W Dh : a comparison function meets the projected boundary data exactly, so every weight multiplies zero. Thus the unweighted L2(Γ N ) penalty, although stronger than the natural H−1/200 (Γ N )norm, leaves the a priori estimate optimal. The mechanism is not specific to finite elements: on Γ N the weight 1 suffices for every trial class, and in every least-squares minimization a weight above the stability threshold can be increased without changing the error bound whenever the trial class contains an accurate comparison function that meets the boundary data. Remark 8.3 (minimum, over-penalization, and conditioning) . Three questions that are often discussed together are logically distinct. (i) Stability asks for a lower threshold. For the present boundary residuals the sharp thresholds are h−1 

> F

on Γ D and 1 on Γ N . (ii) Approximation asks whether the penalized defect is actually being approximated. If a compatible comparison annihilates that defect, its weight is irrelevant: “large weight times zero is zero.” Theorem 8.2 proves this for all α ≥ 1 and β ≥ 0; on Γ D only the approximation of the datum in W Dh remains. The same mechanism is familiar in discontinuous Galerkin methods. In the weakly over-penalized symmetric interior-penalty method of Brenner–Owens–Sung [5], the projected scalar jump is penalized more strongly than in the standard SIP scaling, yet optimal energy and L2 convergence are retained. 

(iii) Conditioning asks for the algebraic price of that choice. A penalty may be harmless for convergence and still make the stiffness matrix poorly scaled. 

Remark 8.4 (large boundary weights beyond finite elements) . The mechanism is not specific to finite elements. On Γ N the unweighted L2 penalty controls the natural norm of every square-integrable residual, by (4.5), so the weight 1 suffices for every trial class. In every least-squares minimization, a weight above the stability threshold can be increased without changing the error bound whenever the trial class contains an accurate comparison function that meets the boundary data, because the penalty multiplies zero. Relation with over-penalized discontinuous Galerkin and least-squares methods. Theorem 9.5 puts several familiar observations into one framework. For scalar jumps, the weakly over-penalized symmetric interior-penalty methods of Brenner, Owens and Sung [5, 6], recalled in Remark 8.3, are a precedent. Classical discontinuous LSFEMs likewise employ strong face penalties, including h−1 

> F

normal-flux jump terms; see Ye–Zhang [29]. For the diffusion equation, the discontinuous variant of the method of Bensow– Larson [2, Sect. 3.3.1] uses all weights of (9.1), and Bensow–Larson [2, Lemma 3.3] already compare with a conforming interpolant, a Brezzi–Douglas–Marini field together with a Scott–Zhang interpolant, so that all interior face terms vanish. Theorem 9.5 uses the same mechanism for general second-order elliptic equations with weakly imposed mixed boundary conditions, and shows why such weights, and any stronger ones as in Remark 9.6, are compatible with optimal approximation. 16 S. Bringmann [7] imposes the extra constraint that the lowest moments of the normal jumps vanish, which permits the weight hF on the flux jump but complicates the computation. Theorems 9.4 and 9.5 show that this constraint is not needed: the method is a pure penalty method with no extra mechanism, and on the unconstrained spaces with the weight h−1 

> F

, the error equivalence holds for every discrete pair and the a priori estimate is optimal. For the boundary residuals and the interior jumps alike, stability determines how much penalty is necessary, compatibility determines whether a stronger penalty enters the approximation, and conditioning determines its algebraic cost. The first of these is sharp for the weak boundary residuals by Theorem 4.4 and Proposition 8.1. The second is settled for both boundary residuals by Theorem 8.2 and, in the fully discontinuous setting, for both interior jumps by Theorem 9.5 and Remark 9.6. Once a stable method admits a zero-defect comparison, the penalty has already done its stability job and disappears from the approximation argument.
