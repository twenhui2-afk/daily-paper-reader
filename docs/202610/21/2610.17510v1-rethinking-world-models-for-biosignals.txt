## Abstract 

World models promise to learn how systems evolve and respond to actions, enabling simulation, planning and decision-making. Bringing this promise to biosignals requires more than accurate prediction of physiological recordings. We examine existing biosignal world models through two requirements for intervention use: a genuine action and evidence of its causal effect. We identify three fundamental bottlenecks rooted in physiology: Temporal Latency , the variable delay between an action and its observable effect; System Openness , the influence of drivers be-yond the modelled intervention; and Endogenous Dynamics , the system’s ongoing evolution without that intervention. Drawing on published evidence, we outline corresponding research directions and propose an Intervention-Effect Validation Checklist to guide evaluation. The aim is to help the biosignal community realise the core promise of world models by learning the latent mechanisms that govern physiological evolution and responses to interventions. 

## 1 Introduction 

World models learn representations of an environment and its dynamics, allowing an agent to simulate possible futures before acting [12, 13]. The learned dynamics can be reused for planning, policy learning and simulation across downstream tasks. The broader promise is to learn mechanisms that explain how a system evolves and responds to actions, supporting decisions through simulation of possible outcomes. Applications span games [21], robotics [34], autonomous driving [14] and interactive environments [3]. In physiology and health, the prospect is to simulate individual trajectories under alternative interventions [24, 17], with work on clinical records [32, 35] and continuously recorded biosignals, including EEG, ECG and wearable streams [36, 5, 19]. Existing approaches differ in what they model, what counts as an action and how they are evalu-ated. We examine whether their evidence supports intervention simulation (Section 2), then identify three fundamental bottlenecks: Temporal Latency , System Openness and Endogenous Dynamics .Intervention effects can be delayed, other factors also drive the signal, and physiological activity continues without intervention (Figure 1b–d). These bottlenecks motivate corresponding research di-rections and an Intervention-Effect Validation Checklist, with the goal of learning latent physiological mechanisms.

## 2 The state of biosignal world models 

2.1 Requirements for intervention simulation 

Definitions of world models remain unsettled [7]. We focus on their use for intervention simulation and decisions. An action-conditioned model predicts p(st+1 | st, a t), where st represents the system’s state and at an action. Biosignals only partially observe physiological state, making latent-state learning part of the problem. Two requirements distinguish this intended use from ordinary prediction. First, at must be a genuine action that could be set differently, such as a stimulus, drug dose or ventilator setting [31]. Another physiological channel or disease onset does not itself specify a selectable intervention. Second, the model must learn the action’s causal effect. Ordinary conditional forecasting has the same mathematical form but may only capture associations. Comparing interventions requires predicting what happens when an action is set differently [22, 27]. Treatment given to sicker patients, for example, can correlate with worse outcomes even when beneficial. Predicting this association does not recover its effect. Thus, conditioning forecasts on future inputs or interventions [16, 18] alone does not establish causal validity. Observational counterfactual methods require identification assumptions, including consistency, treatment overlap and no unmeasured confounding given observed history [2, 28].

## 3 Three fundamental bottlenecks 

Temporal Latency is the delay between an action and its observable effect. The delay can vary between subjects and occasions, so aligning an action with a subsequent recording does not establish when its effect begins. For example, Sheiner et al. [29] modelled the delay between plasma concen-tration and the paralytic effect of d-tubocurarine using an effect compartment, with equilibration rates varying across subjects. Observation timing can itself be informative: deteriorating patients may be measured more often, introducing bias if this timing is ignored [23]. Precisely timestamped stimulation or machine settings resolve uncertainty in action recording but not in physiological response delay. Evaluation must therefore distinguish the two and test sensitivity to plausible delays and the observation intervals present in the data. When several actions precede an observable response, attributing it to the latest action may be misleading. A world model needs enough action history to account for effects that emerge after subsequent decisions. 

System Openness means that drivers beyond the modelled intervention influence the recorded state. For example, Sipinková et al. [30] found that posture and breathing frequency changed the spectral properties of heart rate variability in healthy subjects. Such factors may be only partially observed. Moreover, clinical covariates can predict both subsequent treatment and outcomes while being affected by earlier treatment, complicating causal estimation [26]. Consider a clinician adjusting treatment in response to a deteriorating signal. The subsequent trajectory may reflect the treatment, the condition that prompted it and other concurrent changes. If relevant context is unrecorded, conditioning on the treatment does not separate these contributions [22]. Other drivers can also alter the signal even when actions are assigned independently. Evaluation must therefore account for both their contribution to observed changes and their possible influence on action assignment. 

Endogenous Dynamics are the system’s ongoing changes without the modelled intervention. System Openness concerns other drivers; Endogenous Dynamics concerns the system’s own evolution and how its pre-action state shapes the response. Fox et al. [11] showed that spontaneous activity explains a significant fraction of trial-to-trial variability in task-evoked fMRI BOLD responses. Under direct electrical stimulation, Rabuffo et al. [25] analysed simultaneous intracranial and high-density scalp recordings from 36 people with epilepsy across roughly 320 sessions and more than 10,000 single-pulse stimulations. They found that pre-stimulus brain state predicts responses to identical stimuli, with whole-brain measures more informative than local ones. Restricting stimulation to trials with 3favourable pre-stimulus signatures reduced response variability, suggesting that ongoing state can be used rather than left as an unknown. A model can therefore forecast accurately by learning ongoing dynamics while using little action information. Conversely, a state-dependent response can reflect a genuine interaction between ongoing activity and intervention. Evaluation must account for pre-action state when measuring the action’s contribution, rather than assume an independent additive effect. 

## 4 Toward validated biosignal world models 

4.1 Addressing the three bottlenecks Temporal Latency. Model action histories and delays on time scales distinct from signal sampling. Continuous-time formulations offer a starting point for irregular observations [28]. Test sensitivity to delays and observation schedules, distinguishing recorded action timing from physiological effect onset. 

System Openness. Record co-interventions, physiological context and measurement conditions. Controlled action variation can help separate intervention effects from treatment selection. Observa-tional analyses require identification assumptions and sensitivity tests for unobserved drivers; transfer across action-assignment policies alone cannot rule out confounding. 

Endogenous Dynamics. Compare models with and without action inputs using matched histories and prediction horizons. Test how responses depend on pre-action state, allowing interactions between ongoing activity and intervention rather than assuming additive effects. The pre-stimulus findings of Rabuffo et al. [25] motivate this direction. These are research directions, not established solutions. Progress need not require larger architectures: switched-linear models with roughly 300 ms of causal history explain evoked dynamics under precisely timed intracranial stimulation [1]. Well-characterised interventions provide a starting point for validating more general models. 

4.2 Intervention-Effect Validation Checklist 

Biosignal world models should be evaluated on whether they capture intervention effects despite Temporal Latency, System Openness and Endogenous Dynamics. The following checklist translates these three bottlenecks into concrete evaluation questions. 

1. Temporal Latency. Does the world model predict when an intervention’s effect appears in the signal? Do its predictions remain reliable when the delay varies or observations are taken at irregular intervals? Report how uncertainty in timing affects the predicted response. 

2. System Openness. Can the world model distinguish intervention effects from changes caused by other factors, including the condition that prompted treatment? Compare predicted effects with controlled intervention results where available. Otherwise, explain the assumptions needed to attribute changes to the intervention and assess how unrecorded factors could affect the conclusions. 

3. Endogenous Dynamics. Does providing the action improve predictions beyond what the model learns from the signal’s history alone? Compare both using the same past observations and pre-dicting equally far ahead. Does the model capture how the response changes with the state before intervention? Report the improvement and its uncertainty, including cases where the action adds little. 

4.3 Learning latent physiological mechanisms 

Addressing these bottlenecks and validating intervention effects can bring biosignal world models closer to learning latent physiological mechanisms. Representations must capture delayed action effects, relevant context and ongoing state to support simulation across intervention settings. Fore-casting and representation learning can contribute, but latent representations alone do not identify physiological mechanisms. Identifiability of latent states and controlled dynamics depends on system assumptions and action variation in the data [39]. 45 Conclusion 

World models promise to learn how systems evolve and respond to actions. Realising this promise for biosignals requires confronting Temporal Latency, System Openness and Endogenous Dynamics, even when forecasting is accurate. The research directions and Intervention-Effect Validation Checklist proposed here connect these bottlenecks to model development and evaluation. Progress should be judged by how reliably models capture physiological responses to interventions, bringing the field closer to learning the latent mechanisms that govern those responses. 5References
