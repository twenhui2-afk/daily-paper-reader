Abstract 

Federated learning allows dispersed oilfield sites to train a shared intrusion detector without pooling network traffic, yet its aggregation step forces a choice between privacy and robust-ness. Byzantine-robust rules must read each update in the clear, whereas secure aggregation hides updates from the checks that detect poisoning. We present PVBRF-ID, a two-server protocol in which sites secret-share their updates and the servers jointly test each update’s norm and its cosine similarity to a reference computed on a small clean root set, revealing one accept bit per site. Commitments, Merkle roots and a homomorphic audit make the inclusion of accepted updates publicly verifiable. On a 15-class Edge-IIoTset task with ten sites holding differently distributed data and ten random seeds, PVBRF-ID matched Fe-dAvg on clean data (macro-F1 0.708 vs. 0.706) and withstood sign-flip attacks (0.696 vs. 0.091). Against an adaptive attacker controlling 30% of sites, PVBRF-ID with secure clip-ping reached 0.641, whereas FedAvg fell to 0.046; plaintext multi-Krum remained higher by 0.052. An update-inference attacker recovered each site’s class mix from plaintext updates (Spearman correlation 0.81) but not from one server’s view. Backdoors passed the filter, and noise-based mitigation gave only heuristic protection. The implementation uses Python 3.13, NumPy 2.3, 30 rounds and 10 seeds. 

Keywords: federated learning; intrusion detection; secure aggregation; Byzantine robustness; industrial Internet of Things; oil and gas 

# Introduction 

Oilfield operators run geographically dispersed sites, such as wellheads, gathering stations and pipeline segments, whose operational-technology (OT) and Internet-of-Things (IoT) traffic is heterogeneous. Integrating information technology (IT) with OT in these assets has widened their exposure to cyber attacks[1]. Bandwidth limits, data-sovereignty rules and operational sensitivity keep this traffic at the site, so an intrusion detection system (IDS) trained on pooled traffic is rarely an option[2]. Federated learning (FL)[3] lets such sites train one detector while each keeps its traffic local. Public industrial IoT (IIoT) datasets such as Edge-IIoTset[4] were built with both centralized and federated evaluation in mind. The aggregation step of FL pulls two requirements apart. Compromised sites can poison the shared model[5–7], and the standard defences inspect each update in plaintext. Updates also leak the class mix and other properties of a site’s data[8, 9]. Secure aggregation[10] hides individual updates from the server, and with them the evidence a robust rule needs. This privacy–robustness 1arXiv:2610.12704v1 [cs.CR] 8 Oct 2026 tension has been documented across several federated learning settings[11]; here we build and evaluate an aggregator that resolves part of it. 

Secure and verifiable aggregation. Pairwise-masking secure aggregation[10] hides each up-date from the server. Prio[12] secret-shares client submissions across non-colluding servers and validates them with proofs. RoFL[13] combines commitments with zero-knowledge proofs that bound the L2 norm of an update, and ELSA[14] aggregates with two servers while tolerating malicious participants. Prio, RoFL and ELSA bound the size or check the well-formedness of an update, but none tests its direction against a trusted reference. FLOD[15] adds such a test: two non-colluding semi-honest servers use secure two-party computation to compare binarized client updates with an update from a small root dataset, and they reveal only the weighted aggre-gate. ShieldFL[16] measures cosine similarity between gradients encrypted under two-trapdoor homomorphic encryption. Neither FLOD nor ShieldFL gives a client a way to verify that its accepted update entered the published aggregate, and FLOD’s evaluation uses Gaussian and label-flipping attacks on image benchmarks rather than an attacker adapted to the test itself. Privacy-preserving aggregation in networked and transportation settings has been studied along-side these protocols[17, 18]. 

Byzantine-robust aggregation. Krum and multi-Krum[19] keep the updates closest to their neighbours, and coordinate-wise trimmed mean[20] discards extreme coordinates. FLTrust[21] weights each update by its cosine similarity to an update computed on a small clean root dataset. Adaptive attacks crafted against a known defence[7] show that each rule must face an attacker who knows it. All three rules read every update in plaintext, so none runs inside secure aggre-gation without change. 

Backdoors, leakage and differential privacy. Boosted model replacement injects backdoors that survive averaging[5]; norm clipping and added noise reduce their effect but do not remove it[6]. Leakage from gradients and updates[8, 9] motivates differentially private (DP) training, which clips updates and adds Gaussian noise[22, 23] and is accounted with Rényi DP[24]. Fed-erated IDS for IoT and IIoT are surveyed in [25]. DeepFed[26] encrypts model parameters with the Paillier cryptosystem but does not test robustness to poisoning. Rashid et al. [27] evaluate FedAvg on Edge-IIoTset under a trusted aggregator and leave poisoning defences to future work. Neither measures what the aggregator learns about each site or whether aggregation excludes sites with atypical traffic. Oblivious policy enforcement and privacy-preserving task coordina-tion in industrial and mobile network settings[28, 29] motivate extending these protections to the aggregation layer. We ask whether one aggregator can be private and robust at the same time, at a cost an operator can pay. Our contributions are: 1. Design. We introduce PVBRF-ID, a two-server, secret-shared aggregator. The servers test each update’s norm and a tolerant cosine against a root-set reference and reveal one accept bit per site. Published commitments, Merkle roots and an audit make the inclusion of accepted updates verifiable. 2. A design finding. A reference taken from the previous aggregate is public and attacker-influenced. An adaptive attacker captures it and drives macro-F1 to 0.012; a clean root set avoids this failure. 3. A worst-case evaluation. We benchmark PVBRF-ID on a 15-class Edge-IIoTset task (ten sites, ten seeds, paired statistics) against FedAvg, trimmed mean, multi-Krum and norm-only aggregation, under five non-adaptive attacks and a boundary-seeking adaptive attacker that exploits the cosine tolerance. 4. Measured privacy and fairness. An inference attacker recovers class mix from plaintext updates. Multi-Krum systematically excludes atypical sites, whereas PVBRF-ID with a suitable norm bound does not. 25. Negative results. Backdoors pass the filter, noise-based mitigation is heuristic, and plaintext multi-Krum is more robust at 30% adversaries. Experiments use public IIoT traffic with simulated sites, and no operator data are used. Four cryptographic components are modelled as ideal functionalities. The Methods section defines the threat model (Definition 1), the protocol and the experimental setup. The Results section reports accuracy, robustness, privacy, fairness and cost. The Discussion states when PVBRF-ID is the right choice and what bounds its scope. 

# Methods 

System, threat model and goals 

There are n sites, each holding labelled local traffic. Two servers S1 and S2 operate in separate administrative domains, for example a corporate security operations centre and a regional or contractor domain, and share a public bulletin board. The servers hold a small clean root set, for example captured on a testbed. 

Definition 1 (Threat model) . The protocol operates against three adversary classes. 

(i) Byzantine sites. Up to f < n/ 2 of the n sites are Byzantine. A Byzantine site may submit any vector as its update, coordinate with other Byzantine sites, and adapt its strategy to the full public state, including the bound Bt, the threshold τ , the published reference direction ˆrt,and the accept bitmaps of previous rounds. Byzantine sites cannot forge another site’s Pedersen commitment, break the secret-sharing randomness, or observe a honest site’s local traffic. 

(ii) Honest-but-curious servers. Each server Sj follows the protocol exactly but records everything it sees and tries to infer individual site updates from its view: its own shares {sji },the accept bits {bi}, the other server’s published aggregate share, and all public transcripts. The two servers do not collude and do not share private state. A server cannot deviate from the secure-test protocol or alter an accept bit without detection by the audit. 

(iii) External verifier / transcript attacker. A passive external adversary observes the public bulletin board in every round: the published aggregate ∆t, the accept bitmap {bi}, the Merkle roots, and the global model sequence {wt}. This adversary tries to infer site-level class distributions from the published transcript; it cannot see either server’s private shares. 

Out of scope. Collusion between S1 and S2; compromise of the root set; denial-of-service against the bulletin board; Sybil enrolment of new sites; and membership inference or model inversion from the released global model weights. 

Under Definition 1, PVBRF-ID targets five goals. (G1) Clean accuracy is close to FedAvg. (G2) The model is robust to the attackers of the Attacks subsection. (G3) One server learns nothing about an individual update beyond one accept bit per site per round and the aggregate. (G4) Inclusion of accepted contributions is verifiable. (G5) Honest but atypical sites are not systematically excluded.

Root-set reference and reference capture 

The reference rt is a local update computed on the root set; the servers know it, but the sites do not. The alternative, the previous aggregate, is public and shaped by whatever the servers accepted. An adaptive attacker can steer it so that attackers are accepted and honest sites rejected, a feedback loop we call reference capture.

Data and features 

We used the DNN file of Edge-IIoTset[4]: 2,219,201 flows in 15 classes (Normal and 14 attack types). Each flow was retained independently with probability min(1 , c/N k), where Nk is the class count in the file and the cap c is 100,000 for Normal and 10,000 for each attack class, so the caps are expected rather than exact counts; the two rare classes (MITM, 1,214 flows; Fingerprinting, 1,001 flows) were kept in full. The sample contains 222,947 flows (55% attack; per-class counts in Supplementary Table S1). Identifiers (addresses, timestamps and source ports) were dropped. Each text field yields a presence flag, its log length, its share of special characters, and signature flags for SQL injection, cross-site scripting, path traversal and file upload. Low-cardinality fields are one-hot encoded, and destination ports are encoded as top-15 one-hot indicators plus a high-port flag. The 80 features with the highest one-vs-rest correlation are kept; feature ranking uses all sampled rows, which introduces a small optimistic bias in feature selection that affects all conditions equally and does not alter the comparisons between aggregation rules. Per class, 20% of flows are held out for testing, and a stratified root set of 1,500 flows is withheld from training. 

Sites, model and training 

Ten sites receive class-skewed partitions drawn from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration 0.5 over classes. Across seeds, sites held 6,572 to 38,532 flows with attack shares from 14% to 95%. The model is a multilayer perceptron with layer sizes 80, 24 and 15 ( d = 2 ,319 parameters), trained with 30 local stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps per round (batch 128, learning rate 0.1) for 30 rounds. Supplementary Table S1 lists all parameters. 

Baselines 

We compare against centralized training on pooled data (reference), local-only training, FedAvg[3], coordinate-wise trimmed mean[20] (three values trimmed per side), multi-Krum[19] (assumed 

f = 3 ), secure norm-only aggregation (PVBRF-ID without the cosine test, same Bt), PVBRF-ID with the previous-aggregate reference, and PVBRF-ID with the root reference at τ = 0 and 

τ = −0.3. Trimmed mean and multi-Krum are configured for up to three attackers in every scenario, including clean runs. 

Attacks 

Two of ten sites (20%) attack, or three (30%) where stated. Sign-flip submits −4u. Label-flip maps each label y to (y+1) mod 15 . Targeted flip relabels all attack classes as Normal. Backdoor sets a trigger on the three lowest-ranked features of 50% of attack samples and relabels them Normal; ASR is the share of triggered attack test flows classified Normal. Boosted backdoor scales the same update by three. The adaptive attacker knows Bt, τ and the reference. It submits an update of norm 0.95 Bt along the loss-ascent direction −uhonest , at cosine τ +0 .02 to the reference: 80.02 for τ = 0 and −0.28 for τ = −0.3, where it exploits the tolerance. Supplementary Note 23 documents a correction to this attacker. 

Privacy attacker 

In no-attack runs, during the first 10 rounds, the attacker sees either the per-site updates (plain-text baselines) or one server’s share vectors plus the accept bits (PVBRF-ID); the colluding view combines both shares. Class-mix recovery is the per-site Spearman correlation between the centred output-bias update and the centred class histogram. A top-3 hit occurs when the site’s most over-represented class is among the attacker’s top three (chance 0.20). Similarity is the correlation between output-bias update similarity and class-mix similarity over site pairs. A neighbour hit occurs when the attacker’s most similar site is among the three truly most similar (chance 0.33). The accept-bit correlation, over all 30 rounds, relates a site’s acceptance count to its atypicality, the total-variation distance between its class histogram and the root distribution. This attacker is generic. 

Metrics and statistics 

Macro-F1 on the held-out test set is the primary metric; backdoor ASR and ε are secondary. We report means with t-based 95% confidence intervals over 10 seeds, and comparisons are paired by seed (same partition and test split). 

Reproducibility. Experiments were run with Python 3.13.9, NumPy 2.3.5 and pandas 2.3.3 on Windows 11 (64-bit). Total wall-clock time was 1.1–2.9 hours per configuration depending on whether commitments were enabled. All 870 main runs and 160 supplementary runs are deterministic given the seeds listed in Supplementary Table S1; the four plaintext baselines, which are unaffected by the tolerance-exploiting attacker, reproduced identically across the two runs on every seed. Code, raw per-run results and the figure script are available at https: //github.com/nawaralseelawi/pvbrf-id .

# Results 

Federation recovers rare attack classes 

FedAvg reached macro-F1 0.706 ± 0.008 , against 0.536 ± 0.019 for local-only training (paired difference +0 .170 ± 0.022 , all 10 seeds), and came within 0.010 ± 0.012 of centralized training (0.716; Table 1). The gain concentrated in classes that a site rarely sees locally: recall rose for MITM from 0.73 to 1.00, for DDoS-ICMP from 0.77 to 0.99 and for Password from 0.38 to 0.61 (per-class recall across all 15 classes in Supplementary Table S3; local-only figures are from site 0’s model as a representative site). For an operator, these classes are the attacks a single wellhead or gathering station has never observed, and federation is what makes them detectable at that site. Macro-F1 per round for clean and adaptive 30% runs is shown in Fig. 2.

Backdoors 

The filter did not stop backdoors. The attack success rate (ASR) was 1.000 for PVBRF-ID, FedAvg and norm-only aggregation, because backdoor updates are small and well aligned with the reference (Table 2). Trimmed mean reached 0.054 ± 0.051 , and multi-Krum was bimodal (0.401 ± 0.369 ; ASR 1.0 in five seeds and about 0 in the other five). Boosting the backdoor update by a factor of three passed the loose bound κ = 3 .5 (PVBRF-ID 0.872 ± 0.076 ; norm-only 

0.901 ± 0.071 ), and secure clipping alone removed it ( 0.048 ± 0.048 ). Against plain backdoors, clipping or noise alone had no effect (ASR 0.99). Clipping, noise and the aggregate clip together lowered ASR to 0.411, 0.150 and 0.091 at noise multipliers z = 0 .5,1 and 2, at clean macro-F1 of 0.689, 0.680 and 0.615 (baseline 0.708; Fig. 4). At z = 1 the paired change was −0.849 ± 0.069 in ASR and −0.029 ± 0.015 in macro-F1, and trimmed mean remained better by 0.096 ± 0.069 . The corresponding privacy budgets ( ε = 112 .6, 41.3 and 16.9 at δ = 10 −5 over 30 rounds) are too large to be a meaningful guarantee. Privacy against update inference 

From plaintext updates, the aggregator recovered each site’s class mix with Spearman correlation 

0.81 ± 0.02 and placed the site’s dominant class in its top three 70% of the time (chance 20%; Table 3, Fig. 5). Neighbour identification succeeded 60% of the time (chance 33%). For an operator, this means a plaintext aggregator learns which attack types each field site experiences, which is itself sensitive security information. Given one server’s shares, the same attacker scored 

0.00 ± 0.06 and 0.20. This result is expected by construction, because a single additive share is uniformly distributed, and it serves as an end-to-end check of the implementation. With both servers colluding, the attacker regained plaintext-level success, so non-collusion is an assumption of PVBRF-ID, not a result. The channels that remain open to one server are the accept bits and the revealed aggregate. The accept bit leaked little at κ = 3 .5: its correlation with site atypicality was −0.04 ± 0.25 ,honest rejection was below 1%, and three of ten seeds had no rejections. Whole-update similarity was a weak attack (correlation 0.24 for FedAvg and about 0.03 for multi-Krum), so we report the output-bias variant.

Communication cost 

At model dimension d = 2 ,319 and ten sites, online communication was 1.72 MiB per round, 19.4 times the upload of plain FedAvg (Supplementary Table S2). Excluding the placeholder cost of the ideal comparison, the ratio was 16.8; adding an estimate for ideal secure clipping raised it to about 27.4. Offline Beaver triples added 0.71 MiB per round. Upload alone was eight times the plain upload, because each coordinate is sent as two 16-byte shares. The audit and all inclusion proofs verified. Commitment verification took 8.1 s and the audit 0.8 s per round in a toy 512-bit group implemented in Python; these timings are upper bounds. 

# Discussion 

Where updates may not be exposed to the aggregator, PVBRF-ID keeps accuracy at the FedAvg level on clean data. With secure clipping, it stays within 0.02 to 0.05 macro-F1 of plaintext multi-Krum at 20% to 30% adversaries. It exposes one accept bit per site and a verifiable transcript, and it does not penalize atypical sites. The accept bit is a deliberate trade: FLOD[15] reveals only the aggregate, whereas PVBRF-ID reveals each decision so that sites and auditors can check inclusion. Where plaintext access to updates is acceptable, multi-Krum is the more robust choice at 30% adversaries, at the cost of excluding atypical sites. 

Deployment considerations. PVBRF-ID targets settings where two independently operated servers are available—a corporate security operations centre and a regional or contractor domain is a natural split in the oilfield context—and where a small testbed capture can supply the root set. The protocol adds roughly 19 times the FedAvg communication overhead, which is feasible on corporate WAN links but would be expensive on low-bandwidth satellite or radio channels to remote wellheads. In such settings, the root-guided cosine test could run on plaintext updates at the cost of privacy, or less frequent rounds (e.g., daily rather than hourly) could amortize the bandwidth cost. Table 5 summarises the five design goals and the evidence from the evaluation. The comparison with multi-Krum has one asymmetry. Our adaptive attacker targets PVBRF-ID’s norm bound and reference, while multi-Krum and trimmed mean face only a scaled loss-ascent update. Attacks crafted against distance-based rules[7] would test those baselines on equal terms, and we plan to add them. Robustness in PVBRF-ID also comes from bounded influence, not detection: attackers inside the acceptance cone are never rejected. The evaluation uses one public dataset with simulated sites and a multilayer perceptron whose macro-F1 (0.71) equals the centralized ceiling of our training budget. Hard classes remain hard: Fingerprinting recall is about 0.10, Uploading 0.43 and SQL injection 0.49. A second IIoT dataset and a pilot on operator traffic would test whether the robustness ordering transfers. The norm bound κ and threshold τ were chosen on this data; calibrating them on seeds held out from reporting removes this source of optimism. Four cryptographic components are idealized: secure comparison, per-coordinate range proofs, triple generation and the clipping and noise steps inside the aggregate. Their costs are estimates, and the Pedersen group is a toy. Instantiating the comparison with an existing secure-comparison protocol[33] and generating triples without a dealer would turn the cost estimates into measure-ments. The audit binds published aggregate shares to committed inputs but does not certify the accept decisions themselves. A server that deviates inside the secure comparison step can change a decision while keeping both bitmaps consistent; authenticated shares or a ZK proof of the accept bit would close this gap[34]. Privacy rests on non-colluding servers and does not cover leakage from the released global model. The inference attacker is generic; formalizing it and adding an attacker that combines accept bits with the aggregates across rounds would bound what one server learns. Backdoors are not stopped, and the noise defence is heuristic at ε ≥ 16 .9.Federated intrusion detection across oilfield sites must keep site updates private and still reject poisoned ones. We showed that secret-shared aggregation can keep a root-guided direction test: PVBRF-ID matched FedAvg on clean data (macro-F1 0.708), held 0.641 against a 30% adaptive attacker with secure clipping where FedAvg fell to 0.046, and did not exclude atypical 14 sites.
