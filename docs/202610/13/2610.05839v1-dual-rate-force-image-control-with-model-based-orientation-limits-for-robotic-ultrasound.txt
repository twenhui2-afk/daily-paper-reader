# Dual-Rate Force–Image Control with Model-Based Orientation Limits for Robotic Ultrasound 

Tyler Foster 1, Qiang Zhang 1,2, Member, IEEE , A B M Tahidul Haque 1, and Anh Thu Nguyen 3

Abstract — Robotic ultrasound couples a high-rate contact-force loop with slower, delayed image feedback, so image-guided ultrasound probe rotation can perturb contact force before the resulting image response is observed. We derive a closed-form orientation-rate limit that bounds the modeled rotation-induced estimated-force excursion over a finite horizon while accounting for disturbance rejection by the fast force loop. The limit depends on local contact stiffness, force-loop gains, a conservative rotation-to-force gain bound, the excursion budget, and the prediction horizon. We implement this model in a dual-rate controller with timestamp-based delay reconstruction and joint-torque-based force estimation, and evaluate it on a curved gelatin phantom using paired controller comparisons and component ablations. Relative to unconstrained image guidance, the proposed rate-limited controller reduced first-second root-mean-square (RMS) estimated-force error by 0.40 N while increasing cue-convergence time by 0.94 s. A fixed rate cap near the analytically predicted ceiling produced no resolvable difference in force error and converged 0.32 s faster, indicating that the principal practical value of the model is the rate-design rule rather than online prediction. Delay reconstruction had no resolvable effect at the tested latency. A single-subject popliteal scan demonstrated feasibility, although the image cue was noise-limited on heterogeneous tissue. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Brightness mode (B-mode) ultrasound provides real-time, radiation-free imaging at comparatively low cost, but its image quality depends strongly on how the probe interacts with tissue. A sonographer continuously coordinates probe position, orientation, and contact force while responding to surface geometry, tissue compliance, and the image itself; robotic ultrasound is therefore a coupled sensing-and-control problem rather than a trajectory-following task. Reviews accordingly identify scan-path generation, probe–tissue force regulation, and image-quality or orientation optimization as the central components of autonomous systems [1]–[3], and recent surveys [4], [5] note open gaps, among them that orientation-related interaction forces are often simplified and that real-time image feedback is still rarely integrated into online scan optimization [5]. Image features extracted directly from ultrasound can close the robot-positioning loop [6], and visual servoing on ultrasound confidence has been used to improve image quality through probe orientation [7]. Image and force sensing are therefore established as complementary. Learning-based methods have extended autonomy to navigation, compliant scanning, and complete clinical workflows [15]–[17], includ-ing policies trained under explicit force constraints [18]; this work instead keeps a physically structured force loop and uses the image only as a measured cue. A control issue that is less explicitly addressed is the mul-tirate dynamic coupling between force regulation and image-guided probe correction. Robot state and wrench estimates update at high rates, whereas B-mode images are acquired, transferred, and processed at much lower rates and with non-negligible latency. The difficulty is more fundamental than asynchronous sensing alone, because the two loops are coupled through the contact. An orientation correction computed from an image changes probe indentation, the contact moment, and consequently the normal force before a new image becomes available. Conversely, a fast force regulator reacts to the normal displacement induced by the orientation update and can partially oppose the intended correction. Dual-loop approaches have demonstrated image-dependent force adaptation and robust force tracking [14], Li et al. [19] incorporate trajectory-dependent feedforward and delay compensation for contact-force tracking, and Qian 

et al. [20] combine image guidance with biomechanics-informed force measurement. These approaches do not treat the image-guided orientation loop and the high-rate force loop as a delayed multirate system whose cross-coupling is predicted and used in the controller. This paper treats that coupling as the control problem. We propose a dual-rate orientation controller in which the slow image-guided loop accounts for both its own latency and its effect on the fast contact loop: the image cue is reconstructed to the present instant from the frame’s capture timestamp; the force excursion that a candidate correction would produce is predicted from a conservative bound on the rotation-to-force gain; and the prediction credits the fast loop with the portion of that excursion it will reject within the prediction horizon. This credit separates the method from a one-step safety filter, which must assume the excursion persists and therefore admits a lower rotation rate. While the correction demand is saturated, the constraint reduces to a ceiling on the rotation rate that depends only on the contact stiffness, arXiv:2610.05839v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 the force-loop gains, the gain bound, the force budget, and the prediction horizon, so it can be evaluated before image-guided correction begins, once the local stiffness and a valid upper bound on rotation-to-force gain have been established. The platform has no force/torque sensor ; contact force is estimated from joint torques. This raises the force noise floor and introduces model-dependent bias, so the force margins the controller must respect are small relative to the estimate’s error. The contributions are: 1) a closed-form design rule for the maximum image-guided orientation rate that limits the modeled rotation-induced force excursion over a finite horizon while accounting for force-loop rejection; 2) a paired hardware evaluation on a curved phantom against force-only, newest-frame, and fixed-rate-cap baselines, with ablations isolating the constraint, the fast-loop credit, delay reconstruction, and online sen-sitivity estimation, showing that the ceiling sets the trade-off between cue-convergence time and force er-ror, and that a fixed cap near the predicted rate matches the online constraint in force error; and 3) a single-subject popliteal scan testing feasibility on living tissue. Misalignment with the surface or partial loss of acoustic coupling lowers confidence on one side of the aperture, where image quality falls off from the transducer face downward. We quantify this asymmetry with a dimensionless scalar cue I, with I = 0 for a laterally symmetric image. The cue is computed from the ultrasound confidence map of Karamalis et al. [23], which assigns to each pixel the probability that a random walker released there reaches the transducer before the far field, with edge weights derived from intensity gradients under an attenuation model. Writing 

c(r, κ ) ∈ [0 , 1] for the confidence at row r and column κ,the map is obtained by solving a sparse Dirichlet problem on the pixel graph, here at 96 × 96 on a fixed crop of the displayed image by direct factorization with the symbolic analysis performed once. The per-frame cost on the exper-imental hardware, including crop, resize, solve, and feature extraction, was 22.4 ± 4.8 ms (95th percentile 30.7 ms; 2% of frames exceeded the 33 ms frame interval). Because the map is solved in parallel with capture, the control signal always comes from the previous frame, or from two frames back when a solve overruns. Each result is tagged with the capture time of the frame from which it was computed, reaches the controller a median of 33 .8 ms after that capture, and is held for up to one frame interval. The contribution is not the combination of image and force feedback, which is established [8], [13], [14], but a principled way to set how fast an image-guided correction may rotate the probe under force control. The coupling model gives the ceiling ω∗ of (14) in closed form, and the experiments support it in two ways. Cue-convergence time followed the realized rate, and in every block pairing clearly different rate limits, the lower one had the lower engagement error. A fixed cap of 0.20 rad/s, within 9% of the predicted ceiling 

ω∗, matched the online predictive constraint in force error, neither paired difference resolvable (upper 95% bounds 0.25 

and 0.26 N), while converging 0.32 s sooner. The ceiling therefore carries the force benefit of the prediction and can be imposed directly: compute ω∗ from the contact stiffness, the force-loop gains, the gain bound, the budget, and the horizon, and cap the rotation rate at it. Delay reconstruction had no resolvable effect, although S ran at 57 –82% of its measured value; it also had little to correct: under saturated demand the command ignores the cue’s value, and in the proportional regime the orientation loop crosses over at 

|S|KI ≈ 3.5 rad/s, where the 55 –90 ms capture-to-use delay (Section III, with τf ) costs only 10 –20 ◦ of phase, not counting the scanner’s unresolved internal delay (Section II). An online constraint can improve on the fixed cap where the demand falls below the ceiling or where stiffness or gain changes within a scan, which a single phantom did not test. The rotation-to-force gain depends on surface geometry and contact state. On the cylindrical phantom, it kept one sign and stayed at or below 26 .3 N/rad over the ±12 ◦

sweep (Section V), a narrower range than the rotations of Section VI, and it was not identifiable online from the controller’s own rotations. The prediction therefore uses a bound on the gain rather than a model of it. The gain bound ¯g = 40 N/rad exceeds every band gain measured on the phantom (largest 95% bound 33 .8 N/rad), so the predicted excursion, and with it the admissible rate ω∗, errs toward throttling. Where the true gain is smaller, the bound costs cue-convergence speed. Because comparison-run stiffness varied over only 

1068 –1199 N/m, the predicted dependence of ω∗ on K was validated only locally around this operating region. Several limitations qualify these results. The contact-force estimate has no independent reference during operation and inherits the accuracy bound of Section II, so all force results are stated in estimate space rather than as calibrated force, and they include the effect of the arm dynamics that the quasi-static estimator omits (Section II). The budget 

∆F = 0 .5 N, comparable to that accuracy ( 0.41 N RMS), constrains changes in the estimate rather than in the true force, whose excursion is not independently verified. The confidence map’s principal weakness is that a uniformly anechoic region presents no gradients to the random walk and may be assigned high confidence, so the cue reports asymmetry reliably but is not a contact detector. The vol-unteer scan exposed a second, related weakness. Over the curved popliteal fossa, one edge of the aperture lost acoustic coupling for sustained periods, and the loop did not null the resulting misalignment. The cue assumes that confidence differs between the two sides of the image mainly because of coupling; in heterogeneous tissue, attenuation and structure also lower and spread confidence on the coupled side, which likely diluted the asymmetry the decoupled edge should have produced. The cue is therefore insufficient for alignment on heterogeneous tissue. Surfaces on which the true gain approaches ¯g, such as stiffer tissue or sharper curvature, would need a different bound and were not tested. On tissue the cue was noise-dominated, its magnitude at release comparable to its residual, yet P still lowered 

E by 1.05 N relative to B2, which makes the cue rather than the architecture the binding limitation on living tissue. The cue is low-pass filtered and the filter’s group delay is compensated in (10), but residual cue noise on tissue still converts directly into commanded rotation, and the tilt limit bounds the consequence without addressing the cause. A confidence or cue measure that adapts to heterogeneous tissue rather than one tuned on homogeneous gelatin, or an explicit detector for edge decoupling, is the natural next step and is not attempted here. Finally, characterization is confined to homogeneous gelatin, and the volunteer scan is a single-subject feasibility test that establishes neither clinical safety nor anatomical generalization. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We analyzed the coupling between a high-rate force loop using joint-torque-based estimation and a slower, delayed image-guided orientation loop in robotic ultrasound. Under saturated demand, the proposed constraint, which credits the fast loop’s rejection over the prediction horizon, reduces to a closed-form rotation-rate ceiling computable before each scan from identified system parameters. Across seven paired comparisons on a curved phantom, orientation correction carried a measurable force cost, and the ceiling set the trade-off between cue-convergence time and force error. A fixed cap near the predicted rate matched the online constraint in force error and converged faster, and delay reconstruction had no resolvable effect. For ap-proximately stationary contact properties, the closed-form cap is therefore preferable to the online predictor because it provides comparable force regulation with lower imple-mentation complexity. On a single-subject popliteal scan, the rate-limited controller lowered the RMS force error E

by 1.05 N relative to the unconstrained baseline, although the image cue was noise-dominated on tissue. Future work includes developing an image cue robust to heterogeneous tissue and testing on more subjects and anatomies. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
