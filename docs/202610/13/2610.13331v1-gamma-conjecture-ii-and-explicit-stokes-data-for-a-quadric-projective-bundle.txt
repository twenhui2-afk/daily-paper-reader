# Gamma conjecture II and explicit Stokes data for a quadric projective bundle 

Xiaobin Li 

School of Mathematics, Southwest Jiaotong University, Chengdu, China 

9 October 2026 

Abstract 

We study the connection problem for the quantum differential equation of the Fano fourfold X = PQ3 (O ⊕ O (1)). The problem is to identify its canonical asymptotic solutions with exceptional objects and compute the associated Stokes data. We use exact Mellin– Bessel integral representations and uniform vector asymptotics on sectors wider than π,followed by analytic continuation that preserves the Gamma framing. Cohomological chiral symmetry and mutations of exceptional collections put all eight sections at a common phase. This proves Gamma conjecture II for X and determines a full exceptional collection and its Stokes and central connection matrices at the unit Novikov point and a specified admissible phase. The chiral symmetry also gives a flat symmetric bilinear pairing, an orthogonality identity for the Stokes matrix, and an integral unipotent square root of the monodromy matrix for a full turn. We obtain explicit formulas for the connection data at the opposite phase; on the Gamma lattice, the half-turn acts by tensoring with a square root of the anticanonical bundle. The same spinor integral transform constructs two canonical Gamma sections for PQ2m+1 (O ⊕ O (1)), m ≥ 1, at positive parameters with distinct spectrum. Their sectors have opening greater than π, and their leading constants are independent of the dimension. 

Keywords. Quantum cohomology; Gamma conjecture; Stokes matrix; Cohomological chiral symmetry; Exceptional collection; Mellin–Bessel transform; Helix monodromy.

1.4 The strategy of Proof 

The two spinor sections are the main analytic difficulty. Gamma II is known for the base Q3,but the base and fiber contributions to the projective bundle are analytically coupled. The two-dimensional eigenspace corresponding to u = ±2√r is not preserved by the full quantum connection (Theorem A.20). These exponents alone therefore give no reduction to a connection of rank two. Line bundles generate a subgroup of index sixteen in K0(X) (Proposition B.12), so the spinor classes are also needed to recover the integral object labels. We first determine three sections in the Gamma framing. For the structure sheaf, a positive real integral has a unique strictly convex minimum, which identifies the normalized section with fastest decay. For S0 and S1, we match the exact Mellin–Bessel formulas to their Gamma initial data at the regular end before estimating their asymptotics. The estimates then control the unbounded imaginary Mellin order, together with the Bessel order and the logarithmic branches. They give differentiable vector expansions on sectors of opening greater than π (Theorems 3.1 and 3.5). An expansion on a single ray may allow an exponentially smaller summand, so it need not determine a canonical column. We construct the line bundle sections by continuation. A clockwise loop in the base parameter carries the section of the structure sheaf to the L section. We justify this transport using the explicit connection after Laplace duality and uniform separation of the spectral clusters. Reflection gives L−1, and the chiral half-turn with transported logarithms gives U, LU, L 2U .Together with the spinor sections, these produce the full exceptional collection (L−1, S 0, O, L, S 1, U, LU, L 2U ).

We keep track of the Gamma label of each section throughout the continuation. We then take the auxiliary point q0 = (10 −2)3/4, r = 1, rotate these rays to the common phase ϕ0, and follow the collection to q = 1. Section 4.4 explains the coordinated choice of q0

and ϕ0. A real parameterization of the spectrum proves that this path crosses exactly two walls. The chronological mutations are L3, L 5; L2, L 6, with coefficients 0 , 0; −1, 1 (Theorem 4.11). At an earlier ray crossing, the canonical spinor column is represented by S1 − 4U rather than S1.Using the class at each crossing determines the final objects, shifts and matrix entries. The proof thus passes from characteristic classes to canonical sectorial solutions through three steps: identifying the spinor Gamma markings, proving uniform asymptotics on the required sectors, and continuing the sections to a common phase at the unit point. It uses the existing mirror, asymptotic and mutation theories in this explicit projective bundle calculation. 

1.5 Related works 

Dubrovin formulated a conjectural relation between semisimple quantum cohomology, excep-tional collections and monodromy data [17]. Guzzetti established the Stokes–Euler correspon-dence for projective spaces [26], and Ueda proved it for Grassmannians and smooth cubic surfaces [50, 51]. Iwaki and Takahashi treated orbifold projective lines with at most three orbifold points and positive orbifold Euler characteristic [37]. Cruz Morales and van der Put used multisummation and the monodromy identity to compute Stokes data for differential equations associated with projective spaces, weighted projective spaces and certain Fano hypersurfaces [16]. Galkin, Golyshev and Iritani [21] formulated the Gamma conjectures, and Iritani [32] constructed the Gamma integral structure. The relation between exceptional collections, helices and monodromy data is developed in [11]. Xiaowen Hu and Huazhong Ke prove Gamma II for smooth quadrics [28]; Sheng computes the Stokes and central connection data for

Fang compares central charges modified by the Gamma class with integrals over SYZ T-dual branes for compact semipositive toric orbifolds [18]. Tarasov and Varchenko identify equivariant Stokes bases for projective spaces with exceptional bases in equivariant K-theory [49]. Cotti and Varchenko develop the comparison with equivariant exceptional collections for projective spaces and Grassmannians [13, 14]. Our result concerns the coupled fourfold (1.1); its exceptional collection includes objects other than line bundles. Brown’s toric fibration theorem and quantum Lefschetz give our hypergeometric input [3, 4, 24]. Iritani and Koto construct formal quantum D-module decompositions for projective bundles [36, Theorem 1.7], while Hinault, Yu, Zhang and Zhang prove spectral decomposition theorems and extend framings for formal and non-Archimedean F -bundles, including uniqueness of the projective bundle decomposition map [27]. The reconstruction method of [27] is also incorporated in [36, Section 5.8]. Iritani studies quantum cohomology through shift operators and Fourier transforms in [35]. Cotti’s cyclic stratum and Borel–Laplace constructions reconstruct solution spaces in substantially greater generality [5, 6, 9]. Xu studies regularized limits of Stokes matrices for systems of Poincaré rank one [53] and derives connection formulas for a higher rank analog of Painlevé VI [52]. Tang and Xu construct shrinking solutions from boundary data and compute their monodromy [47]. Lin and Xu compute Stokes matrices for a special confluent hypergeometric system and its analogue with coefficients in an enveloping algebra [40]. Tang and Xu also express Stokes matrices through infinite matrix products and relate the quantum construction to Yangians [48]. Our comparison identifies the canonical sectorial continuations of specified Gamma sections and follows their integral labels to the chosen phase at the unit point. The general asymptotic existence theorems with parameters in [12] provide the analytic setting; the identification of these labels requires the explicit integral formulas and continuation used here. Shen and Shoemaker study Gamma decompositions in the exceptional curve specialization [46]; Cotti’s fiberwise theory distinguishes vertical and absolute quantum spectra [8]. Our connection retains both Novikov variables. The positive integral argument follows the mirror asymptotic approach to Gamma I [23], with related period comparisons in [34]. For del Pezzo surfaces of degree at least three, including non-toric examples, Fang, Wang and Yan Zhou prove the mirror symmetric Gamma statement to leading order for arbitrary K-group insertions [19, Theorem 1.5]. Exponential concentration for scalar quantum periods is proved under a nonnegative convenient weak Landau–Ginzburg hypothesis in [31]; that scalar conclusion does not identify the spinor sections or their connection constants. The counterexamples in [22] require the rightmost eigenvalue to be distinguished from the spectral radius. Hu, Ke, Li and Su give related counterexamples to Galkin’s lower bound conjecture for toric projective bundles [30]. Hu, Ke, Li and Su also prove propagation of the Gamma I property within a connected (SR)-region and use it, together with additional spectral and categorical input, to prove Gamma II for del Pezzo surfaces [29]. Our direct transport at small parameters and explicit mutations give an alternative to the multiphase criterion of [28]. Convergence of the big Frobenius structure [7] alone does not supply the sector estimates needed for this comparison. Section 4 completes the continuation, mutations and matrix calculation. Appendix A gives a coupled mirror realization and the local degeneration geometry; Appendix B records quantitative period asymptotics and the integral helix calculations. The full Gamma II proof concerns the fourfold (1.1); Section 3.4 treats the two spinor columns over odd quadrics.
