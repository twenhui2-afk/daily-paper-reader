A B S T R A C T 

The advanced operation of heat exchangers plays a critical role in the development of efficient and sustainable multivector energy systems. This paper proposes a hybrid physics-informed and data-driven framework for the analysis, identification, and regulation of transient heat transfer in counter-flow heat exchangers. A parameterized physical model combines classical heat-transfer relations with transport equations to capture convective propagation, transport-induced delays, and nonlinear effects associated with flow-rate variations. The heat-transfer coefficient is identified from experimental data, enabling the characterization of flow-dependent heat-transfer dynamics over a broad operating range. The identified model is then used to develop a hierarchical real-time control strategy. An inner loop regulates the volumetric flow rate using a high-order data-driven linear model, while an outer loop regulates the exchanged heat power using a machine-learning controller initially trained on the physical model and subsequently refined with experimental data. Experimental results demonstrate accurate reproduction of transient heat-transfer dynamics and effective heat-flow-rate regulation under varying thermal boundary conditions (identification fit above 94% in normalized root-mean-square error and total variation of the flow rate reduced by half compared to a proportional-integral controller). The proposed framework combines physical interpretability with data-driven adaptation for the analysis and real-time operation of dynamic heat-transfer systems. 

## 1. Introduction 

Industrial energy losses often reach 20 to 50% of the en-ergy consumed (BCS, Incorporated, 2008). Heat exchangers (HE) are key components of multivector energy systems, where their operating conditions may vary significantly in response to fluctuating thermal loads and energy demands. While conventional heat-transfer analysis primarily relies on steady-state formulations, dynamic operation requires an accurate description of transient transport phenomena and their impact on heat-transfer performance. In this context, the ability to predict and regulate the exchanged heat power under varying inlet conditions is essential for efficient ther-mal management and heat recovery. By introducing artificial intelligence to analyze and adapt the operation of these systems in real time, companies can increase their energy efficiency and minimize their ecological impact. Extensive research has been conducted on the design and optimiza-tion of HEs to improve energy efficiency under steady-state conditions. However, transient phenomena, such as pressure drops, variations in thermal load, or phase changes of the working fluids, are rarely studied due to the complexity of their nonlinear and distributed dynamics. In practical ap-plications involving fluctuating thermal loads, intermittent renewable energy sources, or dynamic process operation, transient transport phenomena may become dominant and

Understanding and pre-dicting these effects therefore remains an important chal-lenge in heat-transfer research, particularly because the in-teraction between convective transport, spatial temperature gradients, and time-varying boundary conditions is not fully captured by classical lumped models. A key research direc-tion in HE optimization is the operation and control of dy-namic behavior, particularly in the presence of disturbances and transient conditions, e.g. to ensure that a reference heat flow rate is transferred by the exchanger despite varying operating conditions. Controllability, dynamic performance, and diagnostic criteria are thus at the forefront of the op-erability challenges of HEs (Chew, Klemeš, Wan Alwi and Abdul Manan, 2013), whether for upgrading existing instal-lations or designing new ones. Dynamic modeling of HEs has been a longstanding focus and motivated several control approaches. Early works based on first-principles modeling date back to the 1950s, including the transient analysis of lumped dynamics and state prediction (Takahashi, 1952; Mozley, 1956). Lumped linear models were later simplified through the linear cross-convection model for predictive control by Laszczyk and Richalet (1999), and further extended to include mass flow rate effects under steady flow conditions via parameter-ization by Laszczyk (2017). Several control approaches based on lumped linear dynamics have been successfully implemented in HE experiments. For instance, proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control has been combined with feedforward control (Khare and Singh, 2010), fuzzy logic controllers (Al-Dawery, Alrahawi and Al-Zobai, 2012), and relay auto-tuning and internal model control methods using an auto-regressive with moving average and exogenous

input (ARMAX) representation (Sahoo, Radhakrishnan and Rao, 2017). The existence and uniqueness of solutions for lumped dynamics of compartmental models with constant flow rates are analyzed by Zavala-Río and Santiesteban-Cos (2007), along with a stability analysis of the equilibrium states. This stability property is exploited by Zavala-Río, Astorga-Zaragoza and Hernández-González (2009) to de-sign a controller for flow regulation that only needs a partial state knowledge. Model predictive control techniques for lumped models have also been explored, both for individ-ual units (Dyrska, Horváthová, Bakaráč, Mönnigmann and Oravec, 2023) and for networks of HEs (Jalal and Ali, 2024). More advanced dynamic modeling and control meth-ods account for complex physical processes that involve nonlinearities, uncertainties, and spatially distributed phe-nomena. The temperature dynamics within the HE can be described by a system of coupled, first-order hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs). Specifically, applying heat transport equations and conservation of energy, in con-junction with appropriate assumptions for 1-D modeling, leads to the system of first-order PDEs proposed by Ansari and Mortazavi (2006), which describes the dynamics of the two fluids and of the wall. Dividing the wall enthalpy between compartments of the inside and outside fluids, a simplified model is proposed by Bonne (2014), where the wall dynamics is included in the hot and cold flows (increasing their effective time constants) and the model is reduced to two states. This model is used for real-time estimation and observer design by Hamze, Witrant, Bresch-Pietri and Fauvel (2018); Zobiri, Witrant and Bonne (2017), and enables the analysis of thermal wave propagation along the exchanger. The accuracy of dynamic models is also strongly affected by the heat-transfer coefficient. While stan-dard approximations use parameterized expressions depend-ing mostly on the Reynolds number (Re) (Gnielinski, 1975; Vera-García, García-Cascales, Gonzálvez-Maciá, Cabello, Llopis, Sanchez and Torrella, 2010), considering boundary losses in a more global perspective is also crucial (Kumar, Nakshatrala and Patrick, 2026). Data-based methods and machine learning (ML) algo-rithms have been increasingly applied to enhance HE effi-ciency and address maintenance needs (e.g. to prevent foul-ing, the accumulation of unwanted materials that degrades heat-transfer performance). Although many studies focus on offline design optimization, recent research highlights the potential of ML to improve control system adaptability and performance under varying operating conditions. For exam-ple, a neural network (NN) approach for nonlinear system identification and control is presented by Bittanti and Piroddi (1997), and evaluated on simulation results. Díaz, Sen, Yang and McClain (2001) predict the dynamic behavior of an HE using a NN, and a second network controls the temperature of the air passing over it. Carvalho, Carvalho and Ravagnani (2020) use an artificial NN model in a predictive control method to control the outlet oil stream temperature of an HE. Ragupathi, Vijaya Prakash, Kannan, Hari Ganesh and Justin Maria Hillary (2024) predict heat transfer in spiral tubes using regularized NNs. A physics-informed NN model is used by Majumdar, Jadhav, Deodhar, Karande, Vig and Runkana (2025) for real-time monitoring of an industrial air heater. ML is combined with computational fluid dynamics by He, Ye, Wang, Zhang, Tian, Qiu and Su (2025), in an on-line method for rapid fouling detection, leveraging real-time estimation of the heat-transfer coefficient. Najjar, Gupta, Hare, Kandil and Walthall (2016) utilize a reference quasi-static physical model to train ML algorithms for diagnosis and optimal sensor placement. A Long Short-Term Memory 

NN (capable of capturing long-range temporal dependen-cies) is employed by Hou, Zhang, Yan, Wang, Ma and Jiang (2025) to predict temperature evolution and system health status, supported by experimental validation. More related references can be found in the recent survey on NNs for ther-mal analysis and control of heat exchangers by Mohanraj, Jayaraj and Muraleedharan (2015). This survey highlights three limitations of NNs that are considered in this paper: overtraining errors (need to optimize the training cycles and number of training data), extrapolation errors (representa-tive information on the validity domain), and training data requirement (a large and optimized data set is necessary). These limitations are addressed here by first defining a parameterized physical model prior to the NN definition and then selecting a NN architecture that is suitable for efficient feedback control. In this work, a hybrid physics-informed and data-driven framework is proposed to analyze, identify, and regulate transient heat-transfer mechanisms in counter-flow heat ex-changers. Particular attention is devoted to the characteri-zation of transport-induced delays, convective propagation effects, and flow-dependent heat-transfer dynamics that are typically neglected in steady-state formulations. The main contributions are: 1. a distributed transient heat-transfer model that com-bines transport equations with classical heat-exchanger theory and accurately reproduces thermal-wave prop-agation phenomena; 2. an experimental identification methodology allowing the reconstruction of a flow-dependent heat-transfer coefficient and the derivation of a Nusselt–Reynolds map over a wide operating range; 3. a hybrid machine-learning strategy in which the con-troller is first trained on the identified physical model and subsequently adapted using experimental data, thereby reducing the amount of experimental data required while accounting for unmodeled dynamics.

## 2. Heat Exchanger Test Bench and Control Architecture 

An experimental test bench for HE control has been developed at GIPSA-lab to investigate transport phenomena and transient thermal behavior. This test bench is depicted in Fig. 1, and a technical description of its components is provided in Appendix A. This section describes the system configuration and the proposed control architecture. 

2.1. Heat Exchange Setup 

This study focuses on the counter-flow configuration, in which the hot and cold fluids flow in opposite directions. Fig. 2 shows a schematic representation of the setup, in-cluding the temperature and flow sensors and the actuated valve. The hot fluid in the heated water tank is maintained at 𝑇 ℎ𝑖𝑛 = 60 °C using a thermostat. It is circulated by a pump, and its volumetric flow rate remains constant (PI-regulated) during the experiments. The flow rate is regulated by an actuated valve based on the measurement provided by the volumetric flow meter, ̇ 𝑉 ℎ. The heat from the hot fluid is transferred to the cold fluid in the HE, after which the hot fluid flows directly back to the hot tank. The cold fluid follows an independent path in a similar HE-tank circuit. It flows in the opposite direction through the HE, and its volumetric flow rate, ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 , is the regulated variable used to control the heat exchange. The temperature of the cold tank is not regulated. 

2.2. Overview of the Control Architecture 

The proposed control architecture, shown in Fig. 3, includes two nested control loops: an inner loop for volu-metric flow-rate control and an outer loop for heat-flow-rate regulation. Each control loop includes a reference data set, an offline model, and a real-time controller. The models are designed to capture the time scales relevant to each loop: fast dynamics in the inner loop, dictated by the response time of the actuated valve and the fluid flow, and slower dynamics in the outer loop, governed by the thermal behavior of the HE. Each model depends on tunable parameters that are identified through a learning process based on the reference data set. This data set must be informative, with sufficient amplitude and frequency content to accurately represent the desired operating conditions and distinguish between candidate models. ## 3. Valve Modeling and Volumetric Flow Rate Regulation 

Accurate volumetric-flow-rate regulation is required to achieve effective heat-flow-rate control. This section de-scribes how the inner control loop accomplishes this ob-jective by controlling the motorized proportional valve on the cold-fluid circuit using the measured volumetric flow rate, ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 . The water pump operates continuously at a fixed manometric head, while the volumetric flow rate is adjusted through the actuated valve. 

3.1. Experiments for Dynamic Modeling 

Preliminary experiments on the motorized valve, de-scribed in Appendix B, show that the static gain of the valve varies significantly with ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 . The volumetric flow rate also affects the amplitude of the high-frequency oscillations, shown in Fig. 4. These two nonlinear effects can strongly influence control performance. The control design therefore requires a reference data set that accurately captures the frequency response over the operating range of interest, providing a relatively high-order dynamic model suitable for precise tuning of a proportional–integral (PI) controller. A sampling time of 𝑡 𝑠 = 50 ms is selected based on preliminary valve-identification experiments, and an input signal that is sufficiently rich to excite all relevant frequen-cies is chosen. Specifically, a tenth-order pseudo-random binary sequence (PRBS), designed according to the method described by Ljung (1986), is applied to the valve. This signal is centered at 25 % of the input range with an am-plitude of 10 %. The resulting input range, spanning from 15 % to 35 %, is expected to produce a volumetric flow rate between 30 and 140 L/h, thereby covering a broad operating range. The duration of the PRBS is given by (2 𝑁 − 1) 𝑡 𝑠 ≈51 s , corresponding to 1023 samples with 𝑁 = 10 . The input sequence and the corresponding system response over three consecutive cycles are shown in Fig. 5. 3.2. Method: Offline Model and Controller Design

More specif-ically, the method generates a Hankel singular-value plot representing the relative energy contribution of each system state. An appropriate model order is then selected based on the dominant singular values, and the corresponding state-space model is identified. For the valve, this method yields a fourth-order model with a one-sample delay, induced by the zero-order hold used during data acquisition. ## 4. Dynamic Model of Heat Transfer and Transport Phenomena 

A physics-based model, tailored to the experimental test bench using a dedicated data set, is proposed in this section. This model must capture heat transport phenom-ena and nonlinearities to cover a wide range of operating conditions while remaining computationally efficient for parameter optimization. The dynamics are described by a classical one-dimensional transport equation derived from energy conservation principles and tailored to the specific HE by parameterizing the heat-transfer coefficient and heat losses. The outer-loop controller relies on this model for both tuning and the first-stage training of the NN used in the feedback law.

4.2. Methods 

The schematic representation of the simplified HE model is illustrated in Fig. 2 (top-middle part). It consists of two volumes of length 𝐿 , separated by a wall. In the outer vol-ume, a hot fluid with temperature 𝑇 ℎ(𝑥, 𝑡 ) flows rightward. In the inner volume, a cold fluid with temperature 𝑇 𝑐 (𝑥, 𝑡 )

flows in the opposite direction. To analyze the temperature dynamics of the two flows within the HE, several classical simplifying assumptions are introduced (Bonne, 2014): (i) the flow is one-dimensional (along an axis denoted by 𝑥 in Fig. 2), with the hot fluid moving in the positive direction; (ii) heat diffusion within the inner and outer tubes is negligi-ble; (iii) the wall thickness is neglected, and the heat-transfer coefficient is assumed to be uniform and constant in space; (iv) the connecting pipes and the cold tank are perfectly insulated and do not exchange heat with the surrounding en-vironment; (v) densities and specific heats are constant over the temperature range considered in the experiments. Under these assumptions, heat exchange occurs solely between the two fluids through the inner HE tube and between the hot fluid and the ambient air through the outer HE tube. The only relevant mechanism considered in the energy conservation model is convective heat transfer.

4.2.2. Heat transfer coefficient 

The physical model is tailored to the experimental test bench by parameterizing the heat-transfer coefficient ℎ ac-cording to the reference data set presented in Section 4.1. This parameter is crucial because it governs the amount of heat energy transferred from the hot side to the cold side. It depends on various factors, including the type of medium (gas or liquid), the flow velocity, and the physical proper-ties of the fluid, such as viscosity and other temperature-dependent quantities. Consequently, the behavior of ℎ is highly complex and can exhibit significant variations in response to small changes in these influencing parameters. An accurate de-termination of ℎ is generally possible only under steady-state conditions and requires an exhaustive experimental procedure grounded in fluid-mechanics principles. This typ-ically involves the evaluation of the Prandtl (Pr), Reynolds (Re), Nusselt (Nu), or Stanton numbers (Gnielinski, 1975; Vera-García et al., 2010). This problem is simplified here as only single-phase fluids are considered (constant Pr). In that case the log-mean temperature difference (LMTD) method under steady-state conditions used by Gnielinski (1975) has been shown to provide an accurate estimation. As energy conservation is easier to express for the cold fluid (which gains all its heat from the hot fluid) than for the hot fluid (which also dissipates heat to the surrounding environment), the cold fluid is chosen for determining the heat-transfer coefficient. This effect has a limited impact here but should be taken into account in larger industrial processes. 

## 5. Heat Flow Rate Regulation 

To track a reference heat flow rate ̇ 𝑄 ref , the heat flow controller compensates for the system’s transient behavior by modulating ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 , as depicted in Fig. 3. The hot inlet temper-ature and the hot volumetric flow rate are assumed to remain nearly constant. Heat-flow-rate regulation is achieved by designing a feedback law that directly uses a NN. Follow-ing a transfer-learning strategy, the NN is first trained on input–output data generated with the calibrated physical model of Section 4, which is used to select its architec-ture and hyperparameters and to provide initial network weights. The network is then fine-tuned on a dedicated, limited experimental data set (second-stage training). This approach transfers the knowledge embedded in the physical model to the data-driven controller, reducing the amount of experimental data required and the risk of overtraining on it (Pan and Yang, 2010). The impact of parameter estimation uncertainty on the closed loop and of several design options is also investigated in this section, using the reference phys-ical model and experimental results. 

5.1. Data Set for the Second-Stage Training of the Neural Network 

The experiment used to finalize the training of the NN on real data is similar to the one used in the previous section to evaluate the physical model (in terms of ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 fluctuations), but carried out with colder initial tank and ambient temper-atures. The reference data, shown in Fig. 13, consist of ̇ 𝑉 𝑐 ,regulated by the inner loop proposed in Section 3, and ̇ 𝑄 𝑐 ,calculated from Eq. (12). The first 100 s of data are discarded for the training, as this initial behavior does not correspond to the classical operating conditions for regulation (as dis-cussed in the previous section). The sequence of steps of arbitrary amplitude covers the range of expected operation, and the duration of the steps is set to capture the transient response of the heat flow rate.

## 6. Conclusion 

This work presents a novel physics-aware and data-driven method for real-time modeling and control of heat exchange dynamics in HEs. The physical model accounts for transport phenomena, bilinearities, and nonlinear dynamics, and is calibrated using a steady-state reference data set. An LMTD-based methodology combined with a Dittus–Boelter correlation is employed for accurate parameterization of the heat-transfer coefficient between the two fluids. The heat losses at the boundaries of the HE are included following a similar strategy. This model captures transient heat-transfer mechanisms, including transport-induced delays within the HE and nonlinear coupling effects, while maintaining pre-dictive accuracy. It is thus particularly suitable for online regulation adaptation and diagnostics. The control architecture integrates two nested control loops: an inner loop for precise regulation of the cold fluid’s volumetric flow rate, and an outer loop for accurate tracking of the desired heat flow rate. The inner loop is implemented using a high-order linear ARMAX model identified from experimental data and provides a precise transient regulation of the volumetric flow rate. The outer loop incorporates a nonlinear NN model, trained initially on the physical model and refined with experimental data. Two control strategies for heat flow regulation are evaluated: a classi-cal PI controller and a NARMA-L2-based NN controller. The NARMA-L2 approach significantly improves control smoothness and, with a sufficiently rich architecture, track-ing performance. The best-performing architecture (NN2) exhibits lower tracking errors and reduced actuation effort compared to the PI controller, making it a promising solution for robust and efficient HE control.

## A. Experimental Test Bench 

The HE system, shown in Fig. 1, consists of two large tanks (one for hot water and one for cold water), a network of pipes connecting the components, and two primary circulat-ing pumps ( Priux Home 40-25/180 high-efficiency pumps by Salmson, one for each circuit). Flow control is achieved via four motorized proportional valves (ASCO TM E290 ), which are controlled through a MATLAB ®-Simulink ® interface. The valve input is a % of the input range (10 V), which is con-verted into a voltage reference and subsequently determines the volumetric flow rate circulating through the water circuit. The core of the setup is a tubular double-pipe HE, depicted in Fig. A.1, 1.14 m long, rated at approximately 2.3 kW of thermal power, and constructed from 316L stainless steel by Barriquand Heat Exchangers TM . The hot water tank is equipped with an immersed heater, which brings the fluid to a predefined temperature before circulating it through the external tube of the exchanger (Hamze et al., 2018). The cold water tank supplies fluid to the internal tube. The test bench includes manual valves that allow the operator to adjust flow paths, enabling operation in either parallel-flow or counter-flow mode. Additionally, configura-tions can be set to mix hot water with a colder stream or to connect the cold side directly to the water supply network. Several sensors provide critical measurements. Tempera-ture is measured at both HE boundaries (inlet and outlet for both flows) and in the tanks using PT100 Ω B-Class 

probes (D05979). Volumetric flow rates at the HE inlets are monitored using FT-110 Series TurboFlow ® sensors. Pressure is measured at the HE inlet and outlet for both circuits and in the cold tank using IPS Series PS-G1000-3A4 transducers (0–1 bar range). The system is managed via two desktop PCs: a target PC for real-time data acquisition and a host PC that communicates with it through Simulink ®

xPC Target (Hamze et al., 2018). All components (sensors, valves, etc.) are connected to a National Instruments TM NI PCI-6221 I/O board, which interfaces with the target PC.
