# Phononic Frequency Combs in Twisted Bilayer Transition Metal Dichalcogenide Heterostructures from First Principles 

Naman Arora 1 and Adarsh Ganesan 1

> 1

Department of Electrical and Electronics Engineering, Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani, Dubai Campus, Dubai International Academic City, Dubai, UAE 

(Dated: October 12, 2026) Phononic frequency combs are equidistant spectral ladders generated when a driven vibrational mode transfers energy to an autoparametrically excited subharmonic mode through anharmonic intermode coupling. In previous studies, the coupling strength governing this energy transfer has typically been treated as a free parameter, extracted from a fabricated device, or adopted from an earlier calculation for a specific mode pair. As a result, whether a given crystal can support a phononic frequency comb, and the drive strength required to generate it, has not been predictable a priori. Here we obtain the complete parameter set from density functional theory. Twisted bilayers of MoS 2/WSe 2 and MoS 2/MoSe 2 at the commensurate angle 21 .787 ◦ adopt space group 

P 3 in a 42-atom √7 × √7 R21 .787 ◦ moiré supercell. The second-order force constants provide the mode frequencies; the third-order constants provide the linewidths through the Boltzmann transport equation and hence the quality factors; and the same third-order tensor projected onto the phonon eigenvectors of a chosen mode pair provides the anharmonic couplings κ12 and κ22 . Both bilayers constitute a mode pair within 0.8% of the 2 : 1 resonance with ω1/ω 2 = 1 .9923 in MoS 2/WSe 2

and 1.9943 in MoS 2/MoSe 2. By integrating the resulting two-mode equations, both modes generate combs through a discontinuous transition: at 701 fth in MoS 2/WSe 2 the envelope variance rises from 

2.1 × 10 3 to 1.37 × 10 4 and the tooth count of mode 1 from 7 to 16 within a single 5 fth increment, identifying the onset as subcritical. The spectral entropy peaks before this jump, at 341 fth . The two compounds carry comparable coupling, differing by a factor of 1.2. However, the comb excitation threshold is set by the quality-factors of signal modes: Q2 = 672 for MoS 2/WSe 2 and Q2 = 3844 

for MoS 2/MoSe 2.

I. INTRODUCTION 

A frequency comb is a spectrum of equidistant lines generated by nonlinear mixing, and its optical realisation transformed frequency metrology by providing a coher-ent link between optical and microwave frequencies [1–3]. The vibrational counterpart was demonstrated by Gane-san, Do and Seshia in an aluminium nitride-on-silicon micromechanical resonator [4], where a driven mode at 

ω1 was found to transfer energy to an autoparametrically excited mode near ω1/2, producing a ladder of phase-coherent lines. The mechanism is distinct from the Kerr nonlinearity of optical microcombs [34, 35]: it requires two modes in an approximate 2 : 1 internal resonance and a quadratic anharmonic coupling between them, so that the driven mode modulates the effective spring constant of the subharmonic mode at twice its own resonance fre-quency. The comb spacing is not set by a cavity free spectral range but by the slow-flow beat frequency, the residual detuning between ω1 and 2ω2 [5]. The conditions under which such a comb exists were established subsequently. Qi et al. derived the threshold drive from a slow-flow reduction of the two-mode equa-tions and showed that the threshold is set by the prod-uct of the two damping rates divided by the coupling [6]. Combs have since been reported in quartz [7], carbon nanotubes [8], lithium niobate [9], capacitive CMOS-MEMS resonators [10], graphene membranes [11] and ferroelectric hafnia-zirconia resonators [33]. Frequency combs have been generated in a single driven mode wherein the second mode is replaced by a negative non-linear friction [12]. Also, phononic combs have been ob-served to form spontaneously in the optical phonons of the van der Waals crystals CrGeTe 3 and CrSiTe 3 [30]. The nonlinear landscape surrounding the comb is itself rich: cyclic-fold bifurcations, torus doubling and coex-isting regular and irregular solutions have all been cata-logued [13, 36], and the sensitivity of comb observables to perturbation has been proposed as a sensing mech-anism [14]. Combs have also been predicted in a bulk crystal: Rangwala and Ganesan proposed the idea of driving a Higgs-like phonon mode of hexagonal InMnO 3

to obtain combs through its coupling to the Goldstone-like mode [31]. A distinct route to comb formation has been identified very recently. Using molecular dynam-ics driven by a machine-learned force field trained on ab initio data, Jayakrishnan et al. [29] showed that a sin-gle isolated phonon mode with strong self-anharmonicity and a few available scattering channels produces a comb spontaneously, without any drive, through the coherent superposition of its unequally spaced eigenstates. In this paper, we showcase phononic frequency comb generation in van der Waals heterostructures, formed by stacking two-dimensional layers with a controlled interlayer twist. These materials present themselves as interesting candidates for the realization of internal resonance [15, 16]. The moiré superlattice folds the phonon dispersion of the constituent monolayers into a arXiv:2610.13825v1 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci] 9 Oct 2026 2reduced Brillouin zone, multiplying the number of opti-cal branches and thereby the number of mode pairs avail-able to satisfy ω1 ≈ 2ω2; the formation of the moir’e su-perlattice introduces interlayer shear and layer-breathing modes that are absent in the constituent monolayers, with their frequencies evolving systematically with the interlayer twist. [17, 18, 37–39]. In the twisted bilayer, four-phonon processes become the dominant decay chan-nel, in contrast to the three-phonon processes that domi-nate in the monolayer. This pronounced enhancement of anharmonicity has been attributed directly to the inter-layer coupling introduced by the twist [19]. Previously, Zalalutdinov et al. demonstrated fre-quency comb generation in acoustic cavities formed by MoS 2/h-BN heterostructures operating between 50 and 600 GHz with f × Q product in the order of 10 14 [20]. A molecular dynamics and continuum study of twisted bilayer MoS 2 nanomechanical resonators also showcased that the twist induces a 2 : 1 internal resonance through mode splitting from the anisotropic interlayer potential, and reported combs with their tooth spacing tunable by drive amplitude [21]. While this work establishes phononic frequency-comb generation in a twisted bilayer, the nonlinear coupling coefficients are not derived di-rectly from the underlying electronic structure; instead, the classical interatomic potential employed in the anal-ysis is fitted to available data rather than derived from first-principles calculations. In this paper, we compute the complete parameter set for two twisted bilayer heterostructures, MoS 2/WSe 2

and MoS 2/MoSe 2, at the commensurate angle 21 .787 ◦.The mode frequencies follow from the harmonic force constants; the quality factors from the three-phonon linewidths obtained from the third-order constants through the Boltzmann transport equation; and the cou-pling constants κ12 and κ22 from the same third-order tensor projected onto the phonon eigenvectors. We then integrate the coupled two-mode equations for the ex-tracted model parameters. We chose these two materials to answer two simple questions. A frequency comb needs two vibrations whose frequencies are in a 2:1 ratio. First, does a twisted bi-layer produce such a pair naturally? Second, does this depend on what the bilayer is made of, or does it hap-pen for different materials alike? We address this ques-tion by examining two different chemistries at the same interlayer twist angle. Second, once the resonance is es-tablished, what determines the strength of the result-ing comb? MoS 2/WSe 2 and MoS 2/MoSe 2 share a MoS 2

layer and the same twist angle, but differ in whether the second layer changes both the metal and the chalcogen or only the chalcogen. This comparison therefore isolates the role of chemical asymmetry from that of the moir’e geometry. We find that the internal resonance is generic to the twisted-bilayer architecture, whereas the strength of the nonlinear coupling– and consequently the comb– is strongly chemistry dependent.  

> II. COMPUTATIONAL METHODS

The workflow comprises four stages, applied identi-cally to both MoS 2/WSe 2 and MoS 2/MoSe 2: (i) re-laxation of the twisted bilayer at fixed cell parame-ters, followed by a self-consistent ground-state calcu-lation; (ii) calculation of second-order force constants using the finite-displacement method; (iii) calculation of third-order force constants from randomly displaced atomic configurations, followed by evaluation of phonon linewidths and quality factors; and (iv) projection of the third-order force-constant tensor onto the selected mode pair to determine the anharmonic coupling constants en-tering the frequency-comb equations.  

> A. Structures and relaxation

Twisted bilayers were constructed at the com-mensurate angle 21 .787 ◦ [49], which generates a√7 × √7 R21 .787 ◦ moiré supercell on the triangular lat-tice of the transition metal dichalcogenide monolayer. Each cell contains 42 atoms: 14 in the metal sublat-tices and 28 in the chalcogen sublattices, distributed as MoS 2 over WSe 2 in one compound and MoS 2 over MoSe 2

in the other. Starting geometries were taken from the twisted bilayer MoS 2 structures of Klein et al. [51], with the second layer substituted by WSe 2 or MoSe 2. The upper layer is rotated about an axis through a metal site. The cells are periodic along the stacking axis with 

c ≈ 13 Å; they retain space group P 3 (No. 143, point group 3, C3). Removing the force constants that couple each bilayer to its periodic image along c and re-imposing the acoustic sum rule shifts ω1 and ω2 by −0.53% and 

−0.40% in MoS 2/WSe 2 and by −0.14% and −0.19% in MoS 2/MoSe 2, and leaves both pairs within 1.0% of the 

2 : 1 condition, so the contact across the cell boundary has a minimal effect on the comb modes. Density functional theory calculations were performed with VASP [22] using projector-augmented-wave poten-tials [23] and the PBE parameterisation of the gener-alised gradient approximation [24]. The Mo_sv potential (14 valence electrons, including the 4s and 4p semicore shells), W (6 valence electrons), S and Se (6 valence elec-trons each) were used. A plane-wave cutoff of 380 eV was employed throughout, and the Brillouin zone of the moiré cell was sampled with an 11 × 11 × 1 Γ -centred k-mesh; a single kz point is adequate because the coupling across the cell boundary is weak (Sec. II A). Van der Waals interactions were accounted for using Grimme’s DFT-D3 correction [25], which is essential for describing interlayer binding and coupling in layered systems, where these in-teractions influence the mode splitting investigated here. Ionic positions were relaxed at fixed cell shape and vol-ume ( ISIF = 2 ) to preserve the commensurate moiré ge-ometry and twist angle. Allowing the cell to relax could alter the imposed supercell geometry and compromise the controlled comparison between the two heterostructures. 3Fixing the cell while relaxing the internal coordinates allows the interlayer separation and registry-dependent corrugation to equilibrate under the dispersion correc-tion. At small twist angles, such internal relaxation can drive substantial moiré reconstruction, producing com-mensurate domains separated by domain walls [50]. Elec-tronic self-consistency was converged to 10 −7 eV and relaxation continued until all residual forces fell below 

0.01 eV Å −1 (EDIFFG = −0.01 ). A self-consistent calcu-lation on the relaxed structure, at the same cutoff and 

k-mesh, then provided the ground state from which the electronic density of states and all subsequent force cal-culations were started. Spin–orbit coupling (SOC) was neglected in the calcu-lations. Its effect on phonon frequencies in MoS 2-based dichalcogenides is reported to be below 1 % [40]. Since comb generation depends on the frequency ratio ω1/ω 2

rather than on the absolute frequencies, a common-mode frequency shift would largely cancel in this ratio. The third-order force constants, which govern the anharmonic intermode coupling, are also expected to be less sensitive to SOC than the electronic structure itself. The heavier WSe 2 layer represents the more stringent case; we return to this limitation in Sec. IV B.  

> B. Harmonic force constants

Second-order force constants were obtained by the finite-displacement method within VASP ( IBRION = 6 ,

NSW = 1 ), with LEPSILON = .TRUE. so that the Born ef-fective charges and the dielectric tensor were computed in the same run. The resulting force constants were read by phonopy [26] and stored as fc2.hdf5 . The moiré cell it-self was used as the supercell ( 1×1×1). Phonon frequen-cies, eigenvectors and the density of states were evaluated on a 80 ×80 ×1 Γ -centred q-mesh. The mesh is restricted to the plane in order to describe scattering within the bi-layer; sampling qz would add channels between periodic images, which are not part of the bilayer.  

> C. Anharmonic force constants by random displacement

Calculating third-order force constants presents a com-putational challenge. A systematic finite-displacement approach for these supercells would require 27,722 con-figurations, rendering it computationally prohibitive at the chosen level of theory. We therefore used random displacement sampling [27, 28, 41–43]: ten supercell con-figurations per compound were generated with phono3py, with all atoms displaced simultaneously by 0.03 Å in ran-dom directions, and the forces on each configuration were computed with VASP at the same settings as above. The third-order tensor was obtained by fitting these forces, with the harmonic part fixed to fc2.hdf5 , and written as fc3.hdf5 for use by phono3py in the linewidth calcu-lation below. Because the ten configurations provide fewer indepen-dent equations than there are independent tensor com-ponents, the fitting problem is underdetermined and re-quires regularisation. We minimise

> B. Mode pairs and parametric resonance

The 42-atom cell carries 126 phonon branches extend-ing to ≈ 14 THz, and the scan of Sec. II E returns 60 pairs in MoS 2/WSe 2 and 48 in MoS 2/MoSe 2 within the 

0.05 THz detuning window. The abundance of mode pairs satisfying the resonance condition is itself a key re-sult, arising from the moiré geometry rather than the chemical composition. Folding the monolayer disper-sion into the √7 × √7 R21 .787 ◦ Brillouin zone increases the number of phonon branches sevenfold. Thus, despite its slightly weaker coupling, MoS 2/MoSe 2 has the lower absolute threshold, indicating that differences in damping outweigh the modest difference in coupling strength.  

> C. Comb spectra

Figure 2 compares the two compounds at the same multiple of their respective thresholds, f0 = 1186 fth ,MoS 2/WSe 2 in (a) and MoS 2/MoSe 2 in (b). In both, the spectrum of the driven mode carries a set of equidistant lines about ω1, and the signal mode carries the match-ing ladder about ω2. The time domain shows the en-velope modulation characteristic of several teeth beating against one another. The resolved spectra in the lower panels show the individual teeth. In MoS 2/WSe 2 the teeth are spaced by ≈ 77 GHz, and the same spacing appears about ω1 and about ω2, as required for two lad-ders generated by the same slow-flow beat. The envelope period in the time domain is the inverse of this spac-ing. MoS 2/MoSe 2 follows the same behaviour with fewer teeth, and its teeth are more widely spaced, ≈ 107 GHz, as the resolved spectra show directly.  

> D. Drive sweep and the character of the onset

Figure 3 resolves the transition. Three quantities are plotted against drive: the root-mean-square amplitude of the signal mode, the tooth count, and the amplitude of the driven mode. All three change discontinuously at the same drive. Within an increment of the sweep, from 

696 fth to 701 fth , the envelope variance rises from 2.1 ×

10 3 to 1.37 × 10 4, the tooth count increases from 7 to 16 in mode 1 and from 7 to 17 in mode 2, and the amplitude of the driven-mode saturates abruptly and then declines as energy is transferred to the comb. The simultaneous discontinuity in three independent observables identifies the onset as subcritical: the trivial solution and a finite-amplitude comb solution coexist over a range of drive, and the system jumps between them when the trivial branch loses stability. This is consistent with the negative effective nonlinearity identified by Qi et al. [6] as the condition for a hard transition. 7

Beyond a critical drive, the comb ceases: at 1.0021 × 10 4 fth the tooth count returns toward unity and the driven-mode amplitude falls to a constant level. The driven mode has saturated to the point that the modulation depth available to Eq. (8) no longer sustains parametric growth against the damping. The entropy maximum therefore precedes comb forma-tion rather than coinciding with it, and the two diagnos-tics exhibit an anticorrelated response across the transi-tion: the comb is characterised by large-amplitude mod-ulation and low spectral complexity, whereas the most complex state occurs in the disordered regime immedi-ately below onset. The same ordering is observed in MoS 2/MoSe 2. Its entropy peaks at 2.9287 at 781 , f th ,where the variance is 6.4 × 10 3, before the comb emerges at 991 , f th . Above onset, the entropy decreases and re-mains between 0.69 and 1.96 , consistent with the transi-tion from a spectrally disordered state to a more regular comb. These findings have direct implications for using the system as a physical reservoir. Effective reservoir com-puting requires both a rich transformation of the input, reflected here in the spectral entropy, and sufficient dy-namical memory, for which the envelope variance serves as a proxy. These properties peak in different operating regimes. The comb plateau offers strong amplitude mod-ulation but relatively low spectral complexity, whereas the low-drive regime provides neither property reliably. The most promising operating window therefore lies be-tween the entropy maximum and comb onset, where spec-tral complexity remains high while the variance is already increasing: approximately 341 –701 , f th in MoS 2/WSe 2

and 781 –991 , f th in MoS 2/MoSe 2. This window lies be-low the comb-forming transition, contrary to what the comb spectra alone might suggest, but consistent with the broader observation that recurrent networks can per-form best near the boundary between ordered and chaotic dynamics [48]. These operating windows are candidate regimes for reservoir computing; their computational utility would need to be established through direct bench-marking of memory capacity and task performance.   

> IV. DISCUSSION A. What the twist and chemistry supplies

The two compounds MoS 2/WSe 2 and MoS 2/MoSe 2 al-low the effects of moiré geometry to be distinguished from those of chemical composition, since they share the same twist angle and one constituent layer. In both sys-tems, the resonance condition is readily satisfied: more than 40 mode pairs lie within 0.05 THz of ω1 = 2 ω2,and the best pair in each compound deviates from exact resonance by less than 0.8% . This abundance of near-resonant pairs arises from the sevenfold increase in the number of phonon branches due to √7 × √7 R21 .787 ◦

zone folding and is largely insensitive to the chemical identity of the second layer. The nature of the comb differs between the two com-pounds. Table II makes the comparison. When measured relative to their respective thresholds, MoS 2/WSe 2 ex-hibits an earlier comb onset at 701 , f th , compared with 

991 , f th for MoS 2/MoSe 2, and supports more comb teeth, reaching 22 versus 12. Conversely, MoS 2/MoSe 2 attains the higher peak spectral entropy ( 2.9287 versus 2.6941 ), and its comb persists to the end of the drive sweep. These differences are too pronounced to be explained by the modest factor of 1.20 in κ12 alone. Instead, they are con-sistent with the quality-factor hierarchy established in Sec. III B: the signal-mode damping, which differs by a factor of 5.7, governs how many successive cascade steps can be sustained before the resulting sidebands fall below the detection threshold. Why the coupling should be somewhat larger in MoS 2/WSe 2 follows from what κ12 measures. Equa-tion (5) weights the third-order force constants by the product of three eigenvector components, so it is large when the two modes have substantial amplitude on the same atoms and the anharmonicity there is strong. Re-placing Mo by W and S by Se changes both the mass and the bonding of the second layer, so the modes of the two layers differ in frequency and the ones that do hy-bridise do so over a region where the interlayer potential is strongly anharmonic. > B. Limitations

The quality factors are the least certain quantities reported here. With only ten random configurations, the third-order force constants are weakly constrained. Since the scattering rate in Eq. (3) depends on |V (3) |2,an underdetermined fit may underestimate Γ and con-sequently overestimate (Q). The threshold fth scales as 

1/(Q1Q2); thus, overestimated quality factors lead to an artificially low absolute threshold. However, the drive sweep is expressed in units of fth , eliminating the over-all threshold scale from the comparison. The sequence of transitions within this normalized sweep—including the entropy maximum preceding comb onset, the discon-tinuity at onset, the plateau, and the eventual extinc-tion—depends on (Q) through the damping relative to the detuning. Larger damping would therefore shift the drive range over which the comb persists. The compar-ison between compounds is less sensitive to this uncer-tainty because both are treated using the same computa-tional protocol. Increasing the number of random config-urations is the most direct way to improve the reliability of the quality factors. The coupling constants are better determined than the linewidths, as they are single tensor elements con-tracted with eigenvectors rather than a sum over the whole Brillouin zone. Nevertheless, they inherit the same regularisation bias and should be interpreted as order-of-magnitude estimates rather than quantities known to a precisely specified accuracy. Spin–orbit coupling (SOC) was neglected. This ap-proximation is well justified for M oS 2/M oSe 2, but is less certain for M oS 2/W Se 2, where the tungsten-containing layer may introduce percent-level shifts in optical phonon frequencies. Because comb formation depends on the frequency ratio ω1/ω 2, a common-mode frequency shift cancels, leaving only differential shifts relevant to the resonance condition. We expect the resulting change in ω1/ω 2 to be smaller than the existing detuning of (0.77 %), although this has not been verified. Including SOC is therefore a priority for future calculations, partic-ularly for heterostructures containing heavier elements. Finally, the dynamics are described by a two-mode model. The 42-atom unit cell supports 126 phonon modes, and the scan in Sec. III B identifies more than forty near-resonant mode pairs in each compound. Whether these additional pairs remain spectators over the drive range considered or support competing combs lies beyond the scope of the two-mode reduction. The tooth-count fluctuations observed across the plateau in Fig. 3 are consistent with a two-mode system sampling nearby states, but could also indicate weak coupling to a third mode.  

> V. CONCLUSIONS

We have computed phononic frequency combs in twisted bilayer transition metal dichalcogenide het-erostructures from density functional theory, obtaining the mode frequencies, quality factors and anharmonic coupling constants from the harmonic and third-order force constants from first-principles. Both MoS 2/WSe 2

and MoS 2/MoSe 2 at 21 .787 ◦ generate combs. In MoS 2/WSe 2 the tooth count, the signal-mode variance and the driven-mode amplitude all change abruptly within a single sweep increment at 701 fth , which identi-fies the onset as subcritical rather than supercritical. The spectral entropy peaks immediately before comb formation. For reservoir computing, this suggests that the most productive operating regime lies just below the comb threshold, where the spectral complexity remains high while the oscillation amplitude is already increasing, rather than within the comb state itself. The two compounds exhibit comparable coupling strengths, with κ12 differing by a factor of 1.20, yet their comb dynamics differ substantially. MoS 2/WSe 2 exhibits a lower comb excitation threshold and sustains a larger number of teeth, whereas MoS 2/MoSe 2 achieves a higher peak spectral entropy and maintains its comb even for larger drives. Any commensurate, non-centrosymmetric twist in a van der Waals bilayer can be screened using this approach before device fabrication. The screening criteria comprise two distinct ingredients: the twist geometry determines the availability of near-resonant modes, while their life-times govern the drive required to exploit that resonance. 12
