## ABSTRACT 

Verifier-guided decoding can prevent harmful reasoning steps from contaminating subsequent generation, but typically relies on an external learned verifier. Can a language model instead learn to reject its own bad reasoning steps? This requires distinguishing candidate steps sampled from the same problem and reasoning prefix. We define a prefix’s recoverability as the probability that the frozen generator can complete it correctly. Diagnostic measurements show that most adjacent changes in recoverability are difficult to resolve with practical Monte Carlo budgets, even when cumulative degradation is detectable. Meanwhile, candidates sampled from a shared prefix exhibit a sparse low-recoverability tail, suggesting an opportunity for selective rejection. We introduce S ELF -S TEP REJECTION (SSR), which trains a lightweight LoRA acceptance gate on the generator’s own backbone while keeping the base model frozen. SSR uses confidence-qualified first-passage supervision :steps before the first resolved crossing of a root-relative recoverability barrier are labeled as accepted, the crossing step is labeled as rejected, and unresolved steps and the subsequent suffix are excluded from supervision. Training combines pointwise classification with same-prefix pairwise learning, followed by group-relative policy refinement using final-answer correctness. At inference, the gate accepts candidates or resamples from the unchanged prefix, subject to a per-position attempt cap and an episode-level rejection budget, without an external learned verifier. Across three reasoning models and five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, SSR improves macro-average accuracy over single-pass decoding by 5.4–10.1 percentage points using 1.21–1.40 × as many generated tokens, achieving the highest macro-average accuracy among the evaluated step-level methods for each generator, while full-solution scaling methods require 4.47–8.27 × the single-pass token cost for comparable performance. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reasoning language models benefit from additional inference-time computation, but longer reasoning traces also create more opportunities to commit harmful intermediate steps (Wei et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025; Muennighoff et al., 2025). Once a step is appended, subsequent generation is conditioned on it. A model may recover through reflection, but it may instead elaborate a plausible yet incorrect premise, reducing the chance of eventually reaching the correct answer. A natural response is to verify each step before committing it. Process reward models score interme-diate reasoning (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), and verifier-guided decoders can reject low-scoring steps and sample alternatives (Uscidda et al., 2025). These approaches establish the utility of step-level intervention when a capable verifier is available. However, an external verifier may introduce additional model capacity or supervision from stronger teachers, leaving open whether the generator itself can learn to make these decisions. We therefore ask: Given a reasoning prefix and one newly sampled step, can a language model decide whether to commit that step or reject it and sample again? 

We refer to this task as same-prefix candidate rejection , which requires distinguishing candidates sampled from the same problem and committed prefix. Global verification metrics may reward recognition of problem difficulty, trajectory depth, or accumulated errors without measuring this ability. We therefore train and evaluate candidate discrimination within shared prefixes. A natural supervision signal is the change in recoverability : the probability that the frozen generator, continued from a prefix, eventually produces a correct answer. Yet adjacent changes in recoverability are difficult to estimate reliably because they involve differences between noisy Monte Carlo success rates. Across three reasoning models and five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, only 2.3–24.0% of adjacent recoverability differences are resolved with 16 rollouts per endpoint. Cumulative degradation can nevertheless become detectable even when individual transitions remain unresolved. This motivates supervision based on cumulative degradation relative to the problem’s initial recoverability. Cumulative degradation alone does not establish that resampling is useful. We therefore also examine candidates sampled from shared prefixes. These candidates exhibit variation beyond Monte Carlo noise, with a small low-recoverability tail. This suggests a selective intervention: accept most candidates immediately and spend additional computation on replacing the few that are likely to harm the continuation. Our method, S ELF -S TEP REJECTION (SSR), freezes the generator and trains a lightweight LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) acceptance gate on the same backbone. It uses confidence-qualified first-passage supervision relative to the problem’s initial recoverability: confidently acceptable steps before the first resolved crossing are labeled as accepted, the crossing step is labeled as rejected, and unresolved steps and the subsequent suffix are excluded from supervision. Training combines pointwise classification with same-prefix pairwise learning, directly encouraging the gate to distinguish accepted and rejected candidates that share the same state. We then refine the gate with a within-problem group-relative objective using final-answer correctness. At inference, the adapter is enabled only for acceptance scoring. The decoder commits accepted candidates and resamples rejected ones from the unchanged prefix, subject to a per-position attempt cap and an episode-level rejection budget. Additional sampling is therefore selective and requires no external learned verifier. Across three reasoning models and five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, SSR improves macro-average accuracy over single-pass decoding by 5.4–10.1 percentage points using 1.21–1.40 × as many generated tokens. It achieves the highest macro-average accuracy among the evaluated step-level methods for each generator and remains competitive with full-solution scaling methods, which use 4.47–8.27 × the single-pass token cost. Figure 1 summarizes this accuracy–cost trade-off. This sparse low-value tail motivates selectively rejecting candidates while accepting most candidates without additional sampling. Together with measurable cumulative degradation, this supports training the acceptance gate with root-relative first-passage labels.

At inference time, the base model samples one candidate at a time. The acceptance gate commits the candidate when score ϕ(xt) ≥ η. Each position receives at most M candidate attempts. Rejected candidates are discarded and resampled from the unchanged parent prefix until either a candidate is accepted or the attempt cap is reached; in the latter case, the decoder commits the highest-scoring attempt. Across the episode, at most B candidates may be rejected. Once this budget is exhausted, subsequent candidates are committed directly. SSR generates additional candidates only after a rejection, allocating extra computation selectively within the prescribed budgets. Step-level best-of-K,in comparison, incurs the cost of sampling K candidates at every decision. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 SETUP 

We study Qwen3-14B (Yang et al., 2025), DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (Guo et al., 2025), and gpt-oss-20b (Agarwal et al., 2025) as frozen reasoning generators. Our primary evaluation suite contains contest-mathematics problems from OlymMATH-Easy and OlymMATH-Hard (Sun et al., 2026a), together with AIME 2025, HMMT February 2025, and HMMT February 2026 from MathArena (Dekoninck et al., 2026). We train the acceptance gate on a filtered subset of Omni-MATH (Gao et al., 2025), decontaminated against all evaluation benchmarks, and reserve a separate subset for development. We compare SSR with the following baselines. Single pass is the no-scaling reference, while Majority@8 (Wang et al., 2022) and ESC ( w = 2 ) (Li et al., 2024) represent fixed-budget and adaptive self-consistency. We design fixed-early resampling, low-confidence resampling, and random rejection as controls for intervention timing and candidate selection. Budget forcing (Muennighoff et al., 2025) tests the effect of extending generation. LATTS (Uscidda et al., 2025) provides an external-verifier step-rejection baseline. 4.2 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Unless otherwise specified, all generators use a 32K context window and a per-step limit of Lstep =2048 new tokens, reduced when context space is insufficient. Each generator has a separate rank-16 LoRA gate on the query, output, and MLP projections, with key and value projections unchanged. The adapter is enabled only for scoring, using the frozen language-modeling head’s +/- logits at a fixed marker. Training combines first-passage supervision with group-relative policy refinement. Inference allows M = 8 candidate attempts per position and B = 12 rejections per episode. A threshold η, calibrated before refinement to a target rejection rate of 17.8%, remains fixed during refinement and inference. Problem characterization. Table 2 shows that only 2.3–24.0% of adjacent differences are resolved with 16 rollouts per endpoint, despite nonzero within-prefix variation across all three models. This suggests that noisy local estimates can obscure useful candidate differences, motivating supervision based on cumulative degradation. Meanwhile, only 6.0–11.5% of candidates occupy the low-value tail, supporting selective rejection while preserving most of the trajectory. 

Same-prefix gate analysis. Figure 5 shows that strong global discrimination does not necessarily translate into effective within-prefix selection, where problem difficulty and prefix depth cannot distinguish candidates. SSR’s stronger local discrimination is consistent with its pairwise training objective. Policy refinement raises accepted-candidate value lift from 0.094 to 0.121 with modest changes in discrimination metrics, suggesting improvements in acceptance decisions beyond offline ranking. 

Training-component ablations. Table 3 shows that labeling all below-barrier steps reduces accu-racy by 7.5 points and increases token cost, consistent with unnecessary rejection of steps that inherit earlier degradation. Removing pairwise learning costs 6.1 points, supporting direct supervision of local candidate preferences. Omitting policy refinement costs 4.6 points, suggesting that candi-8Preprint date discrimination benefits from further optimization for final-answer correctness under sequential decoding. 

Sensitivity analysis. On Qwen3-14B (Appendix D, Tables 6 and 7), accuracy plateaus at target rejection rates of 17.8–30.0% while token cost rises, indicating diminishing returns from additional rejection. Step sizes of 1,024–4,096 tokens achieve similar accuracy, whereas shorter and longer steps perform worse. Short steps increase gate decisions, while long steps delay intervention, suggesting a balance between decision frequency and intervention timing. The default Lstep = 2048 uses the fewest tokens within this accuracy plateau. 

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Inference-time scaling and search. Sampling multiple complete solutions and aggregating their answers improves reasoning robustness (Wang et al., 2022; Cobbe et al., 2021). Tree-structured and agentic methods instead branch at intermediate states and use search, learned values, or structured subroutines to allocate computation (Yao et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026; 2021b;a). Adaptive methods control different aspects of this computation: ESC adjusts the number of sampled solutions through early stopping (Li et al., 2024), while s1 uses budget forcing to control the length of a single reasoning trace (Muennighoff et al., 2025). The effectiveness of scaling strategies also depends on problem difficulty and context quality, motivating adaptive allocation or selection tailored to individual problems (Snell et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026b). Multi-agent coordination and collaborative agent frameworks provide other ways to guide reasoning (Zhang et al., 2026d;a; Huang et al., 2026). We study allocation within a single decoding trajectory: the gate decides which candidates warrant replacement before commitment, concentrating extra generation on selected positions while preserving the accepted prefix. 

Process supervision and verifier-guided decoding. Process reward models assign scores to intermediate reasoning steps (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Rollout-based supervision estimates successful completion from a prefix, reducing manual annotation while depending on the rollout policy and sample count (Xiong et al., 2026; 2025a). OmegaPRM lowers labeling cost by searching for the first error under an approximate monotonicity assumption (Luo et al., 2024), while Process Advantage Verifiers assess action-induced progress from a shared state (Setlur et al., 2025). Recoverability and calibrated uncertainty have also been used to guide policy optimization (Bao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b;c). For inference-time selection, Tree-PLV learns from same-prefix rollout preferences to guide tree search (He et al., 2024), whereas LATTS uses an external PRM or LLM critic to reject steps and trigger resampling (Uscidda et al., 2025). Our measurements motivate first-passage supervision when adjacent value changes are difficult to resolve but cumulative degradation is detectable. Building on verifier-guided decoding, we focus on learning a policy-matched acceptance gate from first-passage supervision and evaluating its ability to distinguish same-prefix candidates. 

Self-evaluation and self-verification. Model representations and generated reasoning traces can expose signals useful for verification and responsible reasoning evaluation (Burns et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2026c). Self-Refine uses the same model to generate feedback and iteratively revise its output without additional training (Madaan et al., 2023), although intrinsic self-correction without external feedback can fail to improve reasoning (Huang et al., 2024). Training-based approaches include SCoRe, which learns answer revision through multi-turn reinforcement learning (Kumar et al., 2025), and S 2R, which trains explicit self-verification and correction behaviors (Ma et al., 2025). Related work also explores reflection, self-evolving skills, and agent–environment co-evolution (Wang, 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). Logical grounding and structure-based validation are studied in related reasoning and planning settings (Xiong et al., 2025b; Bao et al., 2026a;b; Lu et al., 2025; 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). S ELF -S TEP REJECTION instead formulates same-prefix candidate rejection: a learned gate evaluates a candidate before commitment and, after rejection, resamples from the unchanged prefix. We introduce first-passage supervision for this acceptance gate, trained as a lightweight adapter while keeping the generator frozen. 9Preprint 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented S ELF -S TEP REJECTION , which trains a lightweight acceptance gate on the generator’s own backbone to reject low-value candidate steps without an external learned verifier. The method combines root-relative first-passage supervision, same-prefix pairwise learning, and policy refinement while keeping the generator frozen. Across three reasoning models and five mathematics benchmarks, SSR improves macro-average accuracy over single-pass decoding by 5.4–10.1 percentage points using 1.21–1.40 × as many generated tokens, achieving the highest macro-average accuracy among evaluated step-level methods for each generator. These results demonstrate the value of learning same-prefix candidate discrimination to guide selective resampling and improve the accuracy–token trade-off. AI USE STATEMENT

## A SELF -S TEP REJECTION 

A.1 FIRST -P ASSAGE LABELING 

First-passage labeling converts trajectory-level value estimates into step-level accept/reject super-vision. For a trajectory p0, . . . , p T , let bVbarrier = (1 − τ ) bV0 denote the root-relative barrier and define gap t = bVbarrier − bVt. The first confidence-resolved step with gap t > 0 is labeled REJECT ;all preceding confidence-resolved steps are labeled ACCEPT . The procedure then terminates and assigns no labels to the remaining suffix. This restriction associates the negative label with the first confidence-resolved crossing of the root-relative barrier, avoiding repeated attribution of inherited damage to later steps. We evaluate dense labeling of every below-barrier step as an ablation. At each prefix, the rollout count is increased only while the confidence interval for gap t intersects zero. An interval lying strictly below zero yields an ACCEPT label, whereas an interval lying strictly above zero yields the first REJECT label and terminates the scan. Prefixes that remain unresolved at Kmax are marked gray and excluded from the supervised objective. This abstention rule avoids converting Monte Carlo ties into arbitrary labels. If bV0 = 0 , the relative barrier is also zero and contains no information about τ ; Algorithm 1 therefore returns no labels. We likewise exclude candidate sets for which every evaluated continuation has zero value, because such sets contain no within-prefix signal for selecting one step over another. Labels are taken from the confidence-qualified decisions recorded during data collection rather than recomputed from the stored value estimates. Adaptive stopping changes the conditional distribution of the stored estimates, but not the recorded decision or committed candidate. The resulting first-passage data are positive-heavy, so we report an always-accept gate as the explicit no-intervention baseline. The relative barrier scales the tolerated degradation with each problem’s recoverability. A fixed absolute margin cannot trigger when the root estimate is below that margin. The root estimate is shared by all trajectories of a problem and can be computed once. A.2 GROUP -R ELATIVE POLICY REFINEMENT 

Policy refinement samples G complete decoding episodes for each problem and uses final-answer correctness as the terminal reward. Gate actions are sampled according to Equation 9. The within-problem group mean provides a parameter-free baseline that reduces the influence of problem diffi-culty without requiring a learned value function. Rewards are terminal and returns are undiscounted, so all decisions in an episode share the group-centered advantage in Equation 10. We center rewards by the group mean without standardizing by the group standard deviation. For binary rewards, the standard deviation is fully determined by the empirical success rate, pbμ(1 − bμ).With G = 8 , standardization would give a group with bμ = 1 /8 approximately 1.5× the group-level scaling of a balanced group with bμ = 4 /8, emphasizing a near-degenerate group whose baseline is estimated from a single successful episode. Mean centering preserves the observed reward scale, and constant-outcome groups naturally contribute zero advantages. Algorithm 2 summarizes the update. A.3 SEQUENTIAL ACCEPT -OR -R ESAMPLE DECODING 

At inference time, S ELF -S TEP REJECTION applies a sequential rejection policy at each committed prefix. The generator first samples a single candidate step, which is committed when its acceptance score is at least η. Otherwise, the candidate is discarded and a replacement is sampled from the unchanged prefix, with at most M total candidate attempts at each position. Generation and acceptance scoring share the same backbone, with the acceptance-gate adapter activated only for scoring (Appendix C). During label collection, each evaluated prefix permits at most M = 8 candidate attempts. Candidate continuation values are estimated within the remaining context, 

Lavailable = Lctx − tokens( q, p t). (12) The resulting confidence-qualified decisions are converted into labels using the first-passage rule in Appendix A.1. A smaller generation cap is treated as an explicit budgeted condition rather than as the model’s context limit. For reproducibility, each estimate retains its decoding configuration, seed, token counts, extracted answer, correctness, finish reason, and exact continuation or cutoff prefix. C.2 GATE ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING 

Input serialization. The acceptance-gate input concatenates the problem, committed prefix, candi-date step, and a fixed marker token. The dynamically adjusted continuation budget keeps the problem, prefix, and candidate within Lctx . The marker is used only for scoring and is excluded from the generation-token budget. We use the pretrained reserved token <|fim pad|> as the marker and require that it not occur in any input segment. The marker is appended at the final input position. 

Token-based readout. The frozen language-modeling head is evaluated at the marker position. Following the token-based readout of Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024), the existing single-token vocabulary entries + and - verbalize ACCEPT and REJECT , respectively. If their logits are ℓ+ and ℓ−,training uses the logit difference z = ℓ+ − ℓ− with binary cross entropy with logits, while inference uses the equivalent two-token softmax score score = exp( ℓ+)/[exp( ℓ+) + exp( ℓ−)] . The marker embedding, vocabulary embeddings, and language-modeling head remain frozen; only the LoRA adapter is trained. Reusing the pretrained language-modeling head provides a stronger inductive bias under limited gate supervision, for which a newly initialized classification head readily overfits. 

LoRA configuration. The only trainable parameters are rank-16 LoRA adapters with scaling factor 32, zero dropout, and no bias. They are attached in every transformer layer to q proj , o proj ,

gate proj , up proj , and down proj . The key and value projections remain frozen. Generation and acceptance scoring maintain separate incremental key–value caches over the committed prefix. Training, development, and test partitions are disjoint at the problem level. We optimize with AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) for three epochs at learning rate 3 × 10 −4, effective batch size eight, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Class-dependent weighting addresses the imbalance in first-passage labels. For same-prefix accept/reject pairs, the pointwise losses are augmented with 

λpair softplus[ −(za −zr )] , with λpair = 0 .5 and softplus( x) = log(1+exp x). This smooth ranking loss encourages a higher logit for the accepted candidate. Checkpoint selection uses within-prefix discrimination and accepted-value lift. C.3 POLICY -R EFINEMENT CONFIGURATION 

Policy refinement uses G = 8 episodes per problem and temperature = 1 .0. We set the target rejection rate to 17.8%, based on the rate measured under oracle-gated decoding. Before refinement, we calibrate η to the corresponding empirical quantile of the initial adapter’s on-policy acceptance scores. The resulting threshold remains fixed throughout policy refinement and deployment; the realized rejection rate is reported separately in Table 4. Each iteration draws 16 fresh training problems, sampling without replacement until the pool is exhausted and then reshuffling it. The resulting groups are combined into an effective batch of 256 gate decisions, and one AdamW update is applied at learning rate 5×10 −5, zero weight decay, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Generation uses temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95 for Qwen3-14B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, and temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, and reasoning effort=medium for gpt-oss-20b. D.1 POWER ANALYSIS FOR LOCAL VALUE DIFFERENCES 

For two Bernoulli proportions near success probability μ, the approximate number of rollouts per endpoint required to resolve an effect δ with 80% power is n ≈ 2(1 .96 + 0 .84) 2μ(1 − μ)/δ 2. Using the magnitude of the mean adjacent change rounded to δ = 0 .05 , the worst-case μ = 0 .5 calculation gives approximately 1,600 rollouts per endpoint. D.2 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS 

All sensitivity results in this subsection are measured with Qwen3-14B. 

Target rejection rate. Table 6 varies the target rejection rate and calibrates η for each operating point. Accuracy improves as the gate is allowed to reject more candidates, then plateaus across target rates from 17.8% to 30.0% while token cost continues to increase. The default target of 17.8% lies near the start of this plateau. 

Step size. Table 7 varies Lstep with the target rejection rate fixed at 17.8%. Very short steps create many decision points and increase scoring and resampling cost. Very long steps delay intervention and yield lower accuracy without reducing generated-token cost below the 2048-token setting. Step sizes of 1,024–4,096 tokens achieve similar accuracy, with the default Lstep = 2048 using the fewest generated tokens in this range. D.3 EXAMPLES
