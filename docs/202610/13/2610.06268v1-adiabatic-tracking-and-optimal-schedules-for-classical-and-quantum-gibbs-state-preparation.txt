We develop a discrete adiabatic framework for Gibbs-state preparation in classical and quan-tum optimisation algorithms. For finite time-inhomogeneous reversible Markov chains, we derive a tracking theorem that bounds the distance from the evolving distribution to the instantaneous stationary state by combining two effects: contraction of previously accumulated error by the Markov dynamics and the new error generated as the stationary distribution changes from one step to the next. For Gibbs paths, this leads to explicit step-complexity bounds and to gap-and fluctuation-adapted schedules controlled by the Markov-chain spectral gap and the energy-fluctuation scale V (β) = 

q

Var πβ (E). In the weak-damping regime, the resulting schedule takes the form ˙β ∝ δ(β)/V (β). We then apply the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem to temperature-dependent Szegedy quantum walks. The quantum-walk eigenphase gap satisfies ∆ w = Θ( √δ), where δ is the spectral gap of the underlying Markov chain. By analysing the finite-difference terms entering the theorem, we show that this square-root spectral amplification translates into a quadratic improvement in the gap dependence only for suitably gap-adapted schedules. Under the corresponding regularity conditions, the quantum adiabatic cost scales as O(δ−1/2min ), compared with the classical O(δ−1min ) relaxation scale.

> I. INTRODUCTION

The adiabatic theorem describes the evolution of a physical system whose governing dynamics vary slowly in time. If the system is initially prepared in an eigenstate of an operator that changes continuously along a path, the theorem provides conditions under which the evolved state remains close to the corresponding instantaneous eigenstate. More refined formulations quantify the required slowness in terms of spectral properties of the generator, in particular the gap separating the eigenvalue of interest from the remainder of the spectrum. In general, a smaller spectral gap requires a slower evolution. In quantum mechanics, the adiabatic theorem is commonly formulated for a family of Hamiltonians H(s), with 

s ∈ [0 , 1] parametrizing the evolution [1–3]. If |n(si)⟩ is an instantaneous eigenstate of H(si) with eigenvalue En(si), the theorem bounds how closely the evolved state follows the corresponding eigenstate |n(s)⟩ as the parameter is varied from si to sf . A central quantity is the instantaneous spectral gap separating En(s) from the rest of the spectrum, together with the rate at which the Hamiltonian changes. For an interpolation between two fixed Hamiltonians, 

H(s) = [1 − f (s)] H0 + f (s)H1, the choice of the schedule f (s) can therefore be used to slow the evolution in regions where the gap becomes small. More recently, adiabatic theorems have also been formulated directly for families of unitary operators rather than Hamiltonians [4, 5]. This formulation is particularly natural for quantum computation, where the evolution is implemented through discrete circuits or parameter-dependent quantum walks. A closely related problem arises for a family of finite Markov kernels {W s}s∈[0 ,1] with stationary distributions 

{πs}s∈[0 ,1] , satisfying Wsπs = πs. In analogy with the quantum adiabatic setting, one may ask whether an initial stationary distribution πsi can be transported along the path so as to remain close to the instantaneous stationary distribution and ultimately prepare πsf . The analogy relates to the spectral properties: πs is the eigenvector of Ws

associated with the dominant eigenvalue 1. For irreducible and aperiodic kernels this eigenvalue is simple, and the spectral gap controls the decay of deviations away from stationarity. For a fixed Markov kernel, the standard notion characterizing convergence to equilibrium is the mixing time. Given a kernel Wsf with stationary distribution πsf , mixing-time bounds estimate how many repeated applications of Wsf

are required to bring an arbitrary initial distribution within a prescribed accuracy ϵ of πsf , typically in total variation distance [6–8]. For reversible chains, these bounds are governed by the inverse spectral gap together with a measure of the initial distance from equilibrium. They are, however, fixed-kernel estimates. They neither determine how the parameter s should be varied along a family {W s} nor exploit the possibility of remaining close to the moving stationary distribution throughout the evolution. In particular, if the initial and final stationary distributions are far apart, directly applying only the final kernel Wsf may lead to unnecessarily pessimistic bounds. This motivates a pathwise formulation closer in spirit to the quantum adiabatic theorem: rather than repeatedly applying only the final kernel, one applies a sequence of slowly varying Markov kernels and asks how accurately the evolving distribution tracks the instantaneous stationary distribution. Adiabatic results for time-inhomogeneous 3Markov chains have previously been developed in Refs. [9, 10]. These results establish convergence to the final stationary distribution, but do not exploit the full local spectral information along the path. In particular, the resulting adiabatic times can exhibit quadratic inverse-gap dependence and do not directly provide a local schedule adapted simultaneously to the variation of the stationary distribution and to the instantaneous contraction rate. Simulated annealing provides a particularly important setting in which such a pathwise analysis is relevant. In simu-lated annealing, Markov-chain Monte Carlo dynamics are used to approximately prepare Gibbs distributions while the inverse temperature is gradually increased. Operationally, one applies a sequence of temperature-dependent Markov kernels, often performing several updates at one temperature before changing to the next according to a prescribed annealing schedule. The schedule and the number of updates per temperature are commonly chosen heuristically, without a bound that directly controls the distance between the evolving distribution and the instantaneous Gibbs distribution throughout the annealing path. In this work, we formulate a discrete adiabatic tracking theorem for finite time-inhomogeneous Markov chains that addresses this problem directly. The resulting bound separates the contraction of previously accumulated error from the error generated by the motion of the stationary distribution itself. For reversible chains, this leads to a local description in terms of the instantaneous spectral gap, norm-comparison factors, and the variation of the stationary state. Applied to Gibbs distributions, the latter is governed by energy fluctuations, allowing us to derive annealing schedules adapted both to the thermodynamic variation of the Gibbs state and to the local relaxation properties of the Markov dynamics. We then develop the corresponding quantum perspective using parameter-dependent Szegedy quantum walks. Szegedy’s quantisation of reversible Markov chains maps the spectrum of the classical transition operator to eigen-phases of a unitary quantum walk, leading to a square-root amplification of the spectral gap [11]. This mechanism underlies quantum simulated-annealing algorithms with a quadratic improvement in the Markov-chain gap depen-dence, from O(δ−1) to O(δ−1/2) [12]. Efficient circuit constructions of the corresponding Metropolis–Hastings quantum walk were subsequently developed in Ref. [13] and further improved in [14]. Here, we revisit this quadratic gap ad-vantage from the perspective of the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem [4, 5]. The quantum-walk eigenphase gap satisfies ∆ w = Θ( √δ), where δ is the spectral gap of the underlying reversible Markov chain. We show, however, that this square-root spectral amplification does not by itself imply a quadratic improvement for a temperature-dependent sequence of quantum walks. By explicitly analysing the first and second finite differences entering the discrete adia-batic theorem, we identify gap-adapted schedules for which the spectral amplification is converted into an adiabatic complexity scaling proportional to δ−1/2min . This also provides a mechanism for understanding why the quadratic im-provement need not be observed for generic annealing schedules, like we have in [13, 15] , despite the square-root eigenphase gap of the quantum walk. The paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we summarize the main findings of the work, while in Section III we discuss related classical and quantum approaches to Gibbs-state preparation and annealing. The remainder of the paper is divided into two parts, devoted respectively to the classical and quantum results. We begin with the classical analysis. In Section IV, we review the basic ingredients of the framework, including Markov kernels, stationary distributions, reversibility, spectral gaps, and the weighted norms used throughout the paper. In Section V, we prove the discrete adiabatic tracking theorem for time-inhomogeneous Markov chains and derive the corresponding uniform-gap and effective-damping bounds. In Section VI, we specialize the theorem to Gibbs-state preparation, relate the variation of the stationary distribution to equilibrium energy fluctuations, and derive the associated gap-weighted tracking schedule. In Section VII, we test this schedule numerically on Sherrington–Kirkpatrick instances and compare it with several standard annealing strategies, including schedules motivated by thermodynamic geometry and friction [16]. The second part of the paper develops the quantum counterpart. In Section VIII, we introduce the quantum discrete adiabatic theorem follow by the Szegedy quantum-walk construction in Section IX, where we relate its eigenphase gap to the spectral gap of the underlying Markov chain. We then apply the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem in Section X, by deriving the bounds on the finite differences of the walk and identifying the gap-adapted quantum schedule required to preserve the square-root spectral amplification. We also present numerical results demonstrating the classical–quantum race-to-target scaling in Section XI. Finally, in Section XII, we summarize the implications of the results and discuss open directions.

> III. RELATED WORK
> 1. Classical adiabatic analogous theorems

We begin by reviewing classical adiabatic results for time-inhomogeneous Markov chains. We have [9, 10], general-izing the earlier work in [10], consider a transition kernel that is gradually deformed from an initial kernel Pinitial to a final kernel Pfinal . They define the adiabatic time Tϵ as the minimum number of steps required for the distribution produced by this inhomogeneous evolution to be within total-variation distance ϵ of the stationary distribution πf

of Pfinal , uniformly over the initial distribution. For a linear interpolation between the initial and final kernels, they obtain 

Tϵ ≥ c tmix (ϵ/ 2) 2

ϵ , (13) where tmix is the mixing time of the final kernel. They further extend the analysis to smoother, entry-dependent interpolations and show how the scaling of the adiabatic time depends on the behavior of the interpolation near its endpoint. As an application, they study adiabatically varying Glauber dynamics for the Ising model, for which the stationary measures are Gibbs distributions associated with the instantaneous Hamiltonians. Their results provide a final-time guarantee expressed in terms of the mixing properties of the final Markov kernel. For reversible chains, inserting standard mixing-time estimates gives, up to logarithmic factors and dependence on the smallest stationary probability, a quadratic dependence on the inverse spectral gap of the final kernel. The analysis prescribes a chosen interpolation between the endpoint kernels, but it does not derive a schedule adapted to the instantaneous gaps or to the local variation of the stationary distributions along the interpolation.  

> 2. Thermodynamic geometry and minimum-dissipation protocols

> 3. Quantum adiabatic theorems and quantized Markov chains

The quantum analogue is based on replacing a reversible Markov kernel by a unitary quantum walk whose spectrum encodes that of the classical chain. For a reversible Markov kernel W with eigenvalues 1 = λ0 > λ 1 ≥ λ2 ≥ · · · , (30) the Szegedy construction associates a quantum walk whose nontrivial eigenvalues occur in conjugate pairs e±iθ k , with cos θk = λk, up to convention-dependent factors in the definition of the walk [11]. If δ = 1 − λ1 is the spectral gap of the Markov chain, the corresponding eigenphase gap satisfies ∆w = arccos(1 − δ) = Θ( √δ) (31) for δ ≪ 1. This square-root relation is the spectral origin of the quadratic gap improvement associated with quantized Markov chains. This mechanism was used in quantum simulated annealing by Somma et al. [12]. Their algorithm combines quantum walks with phase-estimation and quantum-Zeno-type procedures and obtains a complexity proportional to 1

√δmin 

, (32) where δmin is the minimum spectral gap of the classical stochastic matrices along the annealing path, compared with the 1 /δ min dependence of the corresponding classical process. Related implementations of Metropolis–Hastings dynamics as quantum walks were developed in Ref. [13]. A different route is provided by discrete quantum adiabatic theorems formulated directly for families of unitary operators. Rather than evolving under a continuous Hamiltonian, one considers a sequence of slowly varying unitaries 

U (sj ) and asks whether an initial eigenstate remains close to the corresponding instantaneous eigenspace throughout the evolution. Such theorems have been used to obtain improved parameter dependence in quantum algorithms by explicitly controlling the finite differences of the unitary operator and its instantaneous eigenphase gap [4, 5]. For temperature-dependent quantum walks associated with reversible Markov chains, the same spectral relation ∆w(β) = Θ

However, in an adiabatic formulation the complexity also depends on the variation of the quantum walk along the path, through the finite-difference quantities entering the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem. The resulting scaling therefore depends not only on the eigenphase gap but also on the choice of interpolation or annealing schedule.

> A. One-Dimensional Tracking Geometry and the Gibbs Schedule

Our goal is now to extract the Gibbs-preparation schedule that follows from the discrete adiabatic tracking theorem in instantaneous weighted norms. We do this from a geometric point of view. We first introduce the one-dimensional Gibbs manifold and then derive the corresponding geodesic equation using the parallel-transport formulation. The schedule is obtained by analysing how the tracking error accumulates under a sequence of Markov operators for the Gibbs path. This error accumulation naturally induces a metric on the Gibbs manifold. Putting these ingredi-ents together, we obtain the affine, constant-metric-speed parametrization of the Gibbs path. This parametrization minimizes the metric-energy functional induced by the local approximation to the tracking bound among monotone schedules with fixed endpoints and duration. > XI. NUMERICAL VALIDATION OF THE CLASSICAL–QUANTUM SCALING

We numerically compare the classical Tracking evolution with the gap-adapted quantum evolution for SK instances with system sizes n = 6 , . . . , 13. For each system size we generate independent 40 different disorder realizations and use the same Hamiltonian instance for the classical and quantum calculations. To allow a consistent comparison across system sizes, the SK couplings are normalized by the conventional factor 1 /√n, while the inverse-temperature interval is fixed to β ∈ [0 , 0.5]. For the classical dynamics, the temperature path is constructed from the Tracking schedule derived above. For the quantum evolution, we use the Szegedy/LHPST walk and construct the schedule directly from the discrete variation of its state-preparation operator. On a preprocessing grid {βi}, the local schedule weight is taken proportional to ∥W (βi+1 ) − W (βi)∥/∆pw,i , with p = 1 .1 and ∆ w,i the local eigenphase gap. This fully discrete construction is particularly useful near the boundary β = 0, where the corresponding smooth derivative of the state-preparation operator need not remain regular. To compare the two evolutions at the same target accuracy, we define the race cost T⋆ as the minimum number of evolution steps for which both the ground-state success ratio and the approximation-energy ratio satisfy qsucc ≥ 0.99 and qAEAR ≥ 0.99, Eq. (120). The reported value at each n is the median race cost over the disorder realizations. The results are shown in Fig. 6, together with the schedule behavior. The finite-size dependence is well described by exponential fits, T⋆ = Ae bn . For the classical Tracking evolution we obtain TC ≃ 161 .8 e0.2555 n, whereas the gap-adapted quantum evolution gives TQ ≃ 47 .5 e0.1267 n. The fitted quantum growth rate is therefore approximately one half of the classical value, bQ/b C ≃ 0.496. This is consistent with the quadratic improvement predicted from the Szegedy spectral mapping, for which the classical 1 /δ dependence is replaced by a quantum 1 /√δ dependence.  

> XII. CONCLUSION

We developed a discrete-adiabatic framework for Gibbs-state preparation that connects classical Markov-chain tracking, thermodynamic schedules, and quantum-walk dynamics. On the classical side, the tracking theorem sep-arates the damping of previously accumulated error from the error injected by changes in the instantaneous Gibbs state. For Gibbs distributions, the latter is governed by V (β) = pVar πβ (E), while relaxation is controlled by the Markov-chain gap. In the weak-damping regime this leads to the tracking schedule ˙β ∝ δ(β)/V (β) and to the worst-case dependence O(δ−1min ). The numerical SK results also show that the minimum gap alone does not determine the finite-instance cost, consistently with the path-dependent structure of the theorem. We also compared the tracking schedule with thermodynamic-friction schedules [16]. Using the approximation of 26 the energy relaxation time by the inverse spectral gap gives ˙β ∝ pδ(β)/V (β). The friction schedule performs well and improves substantially over linear and variance-only annealing, but the tracking schedule typically performs better overall. This is consistent with its construction: it is derived directly from the discrete tracking recursion and balances the injection of error caused by the changing Gibbs distribution against the damping of previously accumulated error by the Markov dynamics. We also constructed an n = 18 schedule by extrapolating the gap behavior from the smaller exactly treated SK instances and estimating the energy fluctuations numerically. The resulting evolution retained the same qualitative advantage of the tracking schedule, providing evidence that the schedule construction remains effective beyond the sizes accessible to full state-space enumeration. On the quantum side, we applied the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem to temperature-dependent Szegedy walks. The Markov-chain gap is mapped to the eigenphase gap ∆ w = arccos(1 − δ) = Θ( √δ), providing the spectral origin of the quadratic improvement. However, this amplification alone is not sufficient. The discrete adiabatic error also depends on the first- and second-order variation of the walk along the path. By deriving explicit bounds on these terms, we showed that the spectral advantage is preserved by gap-adapted schedules satisfying c1(s) ∝ ∆w(s)p, with 1 < p < 2. For a smooth path this corresponds to ˙β ∝ ∆w(β)p/L 1(β). Under the conditions stated in the main text, this yields the worst-case quantum scaling O(∆ −1

> w, min

) = O(δ−1/2min ). This result is consistent with the quadratic gap improvement of [12], which also exploits the Szegedy phase gap to obtain a 1 /√δ dependence, but through phase-estimation and projection-based dynamics. In contrast, the present construction uses a direct sequence of slowly varying quantum walks and derives the improvement from the discrete quantum adiabatic theorem. The analysis also explains why the quadratic advantage was not visible in the earlier simulations [15]. There, the inverse temperature was varied linearly, so the interpolation did not adapt the walk variation to the local eigenphase gap. As a result, the finite-difference terms in the adiabatic bound could kill the spectral amplification. The present work identifies the missing ingredient: the square-root phase-gap advantage must be combined with a compatible gap-adapted schedule. The new SK simulations support this picture. Using a fully discrete quantum schedule constructed from the finite differences of the walk, the race-to-target costs are well fitted by TC ≃ 161 .8 e0.2555 n classically and TQ ≃ 47 .5 e0.1267 n

quantumly. Thus, the numerical results exhibit not only the factor-of-two reduction in the exponential growth rate expected from the square-root eigenphase amplification, but also a substantial separation in the prefactors. The latter is important from a practical perspective, because it produces a sizeable advantage already at the finite system sizes considered here, rather than only in an asymptotic regime. Although a full implementation of the quantum walk introduces additional overheads [14] that are not captured by the step count alone, the simultaneous reduction in both the growth rate and the prefactor suggests that the advantage may remain relevant for practical accessible regimes in future. These results therefore support the central conclusion that a gap-adapted schedule is the ingredient that converts the spectral amplification of the quantized Markov chain into both the predicted quadratic improvement and a significant finite-size reduction in evolution cost. From a practical perspective, constructing the quantum schedule requires estimates or bounds on the local eigen-phase gap and on the variation of the state-preparation unitary. Exact spectral information is not required; conser-vative bounds, like we have done in the classical tracking theorem for n = 18, are sufficient. Developing efficient ways to obtain these quantities for larger optimization problems, and relating the quantum walk variation more directly to thermodynamic observables, remain natural directions for future work.

a. Linear schedule. As a reference, we first consider a uniform interpolation in inverse temperature,

> a. Construction of the SK Markov dynamics
