# The role of root allometry and root overlap in vegetation pattern formation 

## Jelle van der Voort ∗ Ricardo Martinez-Garcia †‡ § Arjen Doelman ∗

## October 2, 2026 

Abstract 

We investigate how root allometry and below-ground root competition due to root overlap affect the conditions for pattern formation in non-local scalar veg-etation models. We start from a spatially explicit two-component vegetation-water system in which plants acquire soil water through their laterally extended root systems, with root allometry represented by an interaction kernel whose spatial extent increases with the above-ground biomass density. In the limit of fast water dynamics, we eliminate the water variable, resulting in a non-local scalar model for the vegetation dynamics. This reduction reveals that account-ing for root overlap naturally leads to a double integral structure in the scalar model, in contrast to the single convolution term commonly used to model non-local competition for resources. For the resulting scalar model, we identify two distinct mechanisms that can drive Turing instabilities. The first insta-bility mechanism requires root growth to outpace mortality, while the second mechanism relies on the development of competitive pressure zones, in which vegetation growth is impeded by the cumulative competitive pressure exerted by neighbouring areas with higher biomass density. Root allometry promotes the occurrence of both instability mechanisms, while root overlap inhibits the emer-gence of competitive pressure zones. Hence, our results show that both root allometry and root overlap are important features to consider when studying vegetation pattern formation.

Over the last decades, considerable research effort has been devoted to establishing the conditions under which vegetation pattern formation can occur [1–4]. Analyses of scalar PDE models have consistently shown that non-local competitive interactions can induce Turing instabilities that drive patterning [5–14]. In such models, non-local competition is typically represented by a single convolution term where the interaction kernel determines how the dynamics at a given location is influenced by its surroundings. However, in the context of vegetation dynamics, where non-local competition typically stems from root-mediated competition for water, this approach has two important limitations. The first drawback is the simplifying assumption that the interaction range of non-local competition is fixed. This assumption neglects life-stage-dependent effects, such as the much smaller root extents of seedlings and saplings as compared to mature plants. A more suitable modelling framework should account for plants extending their root systems as they grow. This way, plants gain access to previously inac-cessible water sources as they mature, thereby increasing their total water uptake and stimulating further growth. Several modelling studies have included this positive feedback mechanism and shown that it promotes the formation of spatial patterns in vegetation-water models [15, 16] and scalar vegetation models [17–19]. Nevertheless, a systematic derivation of how root allometry affects the onset of patterning through a Turing bifurcation has not yet been carried out. The second shortcoming concerns below-ground root competition when the roots of different plants overlap, which we refer to as root-to-root competition. This form of competition is not captured by scalar models in which non-local competition is repre-sented through a single convolution term. Such an integral accounts for the non-local water uptake through a plant’s own root system and the resulting competitive pres-sure it exerts on above-ground biomass in its surroundings. However, it neglects that neighbouring plants may also exploit the same water sources when their root systems partly overlap. Consequently, when two plants extract water from the same source, yet neither root system extends beyond the above-ground biomass location of the other plant, their competition for that shared source is not taken into account [20]. The cumulative water uptake is therefore overestimated, resulting in an underestima-tion of the competitive pressure between neighbouring plants. Overlap structures of this kind are well established in niche theory [21–23], but have not been considered in models of vegetation pattern formation. The goal of this study is to resolve these two limitations and investigate how the inclusion of root allometry and proper modelling of root-to-root competition affect the conditions for pattern formation in non-local scalar vegetation models. This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce a two-component vegetation-water system that explicitly accounts for root-mediated water uptake, in-cluding root allometry. We then eliminate the explicit water variable by considering the limit of fast water dynamics, thereby obtaining a reduced scalar vegetation model of the full two-component system. In Section 3, we derive the dispersion relation corresponding to the positive homogeneous equilibria of this scalar model, which can be used to determine whether Turing instabilities can occur 1. In Section 4, we derive and interpret the conditions for pattern formation, and analyze how root allometry

# 2 Derivation of a scalar equation

We first introduce a two-component vegetation-water model that includes non-local water acquisition by vegetation through their laterally extended root systems. By considering the limit of fast water dynamics, we obtain a reduced scalar model. Finally, we interpret the novel double integral struc-ture that arises in this model and discuss the representation of both root allometry and root overlap.

x′ accounts for the root characteristics associated with u(x′, t ). This formulation preserves the overall balance between water uptake and depletion (see Appendix A). In particular, the water equation accounts for the depletion of a shared water source by roots originating from different plants, which in turn affects their above-ground growth through the vegetation equation. Hence, below-ground competition when root systems overlap is accurately represented.

## 2.3 Interpretation of the scalar model 

The reduced scalar model (2.4) consists of three distinct contributions: root-mediated vegetation growth, vegetation mortality and biomass spread. Both vegetation mor-tality and biomass spread retain the same interpretation as in the two-component vegetation-water model (2.1), since neither is affected by the reduction procedure. The distinctive feature of (2.4) is its double integral term, which represents vegeta-tion growth through soil water acquisition via laterally extended roots. We explain the meaning of this term working from the inside outward. We start with the argument of θ, i.e., the integral that also appears in the ˙w-equation of (2.1). This integral measures the cumulative root density at the location x′ orig-inating from plants in its surrounding area. The contribution to this quantity orig-inating from above-ground biomass at x′′ is weighted both by its total root density and its root extent through the functions s(u(x′′ , t )) and r(u(x′′ , t )), respectively. The higher the cumulative root density, the greater the competitive pressure for soil water. Therefore, this integral effectively measures the competitive pressure at the location 

x′.Next, the function θ maps this competitive pressure at x′ to the available soil water at x′. Since higher competitive pressure corresponds to a lower soil water availability, 

θ should be decreasing as a function of the competitive pressure. This is indeed the case, as can be deduced from (2.5). Finally, the outer integral plays the same role as the integral in the original ˙ u-equation of (2.1): it measures the non-local soil water uptake of the vegetation located at x

5through its lateral root system, which is characterized by the root functions s(u(x, t )) and r(u(x, t )). The parameter p1 then converts this total water uptake into biomass accumulation. Root allometry is present in the scalar model (2.4) through the function r(u). It appears in the argument of the kernel functions and quantifies the spatial root extent as a function of the above-ground biomass density, analogously to the two-component setting (2.1) and other scalar vegetation models in the literature [17–19]. Root-to-root competition, on the other hand, is included through the double integral structure. This distinguishes our model from scalar models in the literature that describe non-local resource competition using only a single convolution [5–14]. These models include the competitive pressure exerted by the root systems of vegetation, which directly affects above-ground biomass growth. In our model, however, we also account for the fact that this growth depends on an associated root system as well, which supplies the soil water necessary for biomass accumulation. The reduction procedure shows that properly representing this dynamic (i.e., soil water uptake when root systems overlap) requires two interacting integrals rather than a single one.

# 4 Conditions for vegetation pattern formation 

In this section, we determine under which conditions the scalar model (2.4) admits vegetation pattern formation through a Turing instability. For a homogeneous equi-librium ¯ u to be Turing unstable, it must be temporally stable (i.e., stable against ho-mogeneous perturbations) and spatially unstable (i.e., unstable against heterogeneous perturbations). Furthermore, the system must suppress the appearance of unstable perturbations with arbitrarily large wavenumbers, indicating well-posedness. We ver-ify these properties by analyzing the sign of the dispersion relation ω(k; ¯ u) as given in (3.25). Specifically, the equilibrium ¯ u is temporally stable when ω(0; ¯ u) < 0, while it is spatially unstable when ω(k; ¯ u) > 0 over some range of non-zero wavenumbers k.The system is not ill-posed when lim k→±∞ ω(k; u) < 0. 

## 4.1 Well-posedness and temporal stability 

4.1.1 Well-posedness 

Because the non-negative kernel ϕ(x) is normalized, we have lim k→±∞ ˆϕ(k) = 0 (by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma). Moreover, lim k→±∞ β(k; a) = 0 (see Lemma B.2 in 10 Appendix B). Together, these limits imply lim

4.1.2 Temporal stability 

The existence of a positive homogeneous equilibrium in the scalar model (2.4) depends on the specific choices of the functions s(u) and r(u), as well as the parameter values (see Section 3.1). However, whenever such an equilibrium exists, and all homogeneous equilibria are non-degenerate (i.e., not located at a bifurcation point), it can be shown that there must exist a positive homogeneous equilibrium ¯ u that is temporally stable (see Appendix C). 

## 4.2 Spatial instabilities 

The dispersion relation in (3.25) consists of three contributions: a constant term, a term generated by non-local water uptake and a term that comes from biomass spread modelled by diffusion. The influence of the diffusive contribution, −Duk2, is the most straightforward: it decreases the value of the dispersion relation for every k̸ = 0, and its effect becomes stronger when |k| increases. Consequently, biomass spread alone cannot induce a spatial instability, since such an instability requires ω(k; ¯ u) to become positive over some range of non-zero wavenumbers k. On the contrary, biomass spread may even prevent a spatial instability by counteracting the (potential) destabilizing effects of the remaining two terms. This is in clear contrast with the role of biomass spread in classical vegetation-water models [24–26], where the interplay between fast water diffusion and slow biomass spread drives pattern formation through a diffusion-driven Turing instability. Positive values of the dispersion relation must therefore originate from one or both of the remaining terms. Thus, for ¯ u to be spatially unstable, either the constant term must be positive,

4.2.2 Pattern formation through pressure zones driven by root allometry 

Next, we examine the pattern-forming condition (4.3). This condition resembles a well-known criterion for pattern formation through a Turing instability in scalar models with non-local resource competition represented by a single convolution term [10–13, 28, 29], namely inf

This cannot occur when ϕ(x) is positive definite (by Bochner’s theorem). For instance, this means that the Gaussian and Laplace kernels do not satisfy (4.9). Nonetheless, the assumptions imposed on ϕ(x)in Section 2.1 (namely that it is positive, symmetric, normalized and non-increasing in |x|) do not require positive definiteness. Indeed, many ecologically relevant kernel functions, including the parabolic, cosine and top-hat kernels, do satisfy (4.9) as their Fourier transforms take negative values [14]. Such negative excursions are more likely for kernels that decay relatively slowly near their symmetry point, but more quickly further away [9, 30–32]. The ecological interpretation of the condition (4.9) is based on the formation of com-13 petitive pressure zones 2 [10, 11, 14, 30, 33]. Kernels with moderately flat centers and rapidly decaying tails can generate a higher cumulative competitive pressure in the regions between high-biomass areas than within these areas themselves. This spatial asymmetry reinforces biomass accumulation inside denser patches, while vegetation growth is suppressed in the surrounding regions, thereby promoting the emergence of vegetation patterns. In some cases (specifically, when the Turing bifurcation is sub-critical [14]), this instability mechanism may immediately lead to exclusion areas in between densely vegetated areas, corresponding to bare soil regions where vegetation cannot establish [10, 11, 30, 33]. To test whether competitive pressure zones also form in the reduced scalar model (2.4), we performed a series of numerical experiments. We selected parameter sets for which the condition (4.2) was not met, to ensure that observed Turing instabilities were generated solely by (4.3). For each simulation, we evaluated the inner integral of (2.4) (which quantifies the spatial distribution of competitive pressure, as discussed in Section 2.3) together with the resulting biomass distribution after vegetation patterns had formed. Across all considered choices of the functions s(u) and r(u), the compet-itive pressure was consistently higher in the regions between high-biomass areas than within these areas themselves (see the right panel of Figure 3 for a representative example simulation). These numerical observations support the idea that the forma-tion of competitive pressure zones also underlies the instability mechanism associated with the condition (4.3). In contrast to the instability criterion (4.9) for scalar models with a single convolution term, the condition (4.3) may also be satisfied by kernels whose Fourier transform is non-negative for all wavenumbers. # 5 Discussion 

In this study, we investigated how root overlap and root allometry affect vegetation pattern formation. We first formulated the vegetation-water system (2.1) which ac-counts for root-mediated water uptake, including root allometry through the function 

r(u). We eliminated the water variable in the limit of fast water dynamics, resulting in the reduced scalar vegetation model (2.4). The explicit description of soil water 16 depletion in the two-component setting naturally gives rise to a double integral struc-ture in this reduced model, which accounts for below-ground resource competition when root systems overlap, i.e., root-to-root competition. For this scalar model, we derived the dispersion relation which we analyzed to deter-mine the conditions under which Turing instabilities can occur. This analysis revealed two distinct instability mechanisms that can lead to the emergence of spatial vegeta-tion patterns. The first mechanism requires root growth to outpace mortality as the above-ground biomass density increases [14], while the second mechanism relies on the emergence of competitive pressure zones, in which vegetation growth is suppressed by the cumulative non-local water uptake of surrounding areas with higher biomass density [10, 11, 14, 30, 33]. Root allometry and root overlap affect these two instability mechanisms differently. Regarding the root-growth-outpacing-mortality mechanism, we note that a root sys-tem that extends laterally with the above-ground biomass density naturally boosts root growth. Hence, strong root allometry may potentially cause root growth to out-pace mortality, and can therefore drive vegetation pattern formation through this instability mechanism. Nonetheless, even when root allometry is fully absent, the in-stability mechanism may still operate. Root-to-root competition, on the other hand, does not directly influence the existence criterion (4.2) of the root-growth-outpacing-mortality mechanism. Pattern formation through competitive pressure zones, on the other hand, is pro-moted by root allometry but inhibited by root-to-root competition. In scalar vege-tation models neglecting both root allometry and root-to-root competition, non-local resource competition is typically represented through a single convolution. The re-sulting dispersion relation contains the Fourier transform of the interaction kernel, and a Turing instability can occur when this Fourier transform takes negative values. Accounting for root overlap, however, introduces a double integral into the scalar vegetation model, which results in a squared Fourier transform in the dispersion re-lation. Since this quantity is non-negative, the pressure zone instability mechanism disappears, similar to the spatial stability observed in niche theory when overlap structures are considered [23]. When root allometry is additionally included, one of the Fourier transforms is replaced by the function β(k; a), as defined in (3.24), thereby restoring the possibility of pattern formation driven by competitive pressure zones. In particular, for kernels with a non-negative Fourier transform (e.g., kernels with relatively flat centers), competitive pressure zones are more likely to appear when plants invest more resources in lateral root extension than in increasing their total root density, which is consistent with the general idea that long-range competition promotes pattern formation [12]. Our results show that root allometry and root overlap strongly influence the conditions for vegetation pattern formation. Both features should therefore be considered when developing vegetation models for water-limited environments. In several vegetation-water systems, root allometry and root overlap are explicitly represented [15, 16], but this is typically not the case in models in which soil water is modelled implicitly [6, 11, 14]. Our reduction procedure reveals a way to incorporate both features into such scalar models. Root allometry can be represented by allowing the spatial extent of the interaction kernel to increase with the above-ground biomass density, similar to previous work [17–19]. Root-to-root competition requires a double integral structure (as in (2.4)) to model the impact of root overlap accurately, instead of the commonly adopted single convolution representation. Such a double integral may also be relevant 17 for the description of any situation in which organisms forage over overlapping areas, such as central place foragers like ants and termites [20]. Throughout this work, we focused on vegetation pattern formation driven by the ability of plants to access water beyond their above-ground location through their laterally extended root systems. To isolate the associated instability mechanisms, we restricted our attention to ecosystems in which soil water diffusion is negligible, thereby excluding diffusion-driven instabilities. Future work should extend our re-sults by introducing soil water diffusion into (2.1) to investigate how the instability mechanisms identified here interact with diffusion-driven instabilities. In particular, it would be interesting to determine whether these mechanisms reinforce or suppress one another. In our model set-up, we did not consider some of the facilitative interactions that can occur in arid ecosystems. Nonetheless, facilitation plays an important role in vegetation pattern formation; for example, increased water infiltration in vegetated patches can generate spatial self-organization [25, 34]. Similarly, reduced evaporation due to shading has been shown to influence both pattern formation and pattern dynamics [15, 35, 36]. Building on our results, future efforts could investigate how local facilitative interactions, such as those occurring near the stem, and non-local facilitative interactions, for instance within the radius of the crown, interact with root allometry and root overlap to influence the conditions for vegetation pattern formation. Another natural extension of our framework would be to consider multiple competing plant species. With such an extension, one could investigate whether differences in root allometry characteristics between species can enable species coexistence through spatiotemporal niche formation [20, 37]. Lastly, we mainly focused on the emergence of vegetation patterns and did not inves-tigate the dynamics of the resulting patterns far from the pattern-forming bifurcation point. Understanding how spatial vegetation patterns respond to gradual or sudden changes in environmental conditions could provide further insights into ecosystem re-silience [38, 39]. Recent work has started to address this question by showing that the shape of non-local competition and facilitation kernels affects both pattern onset and pattern resilience [40]. A natural next step is to investigate whether patterns induced by the different instability mechanisms identified here respond differently to environmental change, and, in particular, whether one mechanism can maintain spatial patterns under conditions in which the other fails. To summarize, our work addresses the influence of root overlap and root allometry on vegetation pattern formation. Our results show that both features should be considered in studies of spatial vegetation patterns in water-stressed ecosystems, as they alter the existence criteria of the underlying instability mechanisms.

Let ¯ u > u ∗ denote the largest non-degenerate homogeneous equilibrium, which exists as LHS (u) < RHS (u) for sufficiently large u. If LHS ′(¯ u) > RHS ′(¯ u), we would have LHS (u) > RHS (u) for all u > ¯u which contradicts that LHS (u) <RHS (u) for sufficiently large u. Thus, LHS ′(¯ u) < RHS ′(¯ u). Finally, by (C.2), this inequality implies that ω(0; ¯ u) < 0. Therefore, ¯ u is temporally stable.
