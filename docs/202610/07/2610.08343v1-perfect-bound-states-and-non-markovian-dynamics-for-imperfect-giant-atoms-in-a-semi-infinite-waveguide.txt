(Dated: October 7, 2026) Bound states in structured reservoirs require exact interference conditions and are therefore sensitive to imper-fections in the coupling geometry. In this paper, we study a two-level giant atom coupled at multiple points to a mirror-terminated semi-infinite waveguide. The exact atom-photon bound state requires both cancellation of the outgoing radiation amplitude and dispersive matching to the atomic detuning. Generalized delay-equation dy-namics, continued-pole spectra, and real-space photon fields verify this criterion for coupling-strength, position, and coupling-phase imperfections. For representative realizations, retuning restores one-, two-, and three-root responses after strength errors and one- and two-root responses after position errors. The specified position-imperfect three-root configuration fails because its 2π root is no longer dark, consistent with a 58 .77% spectral mismatch. Ensembles of 1000 realizations at each error amplitude show near-unity target-state recovery for strength errors, unity recovery of the specified one- and two-root targets but no three-root recovery for position errors, and no nontrivial exact recovery for independent phase errors. Optimized phase-imperfect responses instead retain finite linewidths. Root matching with realization-dependent control retuning therefore constitutes a compensation procedure, rather than generic passive robustness against arbitrary disorder. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Waveguide quantum electrodynamics provides a controlled setting for studying how propagation, interference, and reser-voir memory reshape light-matter interactions [1–4]. Giant atoms extend this setting beyond the local-coupling limit be-cause the same emitter interacts with a waveguide at sev-eral spatially separated points. Acoustic and superconduct-ing implementations have established this nonlocal coupling architecture [5–8]. Early giant-acoustic-atom theory identi-fied deterministic propagation delay and frequency-dependent interference as intrinsic consequences of this geometry [9, 10]. Subsequent theory and experiment have shown that self-interference can tailor relaxation, Lamb shifts, level structure, decoherence-free interactions, vacuum-Rabi split-ting, internal-resonator interference, Fano features, optical re-sponse, and non-dipolar photon scattering [11–23]. Spatially separated coupling points can also support atom-photon bound states embedded in an otherwise open waveg-uide. Such states appear as nondecaying or oscillating pole contributions and have been studied in giant-atom, chiral, photonic-lattice, and structured-waveguide settings [24–40]. The influence of decoherence on the Hall conductance of two-band systems has also been investigated [41]. More gen-erally, Hofstadter-ladder, metamaterial, topological, broken-time-reversal, and higher-dimensional reservoirs broaden the available interference landscapes for giant-atom quantum op-tics [42–55]. Resonator-array and strong-coupling studies additionally connect the long-time dynamics to atom-photon

Their formation is controlled by destructive radiation interference rather than by a finite photonic band gap alone. The same principle underlies com-plex decoherence-free interactions, chiral networks, nonrecip-rocal excitation transfer and scattering, and long-lived collec-tive states [66–77]. Collisional descriptions, time-dependent couplings, transition-frequency modulation, and auxiliary res-onators provide complementary routes for controlling this in-terference [78–83]. The semi-infinite waveguide adds a distinct coherent-feedback channel, where atom-mirror interference and single-end waveguide studies show that radiation emitted toward the termination can return after a finite propagation time, so a Markovian decay rate cannot generally describe the full dy-namics [84–91]. Related work has treated position-dependent emission and memory in semi-infinite rectangular guides, laser-tunable multilevel dynamics, non-Markovian collective emission, scattering-induced memory, and multiphoton feed-back [92–106]. Delayed feedback can create persistent beats, long-distance correlations, and bound states in the continuum [107–113]. In the clean semi-infinite giant-atom system, the mirror-assisted feedback produces static and oscillating bound components whose frequencies are fixed by imaginary-axis poles [114]. Related studies of nonperturbative giant-atom dynamics, topology, collective radiance, chiral networks, and limits imposed by structured photonic baths further emphasize that retardation and interference are required to be treated at the level of the full pole equation [115–123]. Imperfections make the bound-state question more restric-tive in realistic settings. Random coupling-strength and posi-tion variations can alter decoherence-free behavior and shift or broaden bound-state-in-the-continuum features [124–128]. Complex local couplings add relative phases that rotate the arXiv:2610.08343v1 [physics.optics] 6 Oct 2026 2radiation amplitudes in the complex plane and change the as-sociated non-Markovian dynamics [129–133]. Existing re-sults therefore identify several relevant control channels, but a direct criterion separating cancellation of outgoing radiation from reactive frequency matching is still needed for arbitrary positions and complex local couplings. Without that separa-tion, a long-lived numerical plateau can be mistaken for an exact bound state, and realization-dependent parameter com-pensation can be mistaken for passive statistical robustness. In this work, we derive a unified radiation-dispersion de-composition for a two-level giant atom coupled at arbitrary ordered positions to a mirror-terminated waveguide. Within this framework, we consider bound-state configurations by identifying one, two, or three distinct bound-state roots. The real part of the imaginary-axis pole is identified as the non-negative radiation functional, whose vanishing closes the outgoing channel. The remaining imaginary part defines an independent dispersive-matching function. For config-urations with multiple bound-state roots, all selected roots must satisfy the same matching conditions for common sys-tem parameters. We use this equation pair to distinguish exact matched poles from finite-linewidth transients under coupling-strength, position, and phase imperfections. The ev-idence combines generalized delay-equation dynamics, pole continuation, frequency-domain diagnostics, and real-space fields generated from the same pole set. The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we estab-lish the generalized model and the common pole criterion. In Secs. III–V, we investigate coupling-strength, position, and coupling-phase imperfections. In Sec. VI, we compare the three imperfection channels over disorder ensembles and identify their failure modes. Sec. VII is devoted to conclu-sions. Technical derivations and numerical details are given in the Appendices.

> D. Scope of the imperfection analysis

We distinguish realization-dependent compensation from passive statistical robustness. Under realization-dependent compensation, a given imperfection realization is treated as known, and the available control parameters are retuned un-til the radiation-cancellation and dispersive-matching condi-tions are simultaneously satisfied. In passive robustness, no realization-dependent compensation is allowed. The exact strength- and position-imperfect solutions below concern ex-act bound-state recovery through realization-dependent com-pensation. The phase-imperfect optimization retains finite linewidths and is therefore interpreted as suppression of de-cay rather than exact restoration. The ensemble calculations quantify the success probability of the realization-dependent compensation procedure but do not establish passive statisti-cal robustness against arbitrary disorder.

> VII. CONCLUSION

We have derived a common criterion for atom-photon bound states of a giant atom coupled at arbitrary positions and with complex local amplitudes to a mirror-terminated waveg-uide. On the imaginary axis, the pole function separates into the nonnegative radiation term γ|B(θ)|2 and the reactive mis-match F (θ). An exact simple bound pole exists only when the same real frequency closes the outgoing channel, satisfies the atomic matching condition, and has a nonzero pole derivative. This separation also fixes the interpretation of the dynamics: one matched root produces a stationary nondecaying contri-bution, while two or three matched roots produce persistent pairwise beating. The three imperfection channels deform dif-ferent parts of the same interference problem. Strength errors change path weights, position errors change both propagation phases and delays, and phase errors rotate the radiation ampli-tudes in the complex plane. Representative delay-equation so-lutions, pole linewidths, spectra, and real-space fields consis-tently distinguish exact restoration from finite-time trapping. The ensemble results sharpen that distinction: realization-dependent retuning recovers the tested strength targets almost universally and the position one- and two-root targets univer-sally over the sampled amplitudes, but it does not recover the specified position triple target or any nontrivial independent-phase target. Symmetry-enforced trivial roots θ = qπ remain exact when ∆ = qπ , whereas the optimized responses associ-ated with nontrivial phase targets retain finite linewidths and are therefore quasibound. Finite bandwidth, imperfect reflection, propagation atten-uation, nonguided decay, and pure dephasing will generally move an ideal pole away from the imaginary axis. Quan-tifying those shifts requires an explicit structured-reservoir or lossy-platform calculation and is not inferred from the present delay model. At a broader platform level, en-gineered system-reservoir energy exchange may connect giant-atom waveguides to remote quantum-battery charging and a generalized fluctuation relation [134–136]. Estab-lishing either connection in the present imperfect, mirror-terminated setting requires a separate thermodynamic anal-ysis. Within its stated scope, the radiation-dispersion de-composition provides a practical criterion for exact bound-state recovery through realization-dependent compensation and prevents long-lived transients from being misidentified as disorder-protected bound states. Looking forward, extend-ing the present single-atom radiation-dispersion decomposi-tion to multi-emitter configurations could reveal novel collec-tive non-Markovian dynamics and robust chiral dark-state en-gineering.

> Appendix D: Reality of the bound-state frequency parameter

Following the imaginary-axis parametrization introduced immediately after Eq. (9), we write z = −iθ . It is impor-tant to distinguish the position of the pole z from the char-acter of the parameter θ. In particular, the statement that a bound-state pole lies on the imaginary axis does not mean that 

θ is imaginary. On the contrary, an imaginary-axis pole is ob-tained when θ is real. To make this distinction explicit, write a general complex value as θ = θR + iθ I , with θR, θ I ∈ R.Using this parametrization in z = −iθ gives z = θI − iθ R,so that Re z = θI and Im z = −θR. The corresponding pole contribution to the time-domain excitation amplitude follows from the residue expansion in Eq. (7) as ezT = eθIT e−iθ RT

(with T = t/τ 0). Its magnitude is 

ezT = e θIT . (D1) The relation Re z = θI, together with Eq. (D1), distinguishes the following three cases.   

> 1. Real θ

If θ = θR ∈ R, then θI = 0, and hence z = −iθ R

with Re z = 0 . The pole therefore lies on the imaginary axis. The exponential factor is therefore e−iθ RT , identical to the factor carried by each individual bound-pole term in Eq. (17), and it has unit modulus. Thus, a real value of θ does not produce exponential decay. It is the only case that can represent the frequency of an exact stationary atom–photon bound state. Equivalently, the corresponding physical energy is E = θR/τ 0, which is purely real as required for a normaliz-able eigenstate of the full Hermitian atom-waveguide Hamil-tonian. However, the reality of θ alone is not sufficient. The candidate value needs to also satisfy D(−iθ ) = 0 , where 

D(z) is the dimensionless characteristic function defined in Eq. (9). For real θ, the radiation–dispersion decomposition in Eq. (13) applies. Since γ > 0, its vanishing is equiva-lent to the two conditions in Eq. (14). The first condition cancels the outgoing radiation channel, whereas the second matches the dispersive shift to the atomic frequency. The re-tained pole is additionally simple. Equation (15) identifies 

N (θ) = D′(z)|z=−iθ , and the admissible-root set in Eq. (16) requires N (θ)̸ = 0 . The associated atom–photon eigenstate also has a finite normalization. Consequently, a real θ is a nec-essary but not sufficient condition for an exact bound state.   

> 2. Purely imaginary θ

For a nonzero purely imaginary value, write θ = iχ , where 

χ ∈ R\{ 0}. The relation z = −iθ then gives z = χ ∈ R, and the time-dependent factor becomes ezT = eχT . If χ < 0, the pole lies on the negative real axis and gives a nonoscillatory exponentially decaying contribution. Such a pole describes a transient relaxation mode and not an exact bound state. If 

χ > 0, the contribution grows exponentially. This would in-dicate an instability and is excluded for the passive system considered here. The special value χ = 0 gives θ = 0 and 

z = 0 . Although zero can formally be regarded as both real and purely imaginary, this case belongs to the real-θ boundary and can represent a bound state only if it separately satisfies the admissible-root requirements in Eq. (16) and the normal-ization condition. Therefore, a nonzero purely imaginary θ

cannot describe an exact bound state.   

> 3. General complex θ

For a genuinely complex value θ = θR + iθ I, with θR̸ = 0 

and θI̸ = 0 , the corresponding pole has Re z = θI̸ = 0 .For a stable decaying resonance, θI < 0, and the pole con-tribution is ezT = e−| θI|T e−iθ RT . It therefore represents a finite-linewidth oscillatory resonance or a quasibound tran-sient. Its oscillation frequency is determined by θR, while its amplitude-decay rate is |θI|. It is not a stationary, normaliz-able bound state because its pole does not lie on the imaginary axis. The radiation–dispersion decomposition in Eq. (13) was derived for real θ and should not be applied unchanged to a complex θ. General complex resonances are instead obtained by solving D(z) = 0 with the full characteristic function in Eq. (9), where z = θI − iθ R.

Im θ < 0 ⇐⇒ Re z < 0, (D2) 

Im θ > 0 ⇐⇒ Re z > 0.

A purely imaginary or gen-eral complex nonreal value of θ produces a pole away from the imaginary axis and therefore describes decay, growth, or a finite-linewidth quasibound response rather than an exact bound state.

An unresolved even-multiplicity or tangential zero of a real radiation amplitude may therefore be missed. The numerical cardinalities below are strict with respect to the resolved set in Eq. (E7), rather than a proof that no unresolved sign-preserving zero exists.
