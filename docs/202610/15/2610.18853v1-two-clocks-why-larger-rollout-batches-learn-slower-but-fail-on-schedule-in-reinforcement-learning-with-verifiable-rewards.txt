# Two Clocks: Why Larger Rollout Batches Learn Slower but Fail on Schedule in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards 

Yuante Li 1* , Junwei Quan 2* , Kai Qiu 1, Kate Zhang 1, Yicheng Tao 11 Carnegie Mellon University, 2 University of Toronto 

# yuantel@cs.cmu.edu, junwei.quan@mail.utoronto.ca 

Abstract 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) relies on costly model-generated re-sponses, motivating larger rollout batches and square-root learning-rate scaling. Reported to approximately preserve progress per sample with one optimizer step per rollout, this rule is less understood in practical multi-step training. We develop a two-clock framework that sepa-rates the samples needed to make progress from those consumed before collapse. Under a local quadratic model, the square-root rule preserves first-order drift per sample but not progress: larger batches take fewer, longer updates and lose more to curvature, yielding a learning-rate-dependent critical batch beyond which sam-ple efficiency degrades. A drift-threshold hy-pothesis predicts a complementary failure clock whose sample count remains approximately in-variant along the square-root rule. Controlled GRPO experiments on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B across a 16 × batch range support both predic-tions. Ratio clipping delays failure, especially for larger batches, but the progress gap persists without it. Widening the clip trades stability for a transient speed gain. The progress gap reappears on another dataset, a general-purpose checkpoint, and a 7B model. Finally, we use the two-clock framework to derive a simple multi-step RLVR schedule: start below the critical batch and switch to a larger batch before failure. Our code is available at: two-clocks-rlvr.

rollout batch , the number of responses generated by one policy before it is updated, and rely on the

For Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) with one optimizer step per rollout, the rule approximately works: rollout batches from 512 to 8K responses follow learning curves within about one accuracy point of each other when plot-ted against the number of samples consumed (Li et al., 2026). However, practical RLVR training rarely oper-ates in this one-step regime. To amortize genera-tion, practical RLVR recipes split each rollout into mini-batches and take one optimizer step on each. 1arXiv:2610.18853v1 [cs.LG] 13 Oct 2026 DAPO, for example, uses 16 optimizer steps per rollout (Yu et al., 2025). From the second step on-ward, the current policy has moved away from the policy that generated the data, so the token-level im-portance ratios depart from one and the PPO-style ratio clip begins to suppress gradient contributions from tokens whose ratios move beyond the clipping range. In our multi-step GRPO experiments, three phe-nomena appear together under the square-root rule: 

➀ Progress slows with batch size. Larger rollout batches learn more slowly per sample. ➁ Failure keeps time in samples. Without the clip, larger batches collapse after the same number of sam-ples as smaller ones, despite taking far fewer op-timizer steps. ➂ Clipping helps larger batches most. Although clipping is often viewed as a bot-tleneck on learning (Qi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a), its stabilizing effect is strongest for the larger batches. Figure 1 visualizes this split into two clocks: progress becomes increasingly sample-inefficient with larger rollout batches, while failure stays locked to the number of samples consumed. Prior work studies these effects separately: batch-size invariance (Hilton et al., 2022; Li et al., 2026), critical-batch behavior with the learning rate tuned at every batch (McCandlish et al., 2018), and clip-ping at a fixed batch size. None explains why all three emerge together under the same batch-dependent learning-rate rule. We explain these phenomena with a two-clock framework of batch scaling. Its central observa-tion is that the square-root rule preserves the pol-icy’s drift per sample, the persistent displacement it accumulates, but not the progress that drift pro-duces. A larger rollout batch takes fewer optimizer steps on the same samples, and the square-root rule makes each step longer. Under a local quadratic model, these fewer, longer steps accumulate the same drift per sample but lose more progress to cur-vature. This gives a progress clock , which counts samples to a target accuracy and slows beyond a learning-rate-dependent critical batch. If collapse occurs when accumulated drift reaches a threshold, the same scaling rule gives a failure clock that is invariant in samples. The progress clock follows from the local quadratic model, and the failure clock rests on a drift-threshold hypothesis that we test. We test the two-clock model with controlled GRPO experiments that vary rollout batch size, learning rate, and clipping over a sixteenfold range of rollout batches. The progress clock departs from sample invariance beyond a critical batch: with eight steps per rollout, as the learning rate rises tenfold, the sample-cost exponent climbs from 0.15 to 0.44. Unlike the tuned-learning-rate pic-ture (McCandlish et al., 2018), the critical batch therefore shrinks with the learning rate assigned by the square-root rule and lies well below the gra-dient noise scale. The failure clock, in contrast, does not move along the rule: without the clip, a 4,096 batch fails after 56 to 80 optimizer steps and a 512 batch after 648, yet both fail after 30 to 50k samples. Restoring the clip delays collapse twofold for the small batch and fourfold for the large one, and the progress gap is present with or without it. The progress gap reappears on a second dataset, a second checkpoint, and a 7B model. The separation between the two clocks further yields a simple batch schedule: start below the critical batch for sample-efficient progress, then switch to a larger batch when throughput or stability makes it preferable. The ramp approaches the best fixed batch while generating three quarters of its samples at the large batch. To summarize, our contributions are as follows. 

➀ We introduce a two-clock framework for multi-step GRPO, in which a local model derives a learning-rate-dependent critical batch for progress, while a drift-threshold hypothesis pre-dicts failure at a fixed number of samples along the square-root rule. 

➁ We test these predictions across batch size, learn-ing rate, clipping, model scale, and dataset, with failure-law fits that favor drift scaling over noise-driven alternatives and clip and replay controls showing that the clip delays failure while the progress gap persists without it. 

➂ We derive a practical batch schedule for multi-step RLVR from the two clocks: start below the critical batch and switch to a larger batch before failure. In our experiments, this schedule approaches the best fixed-batch performance.

3 Method: A Two-Clock Model of Batch Scaling

The model first yields a progress law from curvature, then adds a drift-threshold hypothesis for failure, and finally places the ra-tio clip on the drift accumulated within a rollout.

Predictions. The model gives three testable pre-dictions, corresponding to the three phenomena of the introduction. 

P1. Progress slows beyond a critical batch. Sam-ple efficiency degrades with the batch as the curvature term becomes important, more so at higher learning rates, and even where the clip cannot act. 

P2. Failure keeps time in samples. Without the clip, the square-root rule preserves the failure point in samples, and lowering the learning rate delays failure linearly, as the drift law predicts, not quadratically, as a noise-driven 

1/η 2 law would. 

P3. The clip buys stability rather than sustained speed. Larger batches are clipped more, so the clip should delay their failure most without removing the progress gap, and widening it should give this protection up. 

4 Experimental Setup 

We train Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (Yang et al., 2024) with GRPO in verl (Sheng et al., 2025) on DAPO-Math-17k (Yu et al., 2025), using G=8 , tempera-ture 1, and responses up to 3072 tokens. We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a short linear warm-up followed by a constant learning rate. To isolate batch-size, learning-rate, and clipping effects, we disable KL penalty, entropy bonus, dy-namic sampling, and gradient-norm clipping, and test gradient-norm clipping separately. Each run has a budget of 98,304 responses. We evaluate every 8,192 responses on 512 held-out problems, averaging four samples per problem. Additional details are in App. B.

The penalty appears beyond a critical batch.

5.2 Failure keeps time in samples (P2) 

P2 predicts that without clipping, failure is gov-erned by consumed samples rather than optimizer steps and does not move along the square-root rule. 

Failure aligns in samples, not steps. Removing the clip exposes this clock (Fig. 4). At the gradient-norm onset, the 4,096 batch fails after only 7–10 rollouts, or 56–80 optimizer steps, while the 512 batch lasts 81 rollouts, or 648 steps. Yet both fail after 30–50k responses. 

The square-root rule preserves the failure point. 

Across 21 unclipped runs, we vary the optimizer batch by 32 × and the learning rate by nearly 6×

and fit Nfail ∝ bβ η−γ (Table 4). Along the square-root rule the failure point does not move with the batch: β − γ/ 2 = −0.08 over the failed runs and 

0.11 with the censored runs included, and both 90% intervals contain zero, which rules out the batch-blind prediction of −1/2. The fitted γ = 0 .64 –

0.72 rules out the random-walk prediction of 2, and of the three candidate forms only the drift form survives a likelihood-ratio test against the free fit (p ≈ 0.3, against p < 10 −4 for the other two). Fig. 5 plots each run against the law, and fourteen of the eighteen failing runs lie within a factor of 1.5 of it. 

The failure pattern favors accumulated drift. 

Failure depends on the learning rate far more weakly than the quadratic law of a noise-driven random walk, and at the highest learning rates it coincides with a jump of about four orders of mag-nitude in the KL divergence between the updated and rollout policies. The failure point is also not an artifact of disabling gradient-norm clipping: with a norm cap of 1.0, the small batch fails after 36k samples, within the 31–46k range of its seeds. 

Robustness. Failure also keeps time in samples on the 7B model, with a model-specific constant. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the two batches fail 6×

apart in rollouts but only 1.3× apart in samples. Along the square-root rule, larger batches thus fail after the same number of consumed samples de-spite taking far fewer optimizer steps.

The clip delays failure, most for the large batch.

6 Implications 

The two clocks give a simple batch-selection rule. Its one free choice, the switch point, is forgiving over a broad range.

7 Related Work 

Batch-size scaling and invariance. Batch size is a basic scaling variable in optimization, affecting both computational efficiency and training dynam-ics. The linear rule for SGD (Smith et al., 2018), which also motivates growing the batch during training, and the square-root rule for Adam (Mal-ladi et al., 2022) keep training trajectories compa-rable across batch sizes. Hilton et al. (2022) make PPO batch-size invariant by decoupling the clip’s proximal policy from the behavior policy, given a sufficiently small optimizer batch, while Li et al. (2026) show that square-root scaling keeps GRPO learning curves aligned with one optimizer step per rollout over a bounded range of batch sizes. In contrast, we study the multi-step regime, where this batch-size invariance no longer holds and drift preservation does not imply progress preservation. 

Critical batch. Beyond a critical batch, larger batches stop saving optimizer steps and start cost-ing samples. With the learning rate re-tuned at every batch, the critical batch of McCandlish et al. (2018) equals the gradient noise scale and does not depend on the learning rate. Under square-root scaling, by contrast, the learning rate is coupled to batch size, leading to a different critical batch that depends on the learning-rate scale. In LLM RL, larger batches have been found to reach higher final accuracy (Yang et al., 2026b; Khatri et al., 2026) while learning more slowly (Khatri et al., 2026). Our two-clock view connects these observations by separating the batch scale governing progress from the sample scale governing failure. 

Training collapse. Batch scaling also affects training stability. Suk and Duan (2025) derive a step-size threshold above which RLVR collapses, while Song et al. (2026) find that collapse time in asynchronous GRPO scales roughly as 1/η , the fixed-batch case of Eq. (7), and attribute it to direc-tional drift. We add the batch dimension: along the square-root rule, failure is approximately invariant in consumed samples. 

Ratio clipping. Training stability in GRPO is closely tied to ratio clipping. Trust-region meth-ods replace or soften the hard clip (Qi et al., 2026; Dwyer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a; Jia et al., 2026), while Tian et al. (2026) relax it to enable stale-rollout reuse and Khatri et al. (2026) show that the clip threshold changes RL scaling behavior. In contrast to work that treats clipping as a bottle-neck (Qi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a), we find that it mainly buys stability, at a transient cost in speed. 

8 Conclusion 

Scaling rules are often interpreted as promises of invariance. For multi-step GRPO, the square-root rule instead preserves first-order drift per sam-ple, not progress per sample, yielding two clocks: progress slows beyond a learning-rate-dependent critical batch, while a drift-threshold hypothesis predicts an approximately sample-invariant failure point. Our experiments support these predictions and show that ratio clipping buys stability, espe-cially for larger batches, while the progress gap per-sists without it. Together, the two clocks suggest a simple strategy for multi-step RLVR: train below the critical batch for efficient progress, then switch to a larger batch before failure when throughput demands it. 8Limitations 

We broaden our evaluation across multiple check-points, two datasets, and a 7B model, but compute and resource constraints still limit our coverage to one model family and one task domain. Our theory is intentionally local, and direct measurement of the curvature term is computationally expensive at this scale, so we evaluate it through its testable predictions. Failure points also exhibit intrinsic variation across seeds. Finally, we measure sam-ple efficiency in responses, while token, compute, and wall-clock efficiency additionally depend on generation lengths, hardware, and system imple-mentation. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work analyzes the training dynamics of RLVR on public math datasets with open-weight models and releases no new model or dataset. Its main soci-etal relevance is environmental: our batch schedule aims to reduce the number of generated samples a large batch needs to reach a given accuracy, al-though our own study required many training runs (App. B reports the hardware). We see no addi-tional risks beyond those of LLM post-training in general.
