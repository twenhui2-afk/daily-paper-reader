# Gas radiation and the gravity limit of flicker in a co-flowing hydrogen jet diffusion flame 

Tianfang Xie 1, a) and Meng Wang 2

> 1)

School of Aeronautics and Astronautics, Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907, USA 

> 2)

School of Automotive Engineering, Hunan Industry Polytechnic, Changsha 410208, China 

Although weak at normal gravity, gas radiation sets the gravity at which a co-flowing hydrogen jet diffusion flame stops flickering. Flamelet-based simulations with unity Lewis numbers, which reproduce the flicker frequency measured by femtosecond coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering within 4%, are combined with local stability analysis. At one tenth of normal gravity, the adiabatic flame flickers, the radiating flame does not, and switching the emission off restores flicker. Radiation acts through two mechanisms that do not weaken at reduced gravity. The hot outer layer loses buoyancy in proportion to the gas residence time, and a disturbance that displaces the flame also displaces its emission, which damps the disturbance on the lean flank (displaced-emission damping). Freezing the radiative source, which removes only its response to disturbances, slows the downstream recession of the oscillation more than tenfold. Both mechanisms slow the upstream spreading of disturbances. This spreading, competing with the co-flow, sets the onset location and the critical co-flow. Without radiation, the spreading speed scales with the square root of gravity; its radiative reduction does not. At reduced gravity, the flicker limit therefore becomes a straight line relating the square root of the co-flow Froude number to a radiation number, the ratio of buoyant to radiative time scales. The calibrated line places the critical gravity for the experimental co-flow at 0.13 times normal gravity, 2.6 times the adiabatic value. It correctly predicted whether two further flames keep flickering. Beyond the limit, transient wave packets can mimic flicker throughout a short drop-tower test.

I. INTRODUCTION 

Buoyant diffusion flames oscillate at a low frequency, typically 10–20 Hz for burners of centimeter scale, a phenomenon known as flame flicker 1–3 . The oscillation is carried by toroidal vortices that form outside the flame and travel downstream along its surface; they arise in the shear layer between the buoyantly accelerated hot gas and the surrounding air 4–6 . Because these vortices are driven by buoyancy, the flicker frequency depends only weakly on the fuel or the flow rate and scales approximately as ( g/D )1/2, where g is the gravitational acceleration and D the burner diameter 2,6 . Experiments at reduced and elevated gravity give f ∝ gn with n between about 0.4 and 2/3, depending on the burner size and the Froude number 3,7 .Reducing gravity and adding an air co-flow both suppress the flicker. In soot-free hydrogen flames, radiation is weak at normal gravity and is usually neglected. At reduced gravity, however, the gas stays longer in the flame and loses more of its heat to radiation, while the buoyant drive of the flicker weakens. This paper asks how gas radiation shifts the gravity at which a co-flowing hydrogen jet diffusion flame stops flickering. In a spatially developing flow, a convectively unstable region amplifies disturbances while they are swept downstream, and the flow returns to its steady state once they have passed. In an absolutely unstable region, disturbances also spread upstream, and a sufficiently large region of this kind sustains a global mode that oscillates at its own frequency 8,9 . Light and hot jets become absolutely unstable below a critical ratio of jet to ambient density 10–12 . Once a co-flow exceeds a critical fraction of the jet velocity, below about 20% for helium jets 13,14 , it sweeps disturbances downstream and removes the absolute instability, and with it the global oscillation. In jet diffusion flames without co-flow, linear global analysis 15 and direct simulations of hydrogen–nitrogen jet flames 16 find the flicker, a global mode, only below a critical Froude number; local analyses overpredict this threshold by more than a factor of two 15 . For methane flames at normal gravity, this critical ratio correlates with the jet Froude number 21 , and a global analysis of buoyant plumes relates it to the Froude number and the density ratio 22 . A flicker suppressed by the co-flow can be re-excited by acoustic forcing 23 . Reducing gravity suppresses the flicker as well: laminar jet diffusion flames do not flicker in microgravity 24,25 , and helium jets lose their global oscillation in a drop tower 26 . The gravity at which the flicker ceases is less well established. Simulations of hydrogen–nitrogen jet flames without radiation found the flicker still present at 0.05 times normal gravity 27 , whereas in an earlier simulation the outer structures were barely identifiable at half of normal gravity 28 . For a methane flame on a cup burner, simulations predict a steady flame at 0.5 times normal gravity and below; the authors attribute this cut-off to the disappearance of an absolute instability and expect it near zero gravity for high-velocity jets from small nozzles 29 .Radiation can suppress the flicker, and its influence grows as gravity decreases. In a sooting ethylene flame at normal gravity, soot radiation alone suppresses the flicker, the oscillation decaying at an unchanged frequency 30 . In laminar methane flames, reabsorption changes the structure of flickering flames more than that of steady ones 31 . In the cup-burner simulations, the radiative heat loss, small at normal gravity, quenched the tip of the flame at zero gravity 29 . Qin et al. 32 simulated partially premixed methane flames brought suddenly from normal to zero gravity: the flames became nearly steady within 2.2 s, radiation changed the zero-gravity flame far more than the normal-gravity one, and the authors noted that a radiation Damk¨ ohler number varies inversely with the Froude number. These studies leave open whether radiation removes the flicker only by cooling the mean flow, and thereby weakening its buoyant drive, or also by acting on the disturbances themselves, and what then sets the limit beyond which a flame no longer flickers. Hydrogen flames make it possible to study this question for gas radiation alone. Their radiation comes from water vapor only: small at normal gravity, it grows with the size of the flame and the residence time of the gas at reduced gravity, and without soot, it follows the local temperature and composition. In steam-diluted premixed hydrogen flames, water-vapor radiation also acts through the reactants: the fresh gas absorbs part of the emitted radiation, which preheats it and advances the onset of thermodiffusive instability 33 . The reactants of a hydrogen jet flame do not absorb, so this preheating route is absent. The high diffusivity of hydrogen adds differential diffusion, whose effect on the onset and the limit of the flicker is examined here with a differential-diffusion flamelet closure 34 .Here, we show that gas radiation sets the gravity at which a co-flowing hydrogen jet diffusion flame stops flickering. We study the flame whose flicker Dennis et al. 35 measured with femtosecond coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (fs-CARS), combining flamelet-based simulations with local linear stability analysis. Radiation acts on the flicker through two mechanisms. It reduces the buoyancy of the hot outer layer in proportion to the residence time of the gas, and it damps the flicker wave: gas that the wave pushes outward across the lean flank of the flame radiates more than the gas it replaces and loses part of the buoyancy that drives the wave. We call this second mechanism displaced-emission damping; freezing the radiative source separates it from the first. Both mechanisms slow the spreading of disturbances upstream through the hot layer, and the competition between this spreading and the co-flow sets where the flicker begins. Because the spreading speed of the adiabatic flame scales with the square root of gravity, while its radiative reduction does not, the limit of the flicker at reduced gravity becomes a straight line relating the square root of the co-flow Froude number to a radiation number. Calibrated on the simulations, this line predicted the outcome of two flames computed afterwards. Beyond the limit, transient wave packets can mimic flicker within the few seconds of a drop-tower experiment.

> B. Flow equations and thermochemical closures

The flow is described by the variable-density Navier–Stokes equations in the low-Mach-number limit, in cylindrical coordinates ( x, r, θ ) with gravity acting in the −x direction, so that buoyancy enters through the density field. Combustion is represented by a steady flamelet model 36 : a transport equation is solved for the mixture fraction ξ, and the density, temperature, viscosity, and diffusivity are read from flamelet tables. When radiative heat loss is included, a second scalar carries the enthalpy defect (Section II C). Two thermochemical closures are used. The unity-Lewis-number closure (ULe) is used for all radiat-ing simulations and for the series that locate the limit of the flicker, because the present enthalpy-defect formulation assumes equal diffusivities of the mixture fraction and the enthalpy. At normal gravity, the differential-diffusion closure DD-eff and ULe are compared over a range of co-flow velocities, and the adia-batic flames at 0.05 and 0 .1 g0 are repeated with DD-eff to show how differential diffusion changes the onset and the limit of the flicker. The tables are generated from steady counterflow diffusion flames of hydrogen against air at a strain rate of a = 50 s −1, with both streams at 300 K and 0.993 atm, computed with OPPDIF 37 and the GRI-Mech 3.0 mechanism without its nitrogen chemistry 38 . For ULe, all species diffuse with the thermal diffusivity α, and the table is parameterized by the mixture fraction, which is transported with α. For DD-eff, the flamelets are computed with mixture-averaged diffusion without thermal diffusion (the Soret effect). Mixture fractions defined from different elements then no longer coincide, and the table is parameterized by Bilger’s mixture fraction ξB39 , which is transported with its effective diffusivity Deff (ξB) obtained from the flamelet solutions, 1.77 times α at stoichiometry ( ξst = 0 .0284). Two further closures and the effect of the strain rate of the tables are examined in the supplementary material. 4

> D. Numerical procedure and simulation cases

The equations are solved with a finite-difference code originally developed by Pierce and Moin 46 and extended for the present study. It uses second-order central differences for the momentum equations, the QUICK scheme for the scalars, and a second-order semi-implicit time integration at an axial Courant number of 1.0 (supplementary material). At Re = 347, the flow is laminar and resolved by the grid: the dynamic Smagorinsky model retained in the code yields an eddy viscosity below 1.3% of the molecular viscosity in 99% of the cells. At normal gravity, the domain extends over 30 d in the axial and 10 d in the radial direction, with 144 × 108 cells in x and r (minimum spacing 0 .034 d and 0 .014 d) and four azimuthal planes. Both streams enter with top-hat velocity profiles, and convective conditions are applied at the outlet and at the lateral boundary. At reduced gravity, the flames become longer and wider, and their oscillation can recede far downstream. These flames are computed on a domain of 94 d × 30 d (240 × 162 cells), with the co-flow confined to r ≤ 16 d

as in the experiment and a quiescent inlet beyond (Fig. 1b). The grid and the domain were checked by doubling the resolution, by using 8 or 16 azimuthal planes, and by enlarging the domain to 62 d in length or 30 d in width at normal gravity and to 158 d or 45 d at reduced gravity. These changes alter the frequency of a sustained oscillation by at most 3% and the mean flame position by at most 0 .03 d. Apart from two domain effects discussed in Sections III B and III E, they do not change whether an oscillation is sustained or recedes. The speed at which an oscillation recedes changes by at most 5% with the domain. Doubling the radial resolution matters more: the flame with χ = 0 .75 at 0 .1 g0, continued on the finer grid, recedes 26% faster. On 16 planes, the reduced-gravity flames stay axisymmetric, even when seeded with inflow noise (supplementary material). The simulations are listed in Table I. E. Characterization of the oscillation 

The oscillation is characterized by the outer flame edge R(x, t ), the largest radius of the 1000 K isotherm in the diametral plane. Its local swing is obtained by subtracting from R its running mean over one period and taking the peak-to-peak value of the remainder in windows of 1.25 periods. The onset of the oscillation, 

xon , is the most upstream position at which the swing exceeds 0 .25 d in the last 0.5 s of a simulation; during a transient, the most upstream position at which this threshold is exceeded at a given time is called the trailing front. The flicker frequency is obtained at normal gravity from the zero crossings of the temperature time series at the measurement points, and at reduced gravity from the autocorrelation of R. Times are given in s or in units of d/U 0 = 0 .65 ms. The state of a simulation is decided from the swing in successive 0.5 s windows within x ≤ L−10 d, L being the length of the domain; where the co-flow has been changed, only windows after it has been re-established are used (supplementary material). The criteria below are applied in the order lost, receding, and sustained; the first one met determines the state. The oscillation is lost when its swing stays below 0 .1d everywhere in the last window, or falls by more than 30% per window over the last 1 s. It is receding when its onset moves downstream by more than 2 d over the last 2 s. It is sustained when its largest swing exceeds 0 .25 d in each of the last two windows and changes by less than 15% between them, its onset moving by less than 2 d. A simulation that meets none of these criteria is undecided. Radiation changes the temperature field directly, so the oscillation at reduced gravity is also followed in quantities that radiation changes only through the flow: the outer radius of the stoichiometric surface 

ξ = ξst , the peak azimuthal vorticity outside the temperature maximum, in the outer shear layer, and the radial velocity at r = 5 d. The hot outer layer (hereafter the hot layer) is the gas heated by the flame and 6accelerated by buoyancy outside the temperature peak. It is characterized by ∆ U , the difference between the time-averaged axial velocities at the radius of peak temperature and in the co-flow at r = 12 d, and by the ratio ∆ U/U co , both defined where the temperature peak lies off the axis. The time-averaged profiles of this layer are the base states of the stability analysis.  

> F. Local linear stability analysis

Local stability analysis relates the profiles of the hot layer to the growth of disturbances and to their upstream spreading. Under the parallel-flow approximation, the base state at each axial station consists of the radial profiles of the axial velocity U (r), the density ρ(r), and the viscosity μ(r): the time-averaged profiles for oscillating flames, and the profiles of the steady flame for flames without flicker. Two models are used. A simplified model describes the hydrodynamics of the hot layer; it gives its absolute or convective character and the speed at which disturbances spread upstream. A complete linearization of the flamelet model adds the dilatation, the coupling of the density to the composition, and the radiative source with its response to the perturbation. The radiative response is switched on and off on the same fixed flows and mode branch, and the difference measures how it changes the growth rates and the spreading speed. Only this difference is used: the terms it adds change the absolute instability of the layer more than the radiative response does, so the complete linearization does not decide whether a layer is absolutely unstable (supplementary material). Whether an oscillation is sustained is decided by the simulations. > B. Radiative suppression and recovery of flicker at reduced gravity

Reducing gravity changes the role of radiation. After a step reduction of gravity, the radiating flame loses its flicker at 0 .1 g0, the adiabatic flame not until 0 .05 g0 (Table II). As gravity decreases, the flame widens and lengthens (Fig. 4): at 0 .05 g0, the outer 1000 K radius at x/d = 30 grows from 2 .6d to about 5 d, and the stoichiometric contour reaches the axis at x/d = 84–94. The oscillation responds to the step in two stages (Fig. 5a, b, d, e). The normal-gravity flicker near the burner decays first, and its last bulges, together with the disturbance created by the step, form a wave packet of longer wavelength and lower frequency that is carried downstream. Whether an oscillation then remains depends on gravity and radiation. At 0.165–0.378 g0 with radiation, and down to 0 .1 g0 without it, the trailing front of the packet stops and the oscillation settles on a limit cycle, its onset steady to within 1 d. At lower gravity, and at 0 .1 g0 with radiation, the front keeps moving downstream, at 0.4–2.1 times the co-flow velocity, and leaves a steady flame behind it. At fixed gravity and co-flow, the radiative loss alone decides whether the flame flickers (Fig. 6). When the emission is switched on in the oscillating adiabatic flame at 0 .1 g0, the oscillation recedes without stopping, at about 9 diameters per second (0 .29 Uco ) after 3 s, and after 5.9 s, no swing above 0 .25 d remains within 

x ≤ 84 d. When the emission is switched off in the radiating flame that has lost its flicker, the oscillation returns within a second, and over the last 2 s of the 8.1 s record, its onset stays at x/d = 20 .0, as in the adiabatic flame. The strength of the emission sets how fast the oscillation recedes (Fig. 6d). With half the emission,

> C. Radiative loss of buoyancy in the hot outer layer

Radiative cooling reduces the buoyancy of the hot outer layer. At reduced gravity, the radiative loss is no longer small: at 0.1–0.01 g0, the flame radiates 10.3–14.1% of the fuel power within x/d ≤ 30, about twice as much as at normal gravity, and 30–43% up to x/d = 84 (Fig. 7a). The gas stays longer in the flame: it takes about 0.05 s to pass the oscillating region at normal gravity ( x/d ≤ 16), but 0.4–0.5 s to reach x/d = 60–90 at 0 .05 g0, longer than the radiative cooling time of the products, 0.26–0.35 s. The time-averaged flows over 4–6 s after the step show how radiative cooling modifies the mean flow up to the end of the hot layer, where the temperature peak reaches the axis (Fig. 7b, c). > D. Displaced-emission damping of flame disturbances

Radiation also acts on the disturbances themselves. A flame that has lost its flicker is a linear amplifier of disturbances to its hot layer. A gravity pulse of +0 .05 g0 lasting 33.6 ms excites a wave packet in the steady radiating flame at 0 .05 g0. The packet is convected downstream, and its largest displacement increases ninefold between x/d = 20 and 84 (Fig. 8a). Behind the packet, the flame returns to its steady state within 3 s, and a pulse of half the strength produces the same packet per unit pulse, to within 3% up to x/d = 40 (Fig. 8c). Freezing the radiative source separates its response from the mean-flow cooling. > E. Upstream spreading of disturbances against the co-flow

Both radiative mechanisms lower the speed at which disturbances spread upstream through the hot layer relative to the co-flow, S = Uco − V− (Section II F). Where S is smaller than the co-flow velocity, the co-flow sweeps disturbances downstream. The oscillation therefore begins where S, rising along the layer, approaches 

Uco , and near the limit, its upstream end recedes at the speed Uco − S of the linear trailing edge. The limit itself follows the largest spreading speed, Smax , which scales with ( gd )1/2 without radiation; radiation lowers it by an amount that does not scale with gravity. The co-flow sets where the flicker begins and whether it persists, even without radiation. At normal gravity, a stronger co-flow displaces the oscillation rather than suppressing it uniformly (Fig. 11a; values in the supplementary material): it raises the frequency, reduces the swing near the burner, and moves the oscillation downstream, as observed in co-flowing methane flames 20,21 . At 0.35 m/s, the flame edge near the burner is almost steady, while further downstream it swings more than at 0.15 m/s, and at 0.7 and 0.8 m/s (Fr co = 10 .0 and 13.0), the onset settles at x/d = 27 .2 and 35.7–37.7. A measurement confined to the near field would therefore report a suppressed flicker, while the flame still oscillates further downstream, 15 consistent with the shift of the outer vortices beyond the visible flame observed at large co-flow velocities 20 .On a shorter domain of 62 d × 10 d, started from the flame at 0.5 m/s, the flames at 0.6 and 0.7 m/s lose their oscillation through the outflow boundary; these two outcomes are domain effects and are not used to locate the limit. At reduced gravity, halving the co-flow can restore the flicker of the radiating flame. At 0 .1 g0, the halved co-flow of 0.075 m/s gives a sustained oscillation from x/d = 10 .5 once the slower co-flow has crossed the flame twice; at 0 .05 g0, the oscillation that grows after the change recedes beyond x/d = 84 (Fig. 5c, f; Table II). If the velocity profile is fixed relative to the co-flow, changing the co-flow adds the same velocity to every ray velocity of the local impulse response in the laboratory frame, so that S = Uco − V− does not change: the spreading speed is a property of the layer relative to the co-flow. In a simulation, changing the co-flow also changes the velocity excess and the profile of the layer, which the analysis therefore takes from each time-averaged flow. The dotted lines in Fig. 11d mark S = Uco , above which the layer is absolutely unstable. In the flame at normal gravity and 0.7 m/s, S rises along the layer to 0.84 times the co-flow velocity at the onset; at 0 .25 g0 and 0.35 m/s, it stays 13% below the co-flow velocity, and the oscillation of this flame recedes slowly. In the steady flames at 0 .05 g0, S reaches 0.107 m/s at x/d = 60 without radiation and is still rising there; with radiation, it peaks at only 0.066 m/s near x/d = 30 and then falls as the radiating layer cools. Beyond x/d = 60, the co-flow outside these flames is no longer uniform. The local analysis places the onset where S overtakes Uco , as in methane flames at normal gravity 17,18 

(Fig. 11c). Of the thirteen oscillating flames analyzed, ten are absolutely unstable in the axisymmetric mode of the hot layer. In all ten, the upstream boundary of the absolutely unstable region lies within 3 .7d of the onset defined with 0 .1d, although the boundary itself ranges from x/d = 0 .7 to 32 with co-flow, gravity, and radiation. At this boundary, the absolute frequency, which selects the frequency of nonlinear global modes 11,48 , lies 10–20% below the simulated frequency in six of them (at normal gravity with 0.15, 0.35, and 0.5 m/s; the radiating flames at 0.165 and 0 .25 g0; the adiabatic flame at 0 .165 g0; supplementary material). Two adiabatic flames are marginal, and in the flame at normal gravity and 0.7 m/s, the closest to its limit, the layer is convectively unstable at every station upstream of the onset. In the simulations, the onset follows the velocity of the hot layer relative to the co-flow. In the sustained flames at normal gravity with co-flows of 0.15–0.5 m/s and at reduced gravity with 0.15 m/s, the oscillation begins where the velocity excess of this layer over the co-flow, ∆ U , is 4.2–6.3 times the co-flow velocity (swing 0 .25 d; 3.6–4.9 for 0 .1d), while the onset position ranges from x/d = 3 to 22. Without radiation, the limit of the flicker follows the co-flow Froude number. Every adiabatic flame with Fr co ≤ 5.1 sustains its oscillation, and at normal gravity the oscillation persists up to at least Fr co = 13 .0; it recedes slowly at 0 .25 g0 with Fr co = 10 .0 and is lost at 0 .05 g0 with Fr co = 9 .2 (Fig. 12a). > G. Transient flicker and finite observation times

Beyond its limit, a flame does not lose its oscillation at once. The oscillation that follows a reduction of gravity is a packet of the kind that the gravity pulses excite, and the flame loses it first near the burner and then progressively farther downstream. Near the burner ( x/d ≤ 30, about the length of the flame at normal gravity), the flames are steady within 1.0–1.8 s of the step, whereas further downstream the packet keeps the flame oscillating for seconds (Fig. 5a, d). At 0 .05 g0, the packet needs about 2.5 s to leave a flame about 0.45 m long. While it passes, the packet oscillates at a frequency close to that of a sustained flicker at the same gravity, 4.53 Hz at 0 .1 g0 with radiation (Table II): a familiar flicker frequency does not show that the flame sustains the flicker. Three questions must therefore be kept apart: whether the reduced-gravity flame sustains the flicker, how long the packet needs to leave the flame, and how long the observation lasts. Drop towers provide 2.2–9.3 s of microgravity 24,50,51 . A short drop-tower record of the whole flame can therefore capture a transient wave packet even when the flame does not sustain flicker, whereas an observation confined to the near field would 20 show a steady flame. Reports of reduced-gravity flicker from simulations are likewise difficult to interpret without the length of the record 27 . In a 2.2 s drop tower, the surface of a hydrogen jet flame became steady, while the surrounding flow was still evolving at the end of the drop 52 . The transient times computed here belong to this flame and to a step reduction of gravity, but the distinction between convected wave packets and sustained flicker applies to any short reduced-gravity record.  

> IV. CONCLUSIONS

Gas radiation raises the gravity below which a co-flowing hydrogen jet diffusion flame can no longer sustain flicker. In the simulations, the radiating flame loses its flicker between 0.1 and 0 .165 g0, the adiabatic flame only between 0.05 and 0 .1 g0. At the co-flow of the experiment, the calibrated criterion places the critical gravity at 0.13 instead of 0.05 times normal gravity, a 2.6-fold increase, and the increase is at least twofold with the net radiative loss estimated with reabsorption. At one tenth of normal gravity, the emission alone decides whether the flame flickers, and this net loss still removes the flicker. A loss that barely changes the flicker at normal gravity thus controls it at reduced gravity. Radiation acts through two mechanisms whose rates do not fall with gravity. It removes buoyancy from the hot outer layer in proportion to the residence time of the gas, and it damps the disturbances them-selves on the lean flank of the flame, where the emission moves with the gas that the wave displaces. This displaced-emission damping is the gas-radiation counterpart of the Newtonian cooling of internal gravity waves. Freezing the radiative source separates the two. Retaining the mean radiative cooling while sup-pressing the response of the source reduces the speed at which the oscillation recedes at one tenth of normal gravity to less than a tenth of that with the responding source, and the flame stays near the limit of its flicker. Cooling of the mean flow brings the flame close to its limit, and the damping of the disturbances carries it beyond. Both mechanisms lower one quantity, the speed at which disturbances spread upstream through the hot layer against the co-flow. The onset lies where this spreading balances the advection by the co-flow, and near the limit, the oscillation recedes like a pulled front, at the speed of the linear trailing edge. Without radiation, the spreading speed scales with ( gd )1/2, which orders the onsets and the adiabatic limits by the co-flow Froude number; radiation lowers it by an amount that does not scale with gravity. At reduced gravity, the limit of the flicker is therefore the straight line Fr 1/2co ,c = c − Bχ Π, in which the radiation number Π compares the buoyant time with the radiative time scale of the products. The two scalings fix its form, and the simulations fix its constants. Once calibrated, the line predicted on which side of the limit two new flames lie. The gravity at which a co-flowing jet flame stops flickering is thus set by the co-flow and the radiative loss rather than by the flame alone, and for a high-velocity jet from a small nozzle, it need not lie near zero gravity. If the radiative constant grows with the length of the hot layer, radiation also weighs more on larger flames. Beyond its limit, a flame still amplifies disturbances into wave packets that take seconds to leave it and oscillate at the familiar flicker frequency, so that an oscillation seen during the few seconds of a drop-tower test need not be flicker that the reduced-gravity flame sustains. These conclusions rest on one burner and jet velocity, unity Lewis numbers, and an optically thin emission at frozen composition; with differential diffusion, the adiabatic limit stays within the same gravity interval. Other nozzles and jet velocities, radiating flames with differential diffusion, and reduced-gravity experiments with a controlled co-flow would test the constants of the relation. Where low gravity leaves the buoyant spreading of disturbances only slightly faster than the co-flow, gas radiation decides whether the flame flickers.

S4. LOCAL STABILITY ANALYSIS 

Saddle points of the hot-layer branch are located by Newton iteration and retained only if they are pinch points by the Briggs–Bers criterion; the trailing-edge velocity V− follows by continuation along rays from the absolute saddle. The complete linearization of Section II F of the article also perturbs all transport terms, and its radiative source-response term is −ερ (qξ ξ′ + qhh′), with ε = 1 for the responding and ε = 0 for the frozen source; the base sink acting on the density perturbation is kept in both cases. Its coupling terms change the stability far more than the radiative response: with the dilatation and the composition coupling, no absolutely unstable region remains in the flickering flames at 0.165 and 0 .25 g0, and the absolute frequency at normal gravity falls to 6.1–6.8 Hz, against the simulated 14.5 Hz. The simplified model of Eqs. (2)–(4), which locates the onsets and gives the frequency to within 20% (Section S9), is therefore used for the absolute and convective character of the flows, and the complete linearization only for the change caused by the radiative response.

S8. GRAVITY PULSES AND RADIATIVE DAMPING 

Each pulsed flame is compared with the same flame without the pulse, computed on the same grid partition, so that the difference is the response to the pulse. At 0 .1 g0, the oscillation of the frozen-source flame meets the criterion of a sustained oscillation in the last windows of its 12.1 s record (largest swing +0% over the last second, onset +0 .7d over the last 2 s). Over the last 6 s, however, its onset recedes from x/d = 42 .3 to 31 45.7, and its swing falls from 1.5 to 0 .9d at x/d = 60 and from 4.5 to 3 .6d at its largest, at x/d = 83 .7. Without the pulse, the flame stays steady, with swings of 0 .064 d or less. Upstream of the onset, this flame keeps the hot layer of the radiating flame: density, velocity jump, and radius at the temperature peak agree to within 0.06%. In the complete linearization, the radiative response lowers the absolute growth rate by 0.34–0.55 s −1 in six flames between g0 and 0 .05 g0. Its local contributions partly cancel because they differ in phase, leaving 32–49% of their summed magnitude. S10. CALIBRATION OF THE LIMIT RELATION 

The constants of Eq. (9) of the article are calibrated on the 27 unity-Lewis-number cases computed before the test at 0 .165 g0: 16 sustained, which should lie at Uc/U co ≥ 1, and 7 lost and 4 receding, which should lie at Uc/U co ≤ 1. A parameter pair ( c, b ) satisfies a tolerance τ when every sustained case lies at Uc/U co ≥ 1−τ

and every lost or receding case at Uc/U co ≤ 1 + τ . The selected constants, c = 3 .05 and b = 0 .09 m/s, place the sustained cases at Uc/U co ≥ 0.965 and the others at Uc/U co ≤ 1.007, so that τ = 3 .5%; the band of Fig. 12b of the article marks the interval 0.965–1.007 in which the two groups overlap. Every pair that satisfies the same tolerance has c = 3 .05–3.13 and b = 0 .084–0.106 m/s. The receding flames constrain b:without them, the pairs within this tolerance reach b = 0 .059–0.106 m/s. 33
