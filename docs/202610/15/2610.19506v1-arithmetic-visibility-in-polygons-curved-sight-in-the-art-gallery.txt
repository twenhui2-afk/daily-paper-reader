# Arithmetic Visibility in Polygons: Curved Sight in the Art Gallery ∗

## Malik Barrett 

Abstract 

In the classical art gallery problem, a guard sees a target when the segment joining them lies entirely within the polygon. We explore what changes when sight may bend along regular arcs on absolutely irreducible curves, but only those defined by primitive integer polynomials of total degree at most d and coefficient height at most H. We call such curves admissible sight carriers. Arcs can dramatically improve sight. In a one-sided comb, no straight-line guard sees selected targets in two different teeth, yet a single central guard sees a chosen target in every tooth along parabolic carriers. Sight can also fail in two different ways. Geometrically, the polygon may block every sight arc of the allowed degree between two points. For instance, in an alternating comb, the number of guards needed for a row of targets spanning the comb grows linearly with the number of sides at each fixed degree, even for carriers with arbitrary real coefficients. Arithmetically, no admissible carrier may pass through the points at all. Even in the unit square, at fixed d and H,the proportion of rational targets that must guard themselves tends to one as the height bound grows. An algebraic target may lie on no rational curve of degree at most d, however large H is. Both failures can be overcome for finite target sets by allowing the degree and coefficient height to grow. We show that every finite set of algebraic targets in a rational polygon becomes visible from any rational interior guard, with explicit bounds. We also construct, for every d,arbitrarily large Galois orbits in the unit square, all of projective height 4 d+1 , whose points must each guard themselves at degree d for every H. Yet at degree d + 1 and coefficient height one, a single guard sees the whole orbit along Z(Y − Xd+1 ).

# 1 Introduction 

In the classical art gallery problem a guard in a polygonal “gallery” sees a target when the segment joining them stays inside the polygon. One asks for the minimum number of guards needed to see the whole gallery, its guard number . Chv´ atal’s theorem, in Fisk’s proof, then gives ⌊n/ 3⌋ vertex guards for a simple n-gon [13, 17]; see also [27, Theorem 1.1]. We ask what becomes of the guard number if sight is allowed to bend, but only along curves that can be written down with a bounded amount of arithmetic information. The question was prompted by the comb galleries of [15, Section 1.4, Figure 1.17] and by the edge-guarding question posed there as Unsolved Problem 5.

Arithmetic thus enters the sight rule itself, and not only the choice of guard locations, which is where it has usually entered the art gallery literature (see Related sight rules). Morally, d measures how much a sight arc may bend, while H measures how much arithmetic information it may carry. Throughout, the question is the classical one: how few guards suffice to see a given set of targets (Definition 2.4) within a given gallery? Our choice of rational coefficients makes the art gallery problem arithmetic. A rational polynomial vanishing at an algebraic target also vanishes at every conjugate of that target, so choosing a carrier through one point imposes conditions on its whole Galois orbit. However, a polynomial over the target field need not vanish at those conjugates; at degree one such polynomials would simply recover segment visibility (Remark 2.6). 

Sight requirements. Sight, in our sense, has to satisfy two requirements: an admissible polynomial must vanish at the guard and the target, and a regular arc on its real curve must join them inside the polygon. The first requirement is arithmetic and the second is geometric. Regarding the second requirement, comb-shaped galleries demonstrate why straight-line sight can require many guards: separate teeth obscure different parts of the gallery. In a one-sided comb we will exhibit targets such that no two can be seen by a single straight-line guard, yet a single central guard sees them all along parabolic carriers. Curved sight does not always overcome geometric obstructions, however. If the notches of the comb are deep and alternating, a sight arc between suitably placed targets must repeatedly cross a fixed line, and a carrier of degree d has at most d such intersections (see Figure 8). At fixed degree, the guard number therefore grows linearly with the number of sides. This limitation holds even for real coefficients, with no coefficient bound; the obstruction is geometric. Regarding the first requirement, consider the unit square. Since every segment joining two of its points stays inside it, a single straight-line guard sees the whole square. Any increase in the guard number there is thus arithmetic, and arises because the degree and coefficient height are fixed. In fact, almost all rational points, ordered by height, must then guard themselves. For algebraic targets, increasing the coefficient-height budget alone may never help. The sharpest instance of this is proved at the end of the paper (Theorem 9.1): 

Theorem A. For every d ≥ 1 and every M ≥ 1 there is a Galois-stable set T of more than M

algebraic points in the interior of the unit square, all of projective height 4d+1 , such that every point of T must guard itself at degree d, at every coefficient height, whereas at degree d + 1 and coefficient height one a single point of T sees all of T .

Main results and plan of the paper. After setting up the model (Section 2) and the combi-natorics of finite target sets (Section 3), our first comparisons investigate how much of classical guarding the new sight rule retains (Section 4). We recover the classical theory restricted to rational points when we consider degree-one carriers with unrestricted coefficient height. Similarly, for any rational polygon, degree-one edge guarding of rational targets recovers the classical minimum once the target-height bound is sufficiently large, with coefficient height linear in the target height. Edge guarding of quadratic targets turns out to be purely combinatorial. As each nonrational quadratic point lies on exactly one rational line, the degree-one edge guard number is the size of a minimum 2vertex cover of a graph on the polygon’s edges determined by its chords, whatever the quadratic field. As with point guards, the edge guard number for a given target set can collapse with an increase in degree: in a convex n-gon, guarding all quadratic targets requires n − 1 edges at degree one but only one at degree two, with coefficient height unrestricted in both cases. We then use the combs described above to demonstrate both the advantages and the limitations of curved sight in arithmetic guarding. Restricting our attention to an L-shaped gallery, we compare the effects of degree, guard placement, and coefficient field (Section 5). Rational degree-two carriers reduce the edge guard number for quadratic targets from four to one, even in this nonconvex gallery. Furthermore, we construct rational conics joining a fixed guard outside the classical kernel to an explicit family of quadratic targets. If carrier coefficients are instead allowed in a fixed real quadratic field K, the same guard sees every K-point target at degree two. We then examine the arithmetic restrictions on their own. Increasing height can overcome the scarcity of carriers through rational targets (Section 6), but not the absence of a rational polynomial relation of an allowed degree through an algebraic target (Section 7). Allowing both bounds to grow permits every algebraic target in a rational polygon to be joined to any chosen rational interior guard by the arithmetic arc lifting theorem. If the target set is finite, then this can be done for some finite budget d, H . An effective refinement of arithmetic arc lifting gives coarse explicit degree and height bounds from interpolation data and estimates controlling how much the sight arc may be perturbed while remaining regular and inside the polygon (Theorems 8.2 and 8.3). Mere computability would already follow by enumerating carriers and testing sight; the formulas instead quantify the required degree and height. Finally, Section 9 proves Theorem A, which shows that a degree jump from d to 

d + 1 can reduce an arbitrarily large guard number to one, with target height independent of the orbit size. We end with questions suggested by the examples (Section 10). Longer proofs are collected in the appendices; the main text is meant to be readable without them. 

Related sight rules. Arithmetic restrictions were previously examined in the classical art gallery problem. Requiring rational guard coordinates can increase the minimum number of guards [1, Theorem 1] and [26, Theorem 1]. Other work restricts guard locations to a grid [30], or studies the computational difficulty of finding a minimum set of point guards [2]. In these problems, sight still follows straight segments. Changing the sight rule has also been studied. Circular and conic visibility allow arcs from particular families of curves [12, 18], while conic link paths allow several conic pieces and minimize the number needed to connect two points [11]. By contrast, in our model each sight arc must lie on a single absolutely irreducible curve, though different targets may use different carriers. Rather than fixing a family of circles or conics, we bound the degree and coefficient height of its primitive integer polynomial. There is a related arithmetic literature on visibility along polynomial graphs [21, 10, 25]. There, sight follows specified polynomial families, and an intervening lattice point can block visibility. Our targets do not block sight. Instead, arithmetic restricts which carriers are available, and the polygon restricts which connecting arcs can be used. # 3 Computability and finite target sets 

Before turning to particular galleries we observe some basic combinatorial structure underlying finite target sets in galleries. When guards are chosen from the finite target set itself, guarding becomes a domination problem on its visibility graph. At finite budgets, the carrier list is also finite, which will let us compute visibility exactly. To that end, for a finite target set T define the visibility graph 

Vis d,H (P, T ) as the graph on T in which distinct targets are adjacent exactly when they see each other. We recall that a dominating set of a graph is a set of vertices such that every vertex lies in it or is adjacent to one of its elements, and the domination number is the least size of a dominating set. 

Remark 3.1. The connected components of the regular traces of all admissible carriers define a hypergraph on T : its hyperedges are their nonempty intersections with T . This records which targets lie together on one regular component of one carrier. The visibility graph is the 2-section of this hypergraph, although a clique need not lie on one regular component of a single carrier. ♢

Proposition 3.2. 

(i) Visibility is reflexive and symmetric, but need not be transitive. (ii) For a finite target set T , the domination number of Vis d,H (P, T ) is γd,H (P ; T, T ).(iii) If d ≤ d′, H ≤ H′, T ′ ⊆ T , and G ⊆ G′, then 

γd′,H ′ (P ; T ′, G ′) ≤ γd,H (P ; T, G ).

Proof. Reflexivity and symmetry are immediate from Definition 2.2, and the H = 1 graph in Figure 3 shows that transitivity can fail. A target sees exactly itself and its neighbors in the visibility graph, so the covers of T by guards from T are exactly the dominating sets. This gives (ii). # 4 Straight-line visibility and degree-constrained guarding 

Our first task is to check that the model does not lose the classical theory. At degree one and sufficiently large height on a rational polygon, it should reduce to rational straight-line guarding. Once this is settled, the interesting questions are what curved sight adds, and what the arithmetic of the targets takes away. We therefore compare straight-line guarding with curved sight in a one-sided comb, and then with guarding all points over a quadratic field, first by point guards and then by edge guards. Finally, alternating notches will show why degree still limits both point and edge guards. 

## 4.1 Straight-line sight and a one-sided comb 

Proposition 4.1. Distinct points g, t ∈ P (Q) of projective heights at most A, B have a primitive joining-line equation of height at most 2AB . ## 4.2 From finite targets to quadratic galleries 

We now replace a finite target set by all K-points, keeping rational carrier coefficients. At degree one, this arithmetic change can outweigh the polygon’s geometry, even in a convex gallery. 

Proposition 4.5. For a real number field K̸ = Q,

γ1,∞(P ; P (K), P ) = ∞.

Proof. Consider a finite guard set and any interior rectangle within the gallery. # 9 Sight at the next degree 

Arc lifting gives sufficient budgets, but does not determine how quickly visibility might change with increases in degree. Can the guard number change all at once when the degree increases by just one? To explore this possibility, let us return to the unit square S and consider the curve C := Z(Y − Xd+1 ).

Its points over any field K/ Q are ( a, a d+1 ), with a ∈ K. Its real trace in the square is a connected graph, and hence supplies a sight arc between any two of its points. We have now seen both sources of difficulty. A polygon can prevent a regular arc from remaining inside it; the alternating combs make this limitation persist over real coefficients at bounded degree. Arithmetic can instead exclude a suitable equation even in a square. Increasing height removes the scarcity caused by a finite carrier list, but not a target’s relation-degree barrier. Arc lifting overcomes both barriers for each finite algebraic target set, and the present example shows that a single increase in degree can already be enough, with no increase in target height as the set grows. 

# 10 Questions suggested by the examples 

The examples above leave three main questions: which visibility graphs occur, when one guard sees a whole arithmetic gallery, and how small the sight budgets can be. We keep these questions separate from possible changes to the sight rule. Of these, Problems 10.3 and 10.4, on the height scale at higher degree and on sharper effective bounds, respectively, are direct extensions of results developed above. 

## 10.1 Visibility graphs 

Problem 10.1 (Realizing arithmetic visibility graphs) . ## 10.2 Whole-gallery kernels 

At fixed degree and coefficient height, infinitely many rational targets lie on no admissible carrier. Removing both bounds lets every rational interior guard see every algebraic target. Further directions. Pairwise visibility does not record whether several targets lie on one regular component of one carrier. Keeping those component subsets as hyperedges suggests a finer realization question than the graph problem above. Changing the coefficient field, as in the L-shaped example, brings other absolute values into the height. A primitive representative with unit-ideal content need not exist over its ring of integers, so projective coefficient vectors are the natural replacement for primitive integer equations. Lemma E.5. Let S ⊂ Q2 be finite and Galois stable, with N = |S| ≥ 1. In every degree 

m ≥ max {2, 2N − 1, N + 1 }, the rational linear system through S contains a nonempty Zariski-open set of smooth projective plane curves. The discriminant restricts to a nonzero polynomial of degree 

3( m − 1) 2 on this linear system. Proof. We consider the space of all homogeneous degree-m polynomials vanishing at the projective points [ px : py : 1] for p ∈ S. Working over Q, we determine how many independent linear equations on the coefficients are required for the curve to be singular at a specified point. To that end, fix a z ∈ P2 \ S. For each point of S, choose a homogeneous linear polynomial vanishing there but not at z. The product of these polynomials has degree N and vanishes throughout 

S by construction. Furthermore, it is nonzero at z. Multiply this product by the ( m − N − 1)-st power of a homogeneous linear polynomial nonzero at z, and then by an arbitrary homogeneous linear polynomial. This produces degree-m members of the system. In local affine coordinates, the product of the N chosen linear polynomials and the additional ( m − N − 1)-st power is nonzero at 

z. Consequently, varying the final linear polynomial allows us to prescribe the value and both first partial derivatives at z independently. Requiring all three quantities to vanish therefore imposes three independent linear equations on the coefficients. At p ∈ S, use the first-jet interpolation construction from Lemma E.1 over Q which allows us to prescribe both first partial derivatives at p while keeping the values at every point of S equal to zero. The interpolants have degree at most 2 N − 1 ≤ m, whence homogenizing them to degree m

gives members of our system. Since the value at p is already zero, singularity there imposes just two independent linear conditions. Let I be the set of pairs ( F, z ) for which the first jet of F vanishes at z. This is a closed subset of the product of the coefficient space with P2. Outside S, the three conditions on F and the two dimensions available to z give a dimension bound of the dimension of the coefficient space minus one. Over the finite set S, the two conditions give a bound of the dimension of the coefficient space minus two. Thus I has dimension strictly smaller than the coefficient space. As projection by ( F, z ) 7 → F

cannot increase dimension, its image cannot fill that space. The image is also closed because P2

is projective. Its complement is therefore a nonempty Zariski-open subset of the coefficient space consisting of polynomials whose projective zero sets are smooth. The discriminant consequently restricts to a nonzero polynomial on this coefficient space. It is homogeneous of degree 3( m − 1) 2, so its restriction has the same degree; see [19, Chapter 13, §1D, pp. 433–434]. It remains to perturb the approximation to a smooth projective curve without losing the sight arc.
