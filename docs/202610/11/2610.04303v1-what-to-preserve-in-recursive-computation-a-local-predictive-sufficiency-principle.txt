Abstract 

Recursive computation repeatedly compresses or reuses intermediate states, creating a simple tension: infor-mation that must remain useful across longer recursive paths is also exposed to more opportunities for loss before reaching the final prediction. Existing reconstruction or local-prediction objectives provide tractable supervision, but do not ensure that the retained information remains sufficient for subsequent recursive computation. We identify local predictive sufficiency with recursive predictive closure: controlling local predictive deficiencies at individual interfaces controls the resulting discrepancy at the root. We then turn this principle into a tractable training proce-dure. Starting from a variational characterization, we derive finite predictive tests and an empirical predictive defi-ciency that measures predictive value retained across compression. Its predictive sensitivities define margin-relaxed half-space constraints on parameter updates, and we project the host optimizer’s proposed update onto their inter-section only when predictive preservation would otherwise be violated. Across temporal graphs, language memory, vision-language-action control, and recursive self-improvement, the method matches or improves the corresponding host models under matched compression budgets, with larger gains under heavier recursive or memory demands, while better preserving predictive information across successive transformations. Crucially, the same task-agnostic predictive-preservation principle is instantiated across all four settings through host-compatible interventions while keeping the endpoint task, backbone, and evaluation protocol fixed. These results establish predictive preservation at recursive interfaces as a general training principle for recursive compression. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recursive computation is widely used across modern learning systems, including graph and tree-structured propaga-tion (Goller and K¨ uchler, 1996; Scarselli et al., 2009; Tai et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025), recurrent context compression, embodied memory, and recursive self-improvement (Kim et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). Despite their different implementations, these systems share a common structure: information is repeatedly summarized into an intermediate state, passed through a recursive interface, and reused by subsequent computation. Earlier information therefore undergoes more rounds of aggregation and compression, and long-range information must pass through more recursive interfaces before affecting the final prediction. Thus, the information that must remain useful over longer paths is exposed to more opportunities for loss along the way. Compression may attenuate information (Alon and Yahav, 2021; Topping et al., 2022; Banerjee et al., 2022). At each recursive interface, the model therefore faces a concrete question: what should the intermediate state retain? Reconstruction objectives encourage it to preserve information needed to recover its input (Socher et al., 2011), while predictive-state approaches represent state through predictions of future observations (Littman et al., 2001; Kulesza et al., 2015; Hefny et al., 2018). Information-theoretic criteria characterize predictive information more directly (Tishby et al., 1999; Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015; Aguerri and Zaidi, 2018; Poole et al., 2019), but are difficult to estimate and optimize for high-dimensional intermediate states. Recursive computation therefore calls for a tractable criterion that specifies what each interface should preserve for subsequent prediction. 

Local Predictive Sufficiency. A natural approach is to ask every intermediate state to preserve information about the final prediction. Yet the states most exposed to repeated compression are also those for which the final target is sep-arated from the recursive interface by more subsequent transformations, making this preservation signal increasingly 1arXiv:2610.04303v1 [cs.LG] 3 Oct 2026 indirect (Polyanskiy and Wu, 2017). We instead assess preservation where compression occurs. At each recursive interface, we ask whether the compressed state retains the predictive information available before compression for the adjacent predictive pair at that interface. We call this property local predictive sufficiency . Under the recursive model in Sec. 2, recursive predictive closure connects these local conditions to protection of the root target. We then derive an empirical predictive deficiency that approximates the predictive value lost across compression. In projection-based instantiations, the optimizer first produces a task-driven proposal, and predictive feasibility projection modifies that proposal only when it violates the margin-relaxed predictive constraints. The broader framework changes how information is retained at recursive interfaces while leaving the endpoint task and evaluation protocol unchanged. We evaluate the framework in four settings where recursive state compression arises naturally: temporal graphs, language memory, vision-language-action control, and recursive self-improvement. Across these settings, preserving predictive structure at recursive interfaces consistently improves downstream performance, with larger gains when relevant information must survive longer recursive chains. On DailyDialog, our method consistently improves over the corresponding CCM-merge host across the tested memory depths. On LIBERO-Mem T3, it increases full-task success from 1.7 ± 2.9% to 11 .7 ± 2.9% across three independently trained seeds. Similar depth-dependent gains appear in temporal graphs and across recursive self-improvement iterations. Mechanism analyses further show slower attenuation of the predictive statistic across successive recursive interfaces. Our contributions are threefold. (1) Theory. We formulate local predictive sufficiency and establish recursive predictive closure and root predictive stability. (2) Learning. We derive an empirical predictive deficiency and use its predictive sensitivities to constrain task proposals that would degrade predictive structure across recursive interfaces. 

(3) Evaluation. We validate the principle in four recursive settings and show that its benefits become more pronounced as information must survive longer recursive chains. Together, these contributions provide a common view of what recursive intermediate states should preserve and a practical mechanism for protecting it during learning.

Proposition 3 provides a quantitative local-to-global guarantee. It shows that the change in the root predictive law is controlled by the local predictive deficiencies at the individual recursive interfaces, so small local predictive defi-ciencies imply a small global predictive discrepancy. When every local predictive deficiency vanishes, the compressed computation preserves the root target exactly, recovering the conclusion of Proposition 1. This guarantee concerns the population-level local predictive deficiencies and applies when its stated local predictive deficiencies are controlled. The local calibration in Corollary F.1 requires additional test-coverage, readout, context-localization, and independent-sample conditions; the projection bounds only a first-order change in the pooled training statistic. Appendix F.4 further shows that the projected updates bound the finite-horizon change of the pooled empirical predictive deficiency, up to the relaxation margin and residual higher-order terms. Combined with the local calibration above, this connects the quantity controlled during training to the population deficiencies in Proposition 3; details are given in the appendix. The complete proof is given in Appendix F.5.

# 4 Experiments 

Datasets and tasks. We evaluate our method across four recursive settings. For temporal graph learning, we use Wikipedia (Kumar et al., 2019) for node classification and UCI (Panzarasa et al., 2009) for link prediction, with propagation depths controlling recursive neighborhood expansion. For language memory, we use DailyDialog (Li et al., 2017) for response prediction across increasing conversation histories and Multi-Session Chat (MSC) (Xu et al., 2022) for long-term response prediction across increasing session histories. For vision-language-action control, we use LIBERO-Mem (Chung et al., 2026) to evaluate memory during multi-stage manipulation under the T3 setting. For each training run, checkpoint selection uses an internal 20-demonstration development split from the released refined training data; the selected checkpoint is then frozen and evaluated on a separate set of 20 official held-out evaluation initializations. For SRI, we use a six-task cohort from CO-Bench (Sun et al., 2026) as a mechanism-level evaluation of predictive preservation, where optimization candidates are recursively generated and refined from earlier candidates and execution feedback, and retained history conditions subsequent strategy updates. Detailed dataset statistics and matched evaluation protocols across all four domains are provided in Appendix C. 

Models and baselines. Across all four domains, we instantiate our method on a representative recursive model: TGN (Rossi et al., 2020) for temporal graphs, CCM-merge (Kim et al., 2024) for language memory, MemoryVLA (Shi et al., 2026) for vision-language-action control, and Meta n (Kim et al., 2026) for SRI. For temporal graphs, we compare against TGN (Rossi et al., 2020), TGAT (Xu et al., 2020), TCL (Wang et al., 2021), CTAN (Gravina et al., 2024), and DyGFormer (Yu et al., 2023). For language memory, we compare directly against the corresponding CCM-merge host under the same backbone, memory depth, and evaluation protocol. For vision-language-action control, we compare against MemoryVLA (Shi et al., 2026) and report prior-work results for π0, SlotVLA, and E-SlotSSM on the same LIBERO-Mem T3 benchmark as contextual references. For SRI, we compare against Meta n (Kim et al., 2026) with the same backbone, task cohort, recursive search procedure, and optimization budget. Main Findings. As summarized in Table 1, our method consistently improves the corresponding host across four substantially different recursive systems by modifying only how intermediate information is retained. A particularly informative pattern emerges as recursive dependence becomes more demanding. In language memory, the improve-ment over the compressed host is largest at longer histories, where information must survive repeated compression. In temporal graphs, although our TGN-based method trails the strong DyGFormer baseline at shallower propagation on UCI, this gap closes as propagation deepens, reaching comparable AP and higher AUC at the deepest setting. The VLA and SRI results extend this observation beyond explicit depth axes: the same preservation mechanism re-mains effective in long-horizon closed-loop execution and recursive strategy refinement, where retained information is reused in later computation. Complete depth- and history-resolved results are provided in Appendices D.1 and D.2, with additional long-horizon VLA and recursive-strategy analyses in Appendices D.3 and D.4. 

(1) Are the end-task gains consistent with the predictive-preservation mechanism? End-task improvements alone do not identify why the method works. We therefore use Fig. 4 as a mechanism-consistency analysis. In temporal graphs, Fig. 4(a) shows that our method retains stronger finite-test predictive value J across recursive interfaces than TGN and task-only training, including predictive information originating from deeper sources. In SRI, Fig. 4(b) shows that information retained from earlier executions remains more useful when reused during subsequent strategy refinement. The same association also appears during language-memory training: Appendix D.2, Fig. 6, shows that the predictive-preservation signal ∆J and validation PPL improvement evolve broadly in the same direction over the training trajectory. These diagnostics do not directly estimate the population deficiency δv ; together, they provide mechanism-level evidence linking task gains to stronger predictive preservation and downstream reuse. 

(2) Does the optimization actually enforce the intended preservation constraint? The mechanism diagnostics above characterize the learned representations, but do not by themselves show whether the constraint is active during optimization. Appendix D.1, Fig. 5, directly examines this behavior: unconstrained task proposals continue to violate a nonzero fraction of the grouped predictive constraints throughout training, although the violation density decreases as optimization progresses. Thus, the projection is not vacuous; task optimization continues to propose directions that can interfere with predictive preservation. Moreover, Table 9 shows that the constrained first-order quantity closely tracks the actual change in the corresponding window-level predictive statistic, validating the local bridge used by the projection. Table 2 further separates this constrained update from task-only training, an additive predictive-gradient update, and a single global predictive constraint, while the complete controlled ablations are reported in Appendix E.7. Their degradation relative to the full method supports the role of interface-wise constrained optimization beyond merely adding predictive supervision. 

Ablation studies. Table 2 isolates the main design choices under the same UCI depth-3 setting. Task-Only Train-ing controls for the shared host, compressor, and trainable parameterization, testing whether additional learnability alone explains the improvement. Direct Future Prediction asks whether a conventional future-prediction objective can replace the proposed predictive criterion, while the Single-Event Predictive Criterion directly tests the adjacent predictive pair Sv underlying our theoretical formulation. Additive Predictive-Gradient Update uses the same pre-dictive supervision as an auxiliary gradient, separating the contribution of predictive supervision from its constrained parameter-space realization. Global Predictive Constraint further tests whether an aggregated constraint can replace the interface-wise structure used by our method. Across these controls, the observed performance degradations are substantial, supporting the respective roles of the predictive criterion, adjacent predictive pair, and constrained update. Additional ablations on statistical realization and supervision alignment are reported in Appendix E.7.

# 5 Conclusion 

We study how to learn compact states for recursive computation without losing information required by prediction. Our approach builds on local predictive deficiency and recursive predictive closure, and uses predictive feasibility projection to constrain the task proposal. Across temporal graphs, language memory, vision-language-action control, and recursive self-improvement, the method improves host models under matched compression budgets. Mechanistic analyses show preservation of predictive information from deeper sources, while ablations confirm the roles of align-ment, conditioning, and constrained optimization. These results support predictive preservation at recursive interfaces as a principle for compact states across domains. 

Limitations. Our formulation relies on statistical support for estimating local predictive constraints, which may be-come less stable in data-sparse regimes. The feasibility tolerance κ also requires validation-based calibration and may vary across tasks and domains. Developing more data-efficient estimators and adaptive calibration strategies, includ-ing transfer across related tasks, remains a direction for future work. The VLA evidence is restricted to the held-out LIBERO-Mem T3 evaluation initializations and should therefore be interpreted as held-out closed-loop performance under this benchmark setting rather than as broad out-of-distribution generalization.

# B Related Work 

Recursive state compression appears across a broad range of modern learning systems. Temporal graph models re-peatedly aggregate historical interactions into evolving node representations, as in TGN and TGAT (Rossi et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2020); recurrent language-memory methods compress accumulated context into states that are reused across subsequent segments or interactions, as in CCM and AutoCompressor (Kim et al., 2024; Chevalier et al., 2023); em-bodied models such as MemoryVLA maintain compact perceptual–cognitive memories across multi-stage control (Shi et al., 2026); and iterative self-improvement or program-search systems reuse selected candidates, execution feedback, or reflections across successive rounds, as in Reflexion, FunSearch, and ReEvo (Shinn et al., 2023; Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024). Despite their architectural differences, these systems share a common structure: inter-mediate states are repeatedly aggregated, compressed, and reused. This makes the central question not only how to compress a state, but what information must survive compression so that subsequent computation remains predictive. Existing representation-learning methods impose different criteria for deciding whether a compressed state is ade-quate. A classical approach uses reconstruction. Recursive autoencoders, for example, compose child representations into a parent state and train the parent representation to reconstruct its children (Socher et al., 2011). More gener-ally, autoencoding and masked-reconstruction objectives encourage representations to retain enough information to recover designated inputs or local content. A second family instead uses local or future prediction. Contrastive Pre-dictive Coding trains a context representation to predict future latent representations (van den Oord et al., 2018), while joint-embedding predictive approaches such as I-JEPA predict representations of masked target regions from visible context without reconstructing raw inputs (Assran et al., 2023). Such objectives place greater emphasis on predictive structure than direct reconstruction. However, both reconstruction and future-prediction objectives require a target to be specified in advance. In a recursively composed system, accurately reconstructing an immediate input, predict-ing an immediate parent, or predicting a particular future latent does not by itself imply that the target preserves all distinctions needed after subsequent aggregation and compression. Information-theoretic and predictive-state formulations provide a more general perspective on what a representa-tion should preserve. The Information Bottleneck (IB) formalizes compression through retained information about a designated relevance variable (Tishby et al., 1999); distributed and multivariate variants extend this principle to multiple observations and interacting variables (Aguerri and Zaidi, 2018; Abdelaleem et al., 2025), while successive-refinement formulations study information preservation across multiple stages of compression (Charvin et al., 2023). Predictive State Representations (PSRs) instead characterize state through conditional predictions of future observ-ables rather than reconstruction of the complete history (Littman et al., 2001; Singh et al., 2004). These perspectives establish task relevance and future predictability as principled criteria for representation quality. Our setting introduces an additional compositional requirement: the predictive distinctions retained at one interface must remain sufficient for the predictive requirements encountered at the next interface. We therefore do not introduce another reconstruction or fixed future-prediction target; we formulate a local predictive-sufficiency condition whose role is explicitly tied to recursive composition. Directly enforcing such a condition through full conditional distributions or mutual information is generally im-15 practical in high-dimensional learned systems. Mutual-information estimation from finite samples is statistically dif-ficult, with variational estimators exhibiting substantial bias–variance trade-offs (Poole et al., 2019). Predictive-state learning faces a related approximation problem because practical systems must restrict attention to a finite collection of predictive tests (Kulesza et al., 2015). Spectral PSRs, conditional-distribution embeddings (Song et al., 2009), and conditional mean or operator-regression methods (Gr¨ unew¨ alder et al., 2012) therefore characterize predictive relation-ships through finite tests, regressions, or cross-covariance operators. Our empirical predictive deficiency follows this computational philosophy, but the direction of construction is important: the recursive predictive requirement is spec-ified first, and finite tests, ridge readouts, and covariance balancing are introduced only as a tractable approximation to that requirement. Thus, empirical predictive deficiency approximates but does not define this preservation criterion. A separate line of work concerns how preservation or compatibility constraints are enforced during optimization. GEM constrains a current gradient using losses on episodic memories from previous tasks and projects violating gradients through a quadratic program (Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017); A-GEM replaces the collection of per-task constraints with a reference-gradient constraint (Chaudhry et al., 2019); and PCGrad modifies multi-task gradients ac-cording to pairwise gradient conflicts (Yu et al., 2020). More generally, projected and proximal optimization methods allow an optimizer step to be followed by a correction with respect to a prescribed feasible domain, including adaptive methods for constrained stochastic optimization (Duchi et al., 2011; Reddi et al., 2018). We therefore do not regard gradient projection, quadratic programming, or post-optimizer projection itself as a contribution. Our distinction lies instead in what defines the constrained update and where that constraint is imposed. GEM-type methods derive admissible directions from task losses or gradient compatibility, while conventional projected optimization assumes a feasible parameter domain specified independently of the current predictive state. In our setting, the predictive-feasible region is generated dynamically from predictive sensitivities of empirical predictive deficiency at recursive interfaces. Moreover, because the first-order predictive constraint is defined over an actual parameter displacement, we apply it to the task proposal produced by the host optimizer rather than directly projecting the raw task gradient before Adam or AdamW is applied. Given the task proposal tt, predictive feasibility projection returns the closest constrained update d⋆t in the predictive-feasible region Ft. The optimization contribution is therefore not a new projection operator, but the conversion of local predictive sufficiency into data-dependent margin-relaxed predictive constraints on the task proposal. Taken together, prior work provides powerful mechanisms for reconstruction, future prediction, information-theoretic compression, predictive-state approximation, and constrained optimization. The remaining question ad-dressed here is different: what local condition should an intermediate state satisfy when that state will itself be aggre-gated, compressed, and reused in later computation? We formulate this condition through local predictive sufficiency, approximate its local predictive deficiency with empirical predictive deficiency, and use predictive sensitivities to restrict updates that would erode the corresponding predictive distinctions. 16 Table 3: Reproduction map for the four experimental domains. ## C.4 Self-Recursive Improvement 

Purpose and evaluation setting. We use self-recursive improvement (SRI) as a mechanism-level test of predictive preservation under explicit recursive strategy refinement. In this setting, previously generated execution traces are repeatedly reused to construct later strategies, making the retained recursive history directly relevant to subsequent computation. Our comparison is therefore designed to isolate the effect of predictive preservation while holding the realized initialization, capacity, recursive-search opportunity, backbone, and evaluator fixed. 

Benchmark, data, and released host. We evaluate on a fixed six-task cohort from CO-Bench (Sun et al., 2026). CO-Bench provides task descriptions, development instances, held-out test instances, and a common evaluator that maps solver outputs to normalized task scores. We obtain the benchmark data from the official CO-Bench release on Hugging Face, 8 using the format and evaluator provided by the public CO-Bench repository. 9 No task instance is regenerated or custom development/test split introduced; the official evaluator and scoring rule remain unchanged. The recursive host is the public Meta n implementation. 10 We use its released CO-Bench integration ( --benchmark co bench ) and evolutionary archive orchestrator ( --use-archive ). The six-task cohort is fixed before the paired comparison and is identical for Meta n and Ours. The exact task identifiers, repository revisions, and run-level config-urations are retained in the experiment manifest. 

Backbones and API execution. We evaluate three API backbones: gpt-5.5 , gemini-3.1-pro , and 

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash . Within each backbone, Meta n and Ours use the same provider-side model identifier and the same API route. Following the released host protocol, the same backbone is used for the Layer-1 solver and for every invocation of the recursive meta-operation Ω.For each paired comparison, the provider route, decoding configuration, maximum output length, retry policy, prompt templates, solver mode, and execution timeout are frozen before recursive expansion and shared by the two methods. Provider fallback is disabled, so failed requests follow the same retry policy without silently switching to another model or endpoint. Exact provider/model strings and decoding settings are recorded in the run configuration. 

Paired realized initialization. Each Meta n/Ours comparison begins from the same realized Layer-1 state. For a given backbone, the initial task programs, execution traces, development scores, archive state, and associated meta-data are generated once and copied before the two recursive arms are expanded. All locally controlled sources of randomness, including archive sampling and task ordering, use paired settings across the two methods. This design controls the realized API output at the point where the methods begin to differ. Remote model calls need not be bitwise deterministic, and independently regenerating the root would simultaneously alter the initial pro-grams, execution traces, and downstream recursive environment. After the intervention begins, the two recursive trajectories are allowed to diverge as a consequence of their different historical-trace retention decisions; this down-stream divergence is part of the mechanism being evaluated.

Matched recursive-search budget. We use equal recursive-search opportunity as the primary resource control. Before each paired run, a common archive-expansion budget is fixed. Both methods receive the same number of candidate-generation opportunities and the same evaluation allocation associated with each candidate. A candidate-generation slot is consumed once its generation has been attempted. Parsing failures, invalid pro-grams, execution errors, and timeouts therefore remain part of the realized search cost and do not receive free replace-ment proposals. The two arms also use the same execution timeout, retry policy, maximum generation length, and maximum number of host solver or repair calls associated with a candidate. Method-dependent convergence statistics are retained as run diagnostics but do not alter the number of recursive expansions available to either method. Likewise, any difference in realized prompt length or token usage is not converted into additional candidate generations, evaluator calls, or model requests. For resource accounting, we record candidate-generation attempts, evaluator invocations, external LLM requests, input and output tokens, and wall-clock time. Computation required internally by predictive preservation is accounted for separately and does not enlarge the external recursive-search budget. 

Development-only recursive selection. The recursive search uses only the development instances and development feedback supplied by the official CO-Bench evaluator. Held-out scores are not exposed to the Layer-1 solver, Ω,historical-trace retention, archive evolution, stopping logic, or predictive-preservation configuration. After the common recursive-search budget is exhausted, the development-side archive, recursive lineage, and selected strategies are frozen. We report the paper-facing Dev Gain under the same frozen development-based selection protocol for Meta n and Ours. The same six-task development cohort, aggregation rule, evaluator, and score scale are used in both arms. Appendix D.4 reports cohort-level development results and search diagnostics. Held-out results play no role in development-side candidate selection or in the computation of Dev Gain. 

Held-out evaluation. Held-out evaluation begins only after the recursive search and development-side selection have been frozen. The main table reports held-out scores for tasks whose recursive strategy is modified during the frozen development-side refinement process. The modified-task set is identified from the recursive run artifacts before held-out scores are examined. For every task included in this set, the frozen Meta n and Ours solutions are evaluated on the same official held-out instances, and their task scores are reported side by side. If several tasks are modified for a backbone, all such tasks are retained in the comparison; held-out performance is not used to choose among them. The modified-task presentation in Table 1 highlights where the recursive intervention produces an actual down-stream strategy change. For completeness, Appendix D.4 reports the task-wise held-out results for the full six-task cohort together with the unconditional cohort-level averages for Meta n and Ours. Consequently, the complete held-out record does not depend on the direction or magnitude of the main-table differences.

This appendix describes how the common predictive-preservation framework is instantiated in each host system, fol-lowed by controlled ablations and hyperparameter sensitivity. ## E.1 Temporal-Graph Implementation 

Host recursion and recursive interfaces. We use TGN as the common host for UCI temporal link prediction and Wikipedia node classification. For a temporal query with propagation depth L, TGN recursively constructs node representations from temporally sampled neighborhoods. At each propagation layer, information collected from the current temporal subtree is aggregated into an intermediate representation and then reused by the parent recursion. Consequently, signals originating deeper in the temporal neighborhood must pass through more successive recursive interfaces before reaching the task prediction. These reused intermediate representations are the recursive interfaces at which predictive preservation is applied: information needed by later prediction must survive the current aggregation before it can propagate further toward the root. 

Method insertion. We instantiate the recursive interface at each internal propagation layer 0 < ℓ < L . TGN first computes its original temporal aggregation at layer ℓ. The compression map Γ then receives the layer-specific own state together with this vanilla aggregate and produces the representation passed to the parent recursion. The leaf layer (ℓ = 0 ) retains the raw host state, and the task-facing root ( ℓ = L) retains the original TGN aggregation operator. The method therefore acts precisely on intermediate representations that will subsequently be reused at another recursive interface after a further round of temporal aggregation. This construction is shared by both temporal-graph tasks. The host temporal memory, neighborhood construction, temporal aggregation procedure, and task pipeline are retained; the distinction between UCI and Wikipedia remains the original benchmark task and evaluation protocol. 

Predictive records and predictive feasibility optimization. During the same recursive query, the host adapter traces the eligible internal interfaces together with the child–parent relationships actually consumed by the TGN computation. These traces provide the aligned local predictive records used to estimate the finite-test predictive value and empirical predictive deficiency introduced in Section 3. The resulting predictive sensitivities are used by the common constrained-update procedure, with the predictive correction restricted to the compression parameter block 

Γ. All remaining trainable TGN parameters continue to follow the host task optimization. The statistical configuration, hyperparameter selection, and optimization settings associated with the predictive-preservation procedure are reported jointly with the corresponding method-level configurations below. 

## E.2 Language-Memory Implementation 

Host and recursive memory. We use CCM as the host for the language-memory experiments. Under its merge recur 

construction, dialogue history is compressed progressively rather than presented in full at every prediction step. Af-ter processing the first t history turns, CCM aggregates the corresponding compression-token states into a compact memory Mt, which is then reused when the next turn is incorporated. The resulting computation therefore forms a chain 

M1 → M2 → · · · → ML → response prediction .

## E.7 Ablation Study 

We further examine which components of the proposed training procedure contribute to predictive preservation. All ablations are conducted on UCI link prediction at propagation depth 3 using seed 0. Each configuration is run once under the same data split, host architecture, training budget, and evaluation protocol. Accordingly, this experiment is intended as a controlled mechanism-level diagnosis of the full method rather than an estimate of cross-seed variability. The corresponding multi-seed results for the main comparison are reported separately. 

Does the choice of preservation objective matter? Yes. Task-Only Training reaches 92.62 AP and 92.13 AUC, providing the reference performance when no explicit predictive-preservation objective is introduced. Replacing pre-dictive preservation with history reconstruction reduces performance to 90.89 AP and 90.28 AUC, indicating that retaining information sufficient to reconstruct the past does not necessarily retain the information useful for subse-quent prediction. Direct Future Prediction performs better at 92.76 AP and 92.24 AUC, but still remains below the full method at 94.52 AP and 94.25 AUC. This suggests that predictive targets are more suitable than reconstruction targets, while directly fitting future outcomes does not recover the full predictive criterion’s benefit. 

Does the structure of predictive supervision matter? The results indicate that it does. The Single-Event Predictive Criterion reaches 92.41 AP and 91.93 AUC. Replacing the adjacent predictive pair Sv = ( Yv , Y pa( v)) with a single predictive event weakens the local supervision supplied at each recursive interface. Cross-level mispaired supervision reaches 92.73 AP and 92.28 AUC. Its degradation relative to the full method indicates that the correspondence be-tween a compressed state and its associated predictive event is important, even when comparable predictive events are available elsewhere in the recursion. Removing the retained context gives 92.89 AP and 92.50 AUC. The remaining gap to the full method is consistent with the role of context in defining the conditional predictive information that should survive each recursive interface. 

Does the statistical realization of the predictive criterion matter? All three statistical simplifications lead to a larger reduction. Estimating the predictive statistics separately for each tree obtains 91.48 AP and 90.99 AUC, suggesting that the shared empirical estimate benefits from pooling aligned observations across recursive instances. Removing balancing gives 91.30 AP and 90.98 AUC, supporting the use of balanced predictive tests when aggregating discrepancies across outcomes or interfaces. Using a diagonal-covariance approximation gives 91.51 AP and 91.04 AUC, indicating that cross-dimensional dependence in the empirical statistic contains useful information. Together, these results show that implementing the predictive criterion matters beyond the supervision target. 

Does constrained optimization contribute beyond the predictive objective? Yes. The Additive Predictive-Gradient Update yields 92.64 AP and 92.18 AUC, below the 94.52 AP and 94.25 AUC of the full method. This separates the predictive objective from its predictive-feasibility realization: using the same supervision as an additive gradient does not provide the same protection against task proposals that violate predictive preservation. Replacing the interface-wise constraints with the Global Predictive Constraint causes the largest drop, to 90.32 AP and 89.88 AUC. The result is consistent with the recursive structure of the problem: predictive loss can arise at individual recursive interfaces, and an aggregate global restriction does not provide the same local control over these violations. 

Overall. Across the four groups, the full method achieves the highest AP and AUC in this fixed seed-0 run. The ab-lations jointly support four aspects of the design: predictive rather than reconstructive preservation, correctly aligned interface-level supervision, the balanced empirical statistical realization, and interface-wise constrained optimization.
