1. Introduction 

The representation theory of quantum affine algebras provides several noncommutative deformations of Grothendieck rings. Frenkel–Reshetikhin 

q-characters record the joint generalized eigenspaces of the Drinfeld Cartan currents [7]. Their deformations, constructed geometrically by Nakajima and algebraically by Hernandez, lead to quantum Grothendieck rings and algorithms for characters of simple modules [13, 18]. For a Dynkin quiver Q

of type A, D , or E, Hernandez and Leclerc introduced a monoidal category 

CQ and identified its quantum Grothendieck ring with the positive part of the quantum group of the same type [15, Theorem 6.1]. Their construction relates spectral positions, Auslander–Reiten translation, inverse quantum Cartan coefficients, and dual PBW generators. Fedele and Hernandez introduced a deformation involving several toroidal parameters and constructed its flat toroidal character theory [6, Theorem 5.8]. Flatness requires relations between these parameters; it does not hold for the naive unrestricted deformation. Their quotient construction has a particularly rigid local feature: a root monomial Ai,p quasi-commutes with a spectral variable Yj,s through a single combination of the toroidal parameters, and the exponent vanishes unless i = j and |p − s| = 1. They exhibited a multiparameter positive-part phenomenon in type A2 and proposed a wider relationship for the categories CQ [6, §8.3]. We give a uniform algebraic treatment of the two-parameter specialization for bipartite quivers of finite simply-laced type. The central question is local: If a source-side simple-root class is a monomial, how many eigenvalues can its conjugation have on an adjacent distinguished character? In type A,column tableaux can make cancellations visible. At a trivalent vertex, and for non-thin fundamental modules, a support-by-support approach is less natural. We instead use a grading and a small homogeneous dimension. A preliminary issue is essential. Ambient flatness alone does not establish the root grading needed to compare cubic words, and coefficients in a toroidal triangular basis cannot be identified a priori with ordinary tensor-product multiplicities. We resolve both issues by comparing the toroidal product directly with the one-parameter product before invoking rank-two dimensions. 2Standing hypotheses

Method and relationship with cluster structures 

The proof uses a local spectral argument. Suppose the three cubic words 

X2Z, XZX , and ZX 2 lie in a two-dimensional space and X is invertible in the ambient torus. Then a polynomial of degree at most two annihilates Z

under Ad X . Monomial eigenvectors make the spectral bound explicit, and the one-parameter fiber identifies its two roots. The reverse Serre relation follows from a second two-dimensional homogeneous component. Its proof separates the eigenvalues a2, ab , and b2, without assuming a relation between the two mixed products of the eigenblocks. The key extra ingredient is the passage from local A–Y rigidity to the global degree form ωQ. This supplies the missing grading input and also determines the apparently free nonadjacent parameters: they are 1 for this bipartite specialization. We give an explicit local bicharacter construction for the positive part and check the direction of its cocycle ratio on the three Serre words. 4Cluster structures remain part of the setting, rather than an additional unproved hypothesis. We prove a compatibility identity for homogeneous seeds: if G ˜B = 0, then adding the degree-form correction does not change the exchange-column pairing. Thus a one-parameter compatibility constant 2 yields a second constant 1. This result is stated in terms of homogeneous seed data; we do not need a separate identification of all toroidal cluster variables with deformed simple classes. The broader quantum-cluster setting has been developed in [2, 3, 12, 14]. Quantum Grothendieck-ring isomorphisms, positivity, and their cluster-theoretic consequences are studied in [9, 16]; quantum virtual Grothendieck rings and skew-symmetrizable quantum cluster structures are developed in [17]. Related quantum cluster structures on quantized coordinate rings, including the quantum coordinate ring of skew-symmetric matrices, are studied in [21]. Recent work continues to connect quantum Grothendieck rings with monoidal categorification, deformations, and shifted quantum affine algebras [5, 8, 19]. These results concern different categories or quantizations; the present question is the specific Fedele–Hernandez specialization and its finite-type positive-part realization. In particular, our argument does not deduce the realization from a cluster structure. It derives the multiparameter Cartan data from the toroidal commutation forms and the rank-two graded dimensions, and only afterwards compares the resulting presentation with the multiparameter quantum group. The quotient of the spectral weight lattice by root monomials also has an established representation-theoretic role [4, 6]; here we use its explicit bipartite presentation to compute the residual commutation form. Two structural statements drive the proof. The Euler-correction identity identifies the residual toroidal skew direction with the antisymmetric Euler form of Q. The local spectral theorem then converts the rank-two PBW dimension into a quadratic constraint on the adjoint action. The remaining steps reconstruct the Cartan parameters and globalize the local relations by a cocycle/PBW specialization argument. This separates the local rigidity mechanism from the global presentation. For ease of reference, GR(Q) denotes the specialized character-generated algebra inside the based torus, Zi its distinguished simple-root generators, L

the finite root lattice, and ωQ the antisymmetric Euler form fixed by (1.1). The symbol Ad X always means conjugation in the ambient based quantum torus; inverse monomials are not asserted to belong to GR(Q). The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 fixes the coefficient and character conventions. Section 3 proves the lattice quotient and kernel decomposition. Section 4 establishes the character comparison and graded dimensions. Section 5 proves the spectral and Serre statements. Section 6 5constructs the PBW form and proves the generic isomorphism. Homogeneous seed compatibility and explicit branching examples are treated in Sections 7 and 8.

8.3. Reproducible finite checks 

The supplementary program verify_ade_kernels.py uses only integer arithmetic and the Python standard library. It constructs the Cartan matrices, the simple reflections, the Coxeter element, and the inverse-Cartan recurrence independently, and then checks (3.1) and (3.9) on the window (i, p ) = ( i, ξ i − 2k), −12 ≤ k ≤ 12 .

All ordered pairs in this window are checked. In addition, the program walks down each positive Coxeter column to identify the simple-root points and checks (4.8) on every source–sink edge. The total is 181 ,875 ordered kernel pairs, all satisfying the two identities. These finite tests check conventions and computations; they do not replace Theorem 3.5, do not establish an identity for unbounded spectral positions by themselves, and do not enumerate the monomial support of an E6, E 7, or E8 character. 9. Concluding remarks 

The two-parameter deformation has a useful decomposition into a moving one-parameter product and an alternating degree twist. The decisive identity is not merely the local vanishing of most A–Y pairings: it is the identification of their residual form on the spectral quotient with the skew Euler form. This supplies the positive grading and the homogeneous dimensions required by the spectral argument. The quadratic-annihilator principle then explains why non-thin modules and branching vertices do not introduce a third local eigenvalue. The special fiber is used only after the generic spectral bound has been established. Both Serre relations and the nonadjacent commutation factors are consequently determined. The local coefficient ring makes the final specialization argument precise, including the cancellation of the uniformizer in a torsion-free target. Several extensions require separate work. One may seek preferred global integral forms and basis-compatible isomorphisms, rather than the local and generic forms used here. The seed compatibility identity suggests further cluster-theoretic applications, but categorical interpretations of all toroidal cluster variables are not proved by compatibility alone. For non-simply-laced types, the inverse-Cartan recurrence and the local root factors have different shifts; the quotient calculation and the rank-two spectral bound would both have to be modified. None of these stronger statements is an assumption of the present realization.
