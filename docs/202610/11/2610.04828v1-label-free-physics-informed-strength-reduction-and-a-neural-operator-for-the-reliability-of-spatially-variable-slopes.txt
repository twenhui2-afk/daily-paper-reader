# Label-free physics-informed strength reduction and a neural operator for the reliability of spatially variable slopes 

Apisit Robjanghvad, Sompote Youwai ∗

> AI Research Group, Department of Civil Engineering, Faculty of Engineering, King Mongkut’s University of Technology Thonburi, Bangkok, Thailand

Abstract 

This paper proposes a label-free physics-informed neural network (PINN) for non-associated Mohr–Coulomb plasticity with incremental strength reduction for the reliability of spatially variable c–φ slopes. The PINN is trained with a virtual-work loss on the finite-element mesh and solves many random fields at once on a graphics processing unit. With a solver budget fixed on the ho-mogeneous slopes, it reproduces the Abaqus factor of safety within 2 to 3 % root-mean-square on 52 random fields and locates the failure mechanism in the same weak zones, with the plastic strain in the band under-resolved. A neural operator trained only on the PINN solutions predicts the factor of safety, dis-placement path and failure mode of a million fields in seconds. No finite-element solution enters the pipeline: Monte Carlo fields, importance samples and oper-ator training data come from the PINN, and Abaqus serves only to verify the result. Monte Carlo simulation, the first-order reliability method, importance sampling and the operator give consistent failure probabilities down to 10 −3 for the slope studied, but a 3 % scatter in the factor of safety inflates a probability of 10 −3 by up to a factor of two, and the absolute values refer to one mesh. A heterogeneous Bishop analysis gives failure probabilities 2.6 and 8.3 times lower although its critical circles follow the PINN mechanism, because a small difference in the factor of safety is amplified in the tail. The operator also yields the failure mechanism and displacement response conditional on failure. 

Keywords: Slope reliability, Physics-informed neural network, Neural operator, Strength reduction method, Random field, Mohr–Coulomb 

1. Introduction 

Natural soils exhibit spatial variability in shear strength, and slope stability is governed not only by the mean strength but also by the spatial arrange-ment of weak zones. Probabilistic slope stability analysis therefore represents the strength parameters as random fields. The random finite-element method (RFEM) combines such fields with elastoplastic finite-element analysis and the strength reduction method (SRM) [10, 18] in a Monte Carlo framework [16]. In 

> ∗Corresponding author. Email: sompote.you@kmutt.ac.th arXiv:2610.04828v1 [cs.CE] 4 Oct 2026

RFEM, the failure mechanism is not prescribed but emerges along the weakest path in each realisation, while slope failure is treated as a system event [25]. Analyses that neglect spatial variability can yield unconservative estimates of failure probability [17], while a slope classified as safe in a deterministic analysis may still have a substantial probability of failure [8, 16]. The price of this generality is computational. Each realisation requires a sequence of nonlinear finite-element analyses, and crude Monte Carlo requires on the order of 10 4 to 10 5 realisations to estimate a failure probability of 10 −3. Li et al. [32] reported 60,000 finite-element analyses and approximately 500 hours of computation for a direct Monte Carlo reference. Subset simulation [2, 33] and importance sampling [3] reduce the number of required samples, but each sample still requires a full elastoplastic analysis. Limit-equilibrium analyses of random fields are much cheaper [8], but their failure probabilities can differ from finite-element estimates by a factor of about three [32]. Surrogate models replace the expensive analysis with regression models trained on solver output. Gaussian-process regression on the Karhunen–Loève coefficients [67], neural networks and support-vector regression [22], gradient boosting [57], failure classifiers [1], Gaussian processes on reduced variables [36], convolutional neural networks on the discretised random field [64] and graph neural networks on the finite-element mesh [34] predict the factor of safety or the failure class of a realisation, and data-driven predictors of the factor of safety have also been used to shorten the SRM itself [11]. Image-to-image networks go further and predict the failure surface, the sliding volume and the deformation field of a spatially variable slope [59]. These studies leave three gaps. First, every training label comes from a conventional solver, and the number of solver runs ranges from a few hundred [22] to 10 4 [34] and 10 5

[11], so the surrogate inherits the cost of the solver before it saves any, and no surrogate for slope reliability has been built from a source other than a finite-element or limit-equilibrium solver. Second, accuracy near the limit state requires training data in the failure region [67], several studies did not examine failure probabilities below 10 −2 [1, 22], and whether a surrogate trained on Monte Carlo fields alone can be trusted at Pf ≈ 10 −3 has not been tested against an independent estimate of the same probability. Third, with the exception of Wang et al. [59], the surrogates return one scalar per realisation, so the probability that a zone lies in the slip band, the size of the sliding mass and the displacement response given failure, which all need the response of every sample, remain unavailable at the sample sizes that rare events demand. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [49] remove the need for labels by training the network on the governing equations. In solid mechanics they have been formulated in strong form [19], in energy and variational form [14, 27, 52] and, most relevant here, on the finite-element mesh itself: the finite element-integrated neural network of Zhang et al. [63] evaluates the Galerkin weak form with shape functions, Gauss integration and an incremental stress update, and solves elastic and elastoplastic boundary-value problems without labelled data, with a modified Cam-clay model whose flow rule is associated. Energy-based losses extend to associated plasticity [21], but they require a potential and there-fore do not apply to the non-associated flow rules used for soils. In geotechnical engineering, Zhang et al. [66] trained a PINN without labelled data to construct a kinematically admissible failure surface for the upper-bound limit analysis of homogeneous slopes, with an associated flow rule and without the stress field. 2Liu and Jia [37] solved the elastoplastic Mohr–Coulomb displacement field of a homogeneous slope and of a slope with a weak interlayer with a label-free strong-form PINN that uses a non-associated flow rule and a return map detached from automatic differentiation, and obtained the factor of safety by slip-surface stress analysis of the stress field at the working strength, so the limit state is inferred from a single pre-failure stress state rather than reached by strength reduction. Other PINNs for Mohr–Coulomb plasticity still include labelled data in the loss [62, 65], including a Fourier-feature PINN by the present authors [50], and for elastoplastic footings the forward analysis without data has been limited to elas-ticity, with plasticity treated as an inverse problem [7]. Yuan et al. [62], Chen et al. [7] and Robjanghvad and Youwai [50] all report reduced accuracy in shear bands. Taken together, these studies leave the forward problem that the SRM poses unsolved by a PINN. The label-free formulations are limited either to an associated flow rule [21, 63, 66] or to a single stress state at the working strength [37], whereas the formulations that follow non-associated Mohr–Coulomb plas-ticity rely on finite-element labels [50, 62, 65]. No PINN has carried the plastic history from step to step along an incremental strength-reduction path with a non-associated flow rule until a mechanism forms, none has been checked real-isation by realisation against a finite-element SRM on random fields, and none has been run on the thousand or more realisations that a reliability analysis needs. A further obstacle is that a PINN solves one boundary-value problem at a time and must be retrained for every new configuration [9], which is costly when 10 6 random fields are needed. Neural operators learn the map from an input function to the solution and evaluate new inputs at negligible cost [29, 35, 41]. Operators that expand the solution in a reduced basis [23, 31, 42] are well suited to fields on a fixed mesh. Physics can be added to the operator loss [58], but the operators applied in geotechnical engineering, for example to consolidation [9] and to the thermal regime of embankments [61], are trained on the output of conventional numerical solvers, and a recent assessment notes that the cost of generating that training set is often larger than the cost it saves [30]. An operator for slope reliability whose training data contain no finite-element solution has not been reported, and no operator has been used to return the displacement path and the failure mode of a random-field slope rather than a scalar factor of safety. A separate gap concerns the limit-equilibrium estimates against which these methods are usually judged. Li et al. [32] found that limit-equilibrium and finite-element failure probabilities differ by a factor of about three, while Cho [8] and Zhu et al. [67] found the critical surfaces of the two methods at similar positions. Whether the difference in Pf comes from the circular shape of the slip surface or from the limit-equilibrium formulation itself has not been separated on the same random fields, and how a bias of a few percent in F propagates into Pf at 10 −3 has not been quantified. The research gap can therefore be stated in three parts. There is no label-free solver that reproduces the finite-element SRM for non-associated Mohr– Coulomb slopes with spatially variable strength, there is no reliability pipeline for such slopes that is free of finite-element solutions, and there is no estimate of the failure mechanism and the displacement response conditional on rare failure from such a pipeline. The objective of this study is to close these gaps by developing a PINN that performs incremental strength reduction without labelled data, training a neural operator on its solutions alone, and using both 3to estimate the failure probability, the failure mechanism and the displacement response of a spatially variable slope, with the finite-element SRM used only to verify the result and the heterogeneous Bishop method as the limit-equilibrium benchmark. A natural question is why a neural solver is needed at all when the finite-element SRM already solves each realisation reliably. The answer is not speed per field, because on comparable hardware the PINN is not faster than Abaqus for a single slope (Section 7.1), and this study does not claim otherwise. The benefit lies in four things that the finite-element workflow does not provide for a reliability analysis. First, the PINN is trained on the equations alone, so the chain from random field to failure probability needs no labelled data, no commercial solver and no licence per core, and the constitutive model is a few lines of differentiable code rather than a user subroutine. Second, because every realisation is an independent network on a shared mesh, hundreds of realisations are solved as one batched tensor program on a single GPU, whereas a finite-element code solves one realisation per job, so the cost of a Monte Carlo set scales with the number of batches rather than with the number of fields. Third, the PINN solutions are produced in the same framework as the operator that learns from them, so the hours spent on 10 3 PINN solutions are paid once and buy an operator that evaluates 10 6 fields in seconds, a sample size at which a direct finite-element Monte Carlo would take years (Section 7.1). At the scale of this study the total cost is nevertheless comparable, about 11 GPU-hours of PINN runs for the two weak cohesions against an estimated 26 hours of sequential Abaqus runs for the same fields on the reference machine, so the argument is independence from a solver and from labelled data rather than a saving in cost. Fourth, once the operator is trained, the response is a smooth differentiable function of the KL coefficients, which gives the gradient that FORM needs by automatic differentiation and opens the way to inverse analysis against field measurements. The finite-element method keeps its role as the independent reference. It verifies the solver on three homogeneous slopes and 52 random fields, and that comparison is itself a result, because it shows what a label-free solver delivers and what it costs in accuracy. The scope is a two-dimensional plane-strain c–φ slope of one geometry, 3 m high at 1:1, with cross-correlated lognormal cohesion and friction-angle fields of one anisotropic correlation structure, a Mohr–Coulomb model with zero dilation and no pore pressure, and three mean cohesions chosen so that the failure prob-ability ranges from no failure in 500 fields to about 10 −1, with a rare-event case near 10 −3. The PINN–SRM is verified against Abaqus on the same mesh for three homogeneous slopes and 52 random fields, and the failure probability is estimated by crude Monte Carlo simulation, FORM on surrogates, importance sampling and the neural operator. Three-dimensional slopes, other geometries and correlation structures, hardening models and pore-water pressure are out-side the scope and are discussed as future work. The contributions are as follows. 1. A label-free PINN performs incremental strength reduction on the finite-element mesh with a non-associated Mohr–Coulomb return map ( ψ = 0 )and carries the plastic strain from one accepted strength-reduction step to the next. Because no potential exists for non-associated flow, the loss is the discrete virtual work with the stress held fixed, so that its gradient is the finite-element nodal residual, and the factor of safety follows from 4the same displacement-rate criterion as in the finite-element SRM. 2. The PINN–SRM is verified against Abaqus on three homogeneous slopes and on 52 individual random fields, with factors of safety within 2 to 3 % RMS and failure mechanisms in the same weak zones. The dependence of the result on the solver budget is quantified. 3. The failure probability is estimated from 10 −1 to 10 −3 by Monte Carlo simulation, FORM on surrogates and importance sampling. A heteroge-neous Bishop analysis of the same fields underestimates Pf by factors of 2.6 and 8.3, and the critical circles show that the bias comes from the dif-ference between the simplified Bishop formulation and the non-associated strength-reduction model rather than from the circular shape of the slip surface. 4. A reduced-basis neural operator trained only on the PINN solutions pre-dicts the factor of safety, the displacement path and the failure mode of 

10 6 fields in seconds, so that no finite-element solution enters the reliabil-ity pipeline at any stage and Abaqus serves only to verify it. For Pf alone the operator is equivalent to a linear surrogate of ln F , but it additionally yields the failure mechanism and the displacement response conditional on failure at Pf ≈ 10 −3, which a scalar surrogate cannot provide. 2. Problem statement 

2.1. Geometry, loading and boundary conditions 

The problem is a 2D plane-strain slope, as shown in Fig. 1a. The domain is 12 m wide and 5 m high. The slope is 3 m high with an inclination of 1:1 bounded by a 5 m-wide crest and 4 m-wide toe platform at a height of 2 m. The base is fixed in both directions, while the two vertical sides are subject to roller constraints. The ground surface is traction-free. The only load is the self-weight of the soil, with γ = 19 .62 kN m −3, and no pore pressure is considered. The settlement is monitored at the crest point (x, y ) = (5 , 5) m. The domain is discretised using a structured mesh of 4800 bilinear quadrilateral elements (h = 0 .1 m) that conforms to the slope face. The same mesh is used for the PINN and for Abaqus reference analyses (Section 4). The sensitivity of the factor of safety to the mesh is examined in Appendix D.

Factor of safety by strength reduction 

The factor of safety (FoS) is evaluated with the strength reduction method (SRM). In the SRM, the soil strength parameters are reduced by a strength reduction factor s [47], such that 

cs = cs , tan φs = tan φs , (9) where the reduction is applied at each point in the domain. The strength re-duction factor s is increased until the slope can no longer be maintained in equilibrium with the reduced strength. The factor of safety F is defined as the critical value of s at which the prescribed failure criterion is reached. Because the material is path dependent, the stress state at each value of s is obtained incrementally from the state at the previous value of s, with the plastic strain carried forward. The limit is identified with the same displacement-rate criterion in both the PINN and Abaqus (Section 3).

2.5. Cases and quantities of interest 

Three mean cohesion values are considered with the same random-field model and the same set of standard-normal vectors ξ (fixed seed). Thus, the underlying spatial pattern is identical across cases, and the realisations differ only in the scale of the cohesion: 1. ¯c = 35 kPa: a slope far from failure, in which the spatial variability changes the factor of safety and the shape of the slip surface, but failure is not expected. 2. ¯c = 5 kPa: a marginally stable slope, with strength close to the failure limit and a high probability of failure. 3. ¯c = 7 kPa: an intermediate slope in which failure is a rare event. The three cases thus span the range from a slope far from failure to a high probability of failure, with a rare-event case in between. The two weak cases were selected with a preliminary limit-equilibrium screening over ¯c. For each case the homogeneous slope with c = ¯ c and φ = 20 ◦ everywhere serves as the deterministic reference and as the first verification problem. The quantities of interest are the distribution of F , the probability of failure

Label-free PINN for incremental strength reduction 

We solve the strength-reduction problem described in Section 2 for each random field using a physics-informed neural network (PINN) [49] trained only on the governing equations. The training is label-free in the sense that no labelled solution data enter the loss function: no displacement, stress, or factor-of-safety data from a finite-element solver are used. The workflow is summarised in Fig. 2.

3.4. Incremental strength reduction 

Each realisation is analysed with one network that is carried along the SRF path [18]: 1. Gravity: The network, initialised at random, is trained for 6000 Adam iter-ations [28] (learning rate 10 −3) at a strengthened state s0 = 0 .6. Starting below s = 1 ensures that the initial state is stable also for the weakest realisations. The maximum nodal displacement of this state, uref , is the reference displacement of the realisation. 2. Trial steps: The SRF is increased by ∆s = 0 .1 while the soil is elastic and by ∆s = 0 .014 s once any Gauss point has yielded. Each trial step starts from the network of the last accepted state and runs 1000 Adam iterations with a fresh optimiser (learning rate 3 × 10 −4, reduced tenfold over the last 20 % of the step). 3. Bisection: After a rejection, the network is rolled back to the last accepted state and the step is halved, until the interval between the last accepted and the first rejected SRF is below 0.01. A rejection that occurred with a step larger than 0.025 is confirmed once with the final small step. The factor of safety is the last accepted SRF, F = sok .The displacement increment between the first rejected trial and the last accepted state defines the failure mechanism of the realisation, from which the slip band (the 5 % of elements with the largest deviatoric strain increment) and the sliding mass (the elements whose displacement increment exceeds half of its maximum) are extracted. In the Abaqus reference analyses the same rate threshold, 10 uref 

per unit SRF, is applied to the crest displacement. 

3.5. Batched implementation 

Because the realisations are independent, a batch of R networks (typically 

R = 100 ) is trained simultaneously. The weights of all networks are stored as stacked tensors and evaluated using batched matrix products. Each network follows its own SRF schedule, with its own optimiser state, rollback, and bisec-tion. One optimiser iteration for the entire batch is captured once as a CUDA graph [45] and replayed, because the computational cost at this problem size is dominated by kernel launches rather than arithmetic. The acceptance test is evaluated every 50 iterations. 

4. Verification of the PINN–SRM against FEM (Abaqus) 

4.1. Homogeneous slopes 

We first verify the solver on the three homogeneous slopes of Section 2.5 (¯c = 35 , 5, and 7 kPa, φ = 20 ◦), for which a conventional finite-element solution is well-established. The reference is Abaqus/Standard on the same mesh as the PINN (4800 CPE4 elements, whose selectively reduced integration is also a 

¯B-type treatment of the volumetric strain, Appendix A), with the same elastic constants, unit weight, and boundary conditions. The built-in Mohr–Coulomb model with ψ = 0 and the default eccentricity of the flow potential is used, requiring the unsymmetric solver. Gravity is applied in a first step, and the strength is then reduced in a second step through a field variable that indexes tabulated values of c/s and tan φ/s , with increments of at most 0.004 of the step time. The factor of safety from Abaqus is obtained from the crest settlement using the same rate test as the PINN (Eq. (18)). The last converged SRF, which is the usual non-convergence criterion, is reported as an upper bound. Applying gravity at SRF = 1.0 instead of 0.6 changes the Abaqus factor of safety by less than 0.1 %. The simplified Bishop method with a circular slip search on a grid of centres and radii [5] is included as a limit-equilibrium reference. Table 2 and Fig. 4 compare the factors of safety. The mean PINN value of three network initialisations lies within 1 % of Abaqus in all three cases (FPINN /F Abaqus = 1 .007 , 1.010 and 0.990). The three initialisations give the same factor at ¯c = 35 kPa and differ by 1.5 % at ¯c = 5 and 7 kPa, where the limit is approached through a longer plastic path. Bishop is 0.3–3.8 % above Abaqus, the usual difference between limit equilibrium and strength reduction for a homogeneous slope [18]. The SRF–settlement curves of the PINN follow 13 Abaqus up to the limit (Fig. 4a–c). Both methods place the failure mecha-nism through the toe, deep for the strong slope and shallow for the weak ones (Fig. 4, right). The overlap of the thresholded shear bands is 0.74 at ¯c = 35 kPa but only 0.29 and 0.38 at ¯c = 5 and 7 kPa. These bands are one to two ele-ments thick, and the two measures are not identical (shear-strain increment of the failing trial for the PINN, PEEQ increment at the last converged state for Abaqus). Consequently, a shift of one element can halve the overlap, although the underlying mechanism is the same. We monitor the convergence of the solver through the FE nodal residual rather than through the value of the loss. The stress in Eq. (15) is held fixed in the gradient, and the loss value therefore has no minimum at the solution and does not measure accuracy. The gradient of the loss, which equals the nodal residual of Eq. (16), provides this measure. Fig. 3 shows the RMS nodal residual, normalised by the mean nodal gravity load, for ¯c = 35 and 5 kPa. During the gravity step the residual falls from about 0.65 to 0.20. Along the strength reduction the residual remains on a plateau (median 0.18 and 0.17, 5–95 % range 0.16–0.31 and 0.16–0.21) and changes little within each SRF step. The 1000 iterations of a step mainly carry the state to the new SRF rather than reduce the residual further. The residual therefore does not vanish: a level of about 0.17 is the accuracy floor of the present network and optimiser, and the field errors reported below quantify the effect of this floor on the displacement and stress. Near the limit the residual rises (to about 0.35 at ¯c = 35 kPa) as the plastic zone spreads. For comparison, the heterogeneous Bishop analysis of the same fields is 3.6 % above Abaqus on average at ¯c = 5 kPa, with a maximum difference of 8.2 %. It classifies only 10 of the 13 fields that fail in Abaqus as failing. This one-sided error is consistent with the lower Bishop failure probabilities reported in Section 6.6. The failure mechanisms are compared through the most strained 5 % of the elements in each solver (Fig. 6c–g). The median overlap of the two bands is 0.42 at ¯c = 35 kPa, 0.28 at ¯c = 5 kPa and 0.35 for the design points. These values are of the same order as for the homogeneous slopes, where the mechanism is known to be the same. The fields in Fig. 6d–g were selected at the median overlap rather than at the best. In the weak soil, Abaqus develops secondary parallel bands behind the main one (red in Fig. 6e–g), which enter the Abaqus top 5 % and are absent from the single failing increment of the PINN. In the deep mechanism of the strong soil (Fig. 6d), the strain of Abaqus concentrates in the lower part of the band, and the upper part, which the PINN resolves, falls below the 5 % threshold. Complete disagreement (overlap below 0.05) occurs in three fields (two at ¯c = 35 kPa, one at ¯c = 5 kPa). The factors of safety of these fields still differ by only 0.3–4.8 %, which indicates competing mechanisms of almost equal strength. In summary, over 52 random fields the PINN–SRM reproduces the factor of safety of a commercial finite-element code to about 2–3 % RMS without bias 17 and places the failure mechanism in the same weak zone. 5.3. Operator heads 

The operator consists of four heads that share the input ξ. Each head is written as a fixed base predictor plus a network correction whose last layer is initialised to zero, 

ˆy(ξ) = ybase (ξ) + σy ⊙ N (ξ), (21) where σy is a fixed scale (the standard deviation of the target over the training fields, or 0.05 for ln F ) and N is a multilayer perceptron with SiLU activations [13]. Training therefore starts from the best simple predictor, and the network learns only the remaining dependence on ξ. The correction is kept only when the validation loss decreases. 1. Factor of safety. The base is a ridge regression of ln F on ξ [24], with the regularisation weight selected by cross-validation, and the correction is an ensemble of five networks (two hidden layers of 64). The correction lowers the validation error by less than 1 % at ¯c = 5 kPa and not at all at 

¯c = 7 kPa, and the factor-of-safety head is in effect a linear model of ln F

in ξ. The logarithm keeps F positive and makes the dependence on the lognormal strength closer to linear. 2. Gravity state. The base is the mean of ag over the training fields. The correction has two hidden layers of 128. The loss is the squared error relative to ∥ag ∥2.3. Displacement path. The input is (ξ, τ ), extended by τ 2 and √τ , and the output is 

ad(ξ, τ ) = τ [¯ ad(τ ) + σd ⊙ N d(ξ, τ )] , (22) where ¯ad(τ ) is the mean path of the training fields, interpolated in τ , and 

Nd has four hidden layers of 384. The factor τ enforces ∆U(0) = 0. Each state is weighted by the inverse squared norm of the final increment of the realisation, which gives stiff and soft fields the same weight. 4. Failure mode. The base is the mean of am. The correction has three hidden layers of 256. Because the mode has unit length, the loss is 1 −

cos(ˆ am, am).The networks are trained with AdamW [39] and a cosine learning-rate sched-ule [38]. The weights with the lowest validation loss are kept. Training takes about one minute per mean cohesion. Further convergence results are given in Section 5.4. The PINN–SRM takes 15 to 20 s per field and Abaqus 73 s, but the two ran on different hardware, a GPU workstation against a four-core desktop, so the per-field times are not comparable and the PINN is not proposed as a faster replacement for the finite-element analysis of one field. The benefit is that it needs no labelled data and solves many fields at once on one GPU. The main cost of the reliability analysis is the PINN data set (2.2 to 6.9 h). For the 1300 fields of the two weak cohesions the PINN data sets took about 11 GPU-hours, whereas sequential Abaqus runs of the same fields would take about 26 h on the reference machine, so at this scale the total cost is comparable and the saving appears only in the operator stage. After about one minute of training, the operator evaluates 10 6 fields in seconds. Importance sampling needs only 300 PINN solutions but gives Pf alone. The operator also gives the failure mechanism and the displacement response (Section 6.5). This ordering 33 of cost, a few hours of PINN solutions before the operator saves anything, is the pattern that Kumar [30] identifies for operator learning in geotechnics; the saving appears only when the number of required evaluations, here 10 6, is far larger than the training set. 

7.2. Limit equilibrium and small failure probabilities 

The Bishop analysis underestimates Pf although its critical circle follows the PINN mechanism. Over 300 fields at ¯c = 5 kPa, all critical circles pass through the toe, as does the PINN slip band (Fig. 11), and the two factors of safety are strongly correlated ( r = 0 .97 ). The difference is therefore in the value of F , not in the shape of the slip surface. Bishop is about 3 % higher at the median, the usual gap between limit equilibrium and strength reduction [18]. The compar-ison does not separate the two ingredients of that gap, the simplified Bishop assumptions on interslice forces and the non-associated flow rule of the contin-uum model, because neither a rigorous non-circular limit-equilibrium method nor a run with ψ = φ was performed. Cho [8] reported ratios of 0.96 to 0.99 between strength-reduction and Bishop factors of safety for four random fields of an undrained slope. The gap is largest in the weak fields that control Pf . At ¯c = 5 kPa the PINN-to-Bishop ratio is 0.982 for F > 1.3 but 0.961 for F < 1.1 (0.976 and 0.949 at 

¯c = 7 kPa). The gap also grows as the cohesion decreases: it is negligible at 

¯c = 35 kPa, and Zhu et al. [67] found close agreement for undrained slopes. The non-associated flow rule ( ψ = 0 ), which limit equilibrium does not represent, is a possible cause, not tested here. A finer mesh lowers the finite-element F

(Appendix D) and would widen the gap. Cho [8] considered differences of this size to be of no practical consequence. Near Pf = 10 −3, however, a small error in F has a large effect. Scaling the Bishop values at ¯c = 7 kPa by 0.968 gives Pf = 6 .1 × 10 −4 and by 0.95 gives 

1.4 × 10 −3, against 9.1 × 10 −4 from the PINN. A constant correction is therefore unreliable in the tail, consistent with the factor of about three between limit equilibrium and finite-element estimates reported by Li et al. [32]. Limit equilib-rium remains a useful guide to the failure region, as in the importance-sampling centre of Section 6.4, but small failure probabilities should be computed from continuum solutions. 

7.3. Neural operator versus scalar surrogates 

For Pf alone, the operator offers little beyond a simple surrogate. The factor-of-safety head is effectively a ridge regression of ln F , and the ridge, Gaussian-process, and neural-network surrogates of Section 6.3 give similar reliability indices at ¯c = 5 kPa (1.23, 1.24, and 1.13). He et al. [22] likewise found a linear support-vector regression sufficient. The added value is the full response of every sample. From the same 10 6 fields, the operator gives the slip-band probability conditional on failure, the size of the sliding mass, and the exceedance probabil-ity of the crest displacement along the path (Section 6.5), which a surrogate of 

F cannot show, although that displacement is not a physical settlement. Like any surrogate, the operator is accurate in the tail only when its training data cover the failure region. Without the importance samples, the linear surrogate gives 1.6 × 10 −3 instead of 6.7 × 10 −4 (Table 3). The same caution applies to deep surrogates trained on Monte Carlo samples only [34]. The operator was 34 not trained on Abaqus solutions of the same fields, so whether PINN labels give a better or worse operator than finite-element labels remains untested; the benefit claimed here is independence from a solver and from labelled data, not a demonstrated gain in accuracy or cost. 

7.4. Limitations 

The factor of safety of the PINN–SRM depends on the solver budget (Ap-pendix E). The budget used here was fixed on the homogeneous slopes and verified on 52 random fields. A larger budget converges the plastic strain better but detects failure earlier, and F falls 4 to 6 % below Abaqus at ¯c = 5 and 7 kPa. The pipeline therefore contains no finite-element solution, but its answer is set by a finite-element calibration, and the calibration error is amplified in the tail. By the lognormal fit of Section 6.2, a uniform reduction of F by 4 to 6 % would raise Pf at ¯c = 5 kPa from 0.11 to about 0.24 to 0.32, and by the linear surrogate of Section 6.4, taking the mean of ln F at ¯c = 7 kPa as 2 % below the homogeneous value as at ¯c = 5 kPa, it would raise Pf at ¯c = 7 kPa by a factor of about four to nine. The failure probabilities reported here are therefore those of the verified budget, and a step control based on convergence rather than a fixed number of iterations is needed before they can be read as budget-independent. The agreement in F may partly reflect compensating errors. The plastic strain in the band of the weak slopes is about one quarter of the Abaqus value at 

0.95 F , yet F agrees within 1 %, and when the plastic flow is converged better F

falls below Abaqus (Appendix E). Under-converged plastic flow stiffens the band and delays the failure trigger, which may offset whatever drives the converged solver low. This was not isolated by a separate test. For the same reason the accepted states are approximate rather than exact equilibrium states: the nodal residual plateaus at about 0.17 of the mean nodal gravity load, far above the tolerance of a converged implicit finite-element step, and the factor of safety is the strength-reduction factor at which the optimiser can no longer carry the state within the budget. That this factor coincides with the Abaqus value within 2 to 3 % is an empirical finding for this budget and this problem, not a property guaranteed by the formulation. The random error of the solver biases Pf upward. On the weak-soil fields the PINN scatters by 2.9 % RMS about Abaqus with no systematic sign, and three initialisations of the homogeneous weak slopes differ by 1.5 %, which is part of that scatter. Zero-mean scatter in ln F widens the distribution of F and so raises the probability in the lower tail. If the whole 2.9 % is attributed to the PINN, deconvolving it from the lognormal fit at ¯c = 5 kPa lowers Pf from 0.127 to 0.115, and deconvolving it from the linear surrogate at ¯c = 7 kPa lowers Pf

by about a factor of two. The PINN estimates are therefore more likely high than low in the tail, by an amount that is small at Pf ≈ 10 −1 and comparable to the estimate itself at Pf ≈ 10 −3.The absolute failure probabilities refer to the mesh with h = 0 .1 m. A finer mesh lowers F of the weak soils by 2 to 4 % (Appendix D), which would raise Pf

by a factor of two to three at ¯c = 7 kPa. This bias is common to the PINN, the operator and Abaqus, so it does not affect the comparisons between them, but it does widen the gap to the mesh-independent Bishop analysis, and the random-field Abaqus runs did not converge on the finest mesh, so the mesh-converged reference is not known. 35 The operator is trained for one mean cohesion and must be retrained for other statistics of the field. Including the statistics as inputs, as in the meta-model of Liu et al. [36], would remove this restriction. Its factor-of-safety head is in effect a ridge regression of ln F , and the operator estimates of Pf lie 8 and 23 % below the reference values, within their intervals, while the operator un-derestimates the spread of the sliding-mass size. The operator intervals reflect sampling error only. A systematic error of 2 % in F , the size of the held-out RMS error, would move Pf by about −30 to +40 % at ¯c = 5 kPa and by a factor of about two in either direction at ¯c = 7 kPa, so the sampling intervals understate the uncertainty of the operator by an order of magnitude in the tail. The operator also predicts displacements but not stresses, and the crest displacement it returns is accumulated along an artificial weakening path from 

s0 = 0 .6, so it is a displacement response and not a serviceability settlement (Section 6.5). This study considers one plane-strain slope geometry, one correlation structure of the strength, deterministic stiffness, no pore pressure and a non-associated flow rule with ψ = 0 . The factors of 2.6 and 8.3 between the PINN and Bishop estimates belong to this slope and these mean cohesions, and the share of the non-associated flow rule in that difference was not tested. Other geometries, anisotropic correlation, random stiffness, a rigorous non-circular limit-equilibrium benchmark and three-dimensional slopes remain to be examined. 

8. Conclusions 

A label-free PINN–SRM and a neural operator trained on its solutions were developed for the reliability analysis of c–φ slopes with spatially vari-able strength. The introduction identified three gaps, a label-free solver that reproduces the finite-element SRM for non-associated Mohr–Coulomb slopes with spatially variable strength, a reliability pipeline for such slopes that is free of finite-element solutions, and an estimate of the failure mechanism and the displacement response conditional on rare failure, together with the unresolved cause of the difference between limit-equilibrium and finite-element failure prob-abilities. The conclusions are grouped accordingly. 1. The label-free solver. The PINN–SRM computes the factor of safety without labelled data by carrying the plastic history through the strength-reduction path with a non-associated flow rule. With a solver budget fixed on the homogeneous slopes, it agrees with Abaqus within 1 % on those slopes and within 2 to 3 % RMS on 52 random fields, and places the failure mechanism in the same weak zones. The agreement depends on the solver budget by 4 to 6 %, which by the fitted distributions would move 

Pf at the two weak cohesions by a factor of two to nine, and the plastic strain within the band is under-resolved, so the budget must be checked against a reference before the solver is applied to other problems. 2. The finite-element-free pipeline: spatial variability. Spatial vari-ability governs the reliability of the weak slope. At ¯c = 5 kPa the homo-geneous analysis gives F = 1 .136 , whereas Monte Carlo simulation of the PINN random fields gives Pf = 0 .113 .36 3. The finite-element-free pipeline: rare events. Importance sampling centred on a Bishop-based design point estimates Pf = 9 .1 × 10 −4 at 

¯c = 7 kPa from 300 PINN solutions, with a coefficient of variation of 15 %. Surrogates of F are accurate in the tail only when trained with data from the failure region, since the linear surrogate fitted to the Monte Carlo fields alone gives 1.6 × 10 −3.4. The finite-element-free pipeline: the operator. The neural opera-tor, trained on the PINN solutions alone, evaluates 10 6 fields in seconds and gives Pf within the reference intervals at both cohesions, although 8 to 23 % below the point estimates, and its sampling intervals exclude a model error that is of the order of the estimate itself at Pf ≈ 10 −3. No finite-element solution enters the pipeline at any stage. Abaqus confirmed the factors of safety of the 52 verification fields and the limit-state position of the design points, and served only as that check. 5. The failure mechanism and displacement response given failure. 

From the same 10 6 operator samples, the probability that an element lies in the slip band given failure agrees with the PINN at both cohesions, the operator underestimates the spread of the sliding-mass size, and at ¯c =7 kPa a crest displacement of 9 mm along the strength-reduction path is 3.9 times as likely as failure. These quantities are the value of the operator over a scalar surrogate, because for Pf alone it reduces to a linear model of ln F . The displacement is accumulated from a strengthened gravity state with deterministic stiffness and is not a serviceability settlement; a serviceability analysis requires gravity at s = 1 with random stiffness. 6. The limit-equilibrium question. The heterogeneous Bishop analysis underestimates Pf by factors of 2.6 and 8.3, with interval-bound ranges of 1.7 to 4.2 and 4.3 to 16. Its critical circles follow the PINN mechanism, so the error comes from a bias of about 3 % in F between the simplified Bishop formulation and the non-associated strength-reduction model, not from the circular slip surface. The bias grows in the weak fields, is ampli-fied in the tail, and is not removed by a constant correction. The share of the non-associated flow rule in the bias was not tested. Measured against the finite-element workflow it is meant to complement, the framework offers four benefits and carries one cost. It needs no labelled data, no commercial solver and no licence per core, because the PINN is trained on the equations alone and the constitutive model is differentiable code. It solves hundreds of realisations as one batched program on a single GPU, so the cost of a Monte Carlo set scales with the number of batches rather than with the number of fields. Its solutions feed an operator in the same framework, so a few hours of PINN runs are paid once and buy 10 6 evaluations in seconds, where a direct finite-element Monte Carlo at Pf ≈ 10 −3 would take years. Its operator is differentiable with respect to the random-field coefficients, which gives the FORM gradient directly and opens the way to inverse analysis. The cost is accuracy that depends on a solver budget fixed against a reference, a residual that does not vanish, a plastic strain that is under-resolved in the band and a random scatter of 3 % in F that inflates a failure probability of 10 −3 by up to a factor of two, so the finite-element method remains necessary as the independent check, the tail probabilities are bounded rather than exact, and the PINN is not a faster substitute for the finite-element method on a single slope. 37 Future work will replace the fixed solver budget with convergence-based step control and recompute the failure probabilities under it, apply gravity at s = 1 

with a random stiffness so that a serviceability limit state can be assessed, train the operator on finite-element solutions of the same fields as the control that this study lacks, add a physics loss to the operator (PINO), include the field statistics as operator inputs, and extend the method to three-dimensional slopes.

The factor of safety was recomputed with Abaqus on meshes with h =0.2, 0.1 and 0.05 m, with the random fields evaluated at the new centroids by Eq. (C.3) (Fig. D.1). The coarse mesh is within 2 % of the paper mesh (h = 0 .1 m) in all cases. For the homogeneous slopes, refinement to h = 0 .05 mchanges F by +0 .1, −2.0 and −3.7 % at ¯c = 35 , 5 and 7 kPa. For the random fields, the implicit solver stops on the finest mesh before a mechanism forms, and the last converged strength reduction factor is not a factor of safety. With automatic stabilisation on both meshes, the finest mesh is 2.6 % below the paper mesh for field 90, consistent with the homogeneous results. Stabilisation itself raises F by 11 % and is not used for reference values. Appendix E. Sensitivity to the solver budget The homogeneous slopes were solved again with plastic steps of 1.4 or 0.7 % of the SRF, 1000 or 3000 iterations per step and two stricter failure rules (Fig. E.1). With the budget of the paper (1.4 %, 1000 iterations), F lies within about 2 % of Abaqus at all three cohesions. With 0.7 % and 3000 iterations the elds converge better: at ¯c = 5 kPa the error of the plastic strain falls from 83 to 44 % and the correlation with Abaqus rises from 0.51 to 0.90. The failing trials, however, then appear below the Abaqus limit, and F is 4 to 6 % lower than Abaqus at ¯c = 5 and 7 kPa. At ¯c = 35 kPa F does not depend on the budget. The factor of safety of the weak slopes therefore depends on the budget by a few percent, and the agreement with Abaqus holds for the budget used in the paper.
