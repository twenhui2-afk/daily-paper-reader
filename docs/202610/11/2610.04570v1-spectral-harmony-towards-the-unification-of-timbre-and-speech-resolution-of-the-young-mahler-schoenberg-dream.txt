# “Spectral Harmony ”

# Towards the Unification of Timbre and Speech: 

# Resolution of the Young Mahler -Schön berg Dream 

Metric Space Models for Music on Informational Geometry 2

Yusei TAMURA (1, Shigekazu ISHIHARA (2 and Ken ITO (1 

(1 The University of Tokyo 

(2 Hiroshima International University 

In this article, we present a method of spectral serialization—envisioned by G. Mahler and A. Schönberg [1] between the late 19th and early 20th centuries—that enables the "selection of timbres rooted in musical structure." Based on information geometry, we evaluate the Wasserstein distance between polyphonic timbre spectra, as demonstrated in our previous paper [2], in terms of the second moment of spectral chords comprising three or more notes, expressed in squared hertz (Hz²). By embedding these quantities in a Banach space, we analyze specific passages from works by R. Strauss, Mahler, A. Berg, and A. Webern from the perspective of timbre, showing how the same chord exhibits different affinities and divergences depending on orchestration. Taking the wah-wah mute as an example, we propose a model that treats instrumental timbre and speech within the same Banach space, and we attempt to quantify the "vowel triangle" in Hz. These efforts constitute a partial but integrated solution to the historical "three problems of Schönberg"—serialism, Klangfarbenmelodie , and Sprechgesang —which neither Mahler nor Schönberg in their lifetimes, nor the postwar generation of composers including Nono, Boulez, and Stockhausen, was able to resolve fully. We aim to reconsider musical thought as a whole from the perspective of timbre and to open new possibilities for interpretation, performance, and composition. All tools are publicly available on GitHub, so that anyone can reproduce and extend these analyses. 

Keywords: timbre (tone color, Klangfarbe), polyphony, segregation, independence of voices, affinity, harmonic blending,

0：Introduction: Contrasting Perspectives on ‘Timbre’ — Ray Kurzweil and Luigi Nono 

Ray Kurzweil is widely known today for his concept of the ‘Singularity’ [3], which he introduced in 2005. Yet his earlier work, dating from the 1970s, was concerned with a very different problem: shortly after graduating from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Kurzweil began developing a device that could read printed text aloud for people with visual impairments. In collaboration with AT&T Bell Laboratories, he subsequently developed and commercialized the world’s first practical print-to-speech system, combining optical character recognition (OCR) with speech synthesis. The visually impaired musician Stevie Wonder was among its first users. 

In 1982, while visiting Wonder’s studio, Kurzweil was asked whether a computer could reproduce the sound of a real acoustic instrument. 

Kurzweil subsequently appointed Wonder as an adviser and began developing a sampling-based synthesizer. In 1984, he introduced the Kurzweil K250, an instrument that enabled users to digitally record the sounds of acoustic instruments and reproduce and perform them through a keyboard or similar interface. The K250 thus represented an important step toward the digital reproduction of instrumental timbres that had previously been associated almost exclusively with acoustic sound production. 

What Stevie Wonder sought was the ability to assign the sounds of real instruments—or digitally recorded samples sufficiently close to those sounds—to an electronic keyboard, which was considerably more convenient for him to play given his visual impairment [4]. 

Until the early 1980s, electronic and electroacoustic sounds were generally readily distinguishable from those produced by acoustic instruments. Electric pianos, electronic organs, and analog synthesizers all possessed characteristic sonic qualities of their own. The music of the West German group Kraftwerk, formed in 1970, provides a particularly clear example of a musical aesthetic in which the artificiality of electronic sound was not concealed but deliberately foregrounded. Electronic instruments were therefore often employed precisely in contexts that emphasized their difference from acoustic instruments. 

The work of Stevie Wonder and Ray Kurzweil can be understood in this context as part of a broader technological and musical shift toward the reproduction of familiar acoustic sounds by electronic means. Rather than celebrating the sonic distinctiveness of electronic instruments, their work pursued the opposite goal: making electronically generated or reproduced sounds increasingly difficult to distinguish from their acoustic counterparts. In this sense, the development of sampling technology marked a transition from the production of explicitly ‘synthetic’ timbres toward the digital simulation of natural instrumental sounds. This issue was particularly significant in the development of text-to-speech technologies for people with visual impairments. Here, the problem was not merely to reproduce speech intelligibly, but to reproduce the expressive and affective qualities of human speech. Although text-to-speech systems capable of conveying relatively sophisticated emotional expression did not become commercially widespread until the 2020s, the fundamental problem of how electronically generated sound can reproduce the expressive characteristics of natural human sound remains far from fully resolved. 

Now, whilst ‘instrumental sound’ has been actively re-engaged with by computer-driven electronic media since the 1980s, composers such as Luigi Nono criticized this trend in the late 1980s, stating that ‘the exploration of essential timbre had ceased, and efforts were confined to the superficial imitation of existing instruments [5]. 

There is no doubt that the innovation commissioned from Kurzweil by Stevie Wonder was, in this sense, a typically ‘music-structure-independent’ application. What, then, might be the nature of the ‘conception of timbre directly linked to musical structure’ that Schönberg proposed and which Nono and others sought to carry forward? 

In our previous article, we outlined the key points of an approach from ‘information geometry’ that addresses this question. In this article, through a ‘timbral-harmonic analysis’ of the works of Gustav Mahler—a composer and conductor who, alongside Schönberg, envisioned the future of music through ‘timbre’ but passed away before realizing his full potential—we wish to demonstrate, using Wasserstein geometry, the realization of the ‘serialization unifying timbre and speech=singing’—a concept imagined in the 1950s but never realized—as an example of the ‘treatment of timbre directly linked to musical structure’. 

1. Gustav Mahler's Prophetic Vision and his Untimely Death 

According to the violist Natalie Bauer-Lechner (1858–1921) [6], a close friend of Gustav Mahler, Mahler articulated clear and distinctive views on instrumental timbre during his lifetime. 

For Mahler, timbre was not merely a means of providing ‘coloring of sound for effect’; rather, it served to differentiate melodic lines and clarify the structure of polyphonic textures. He also observed that when instruments are played at the extremes of their registers, or when unconventional playing techniques are employed, distinctive and often highly charged timbral qualities emerge. In this respect, his thinking anticipated several concerns that would become prominent in twentieth- and twenty-first-century music, including register-based listening, extended playing techniques, and the incorporation of noise as a musical element. 

Mahler’s observations also point toward a conception of timbre that encompasses spatial perception. He noted that the timbre of a banda —a group of brass instruments positioned behind or off the main stage, for example—could introduce a new dimension into musical experience: one that was not À Vinko Globokar, le maestro 

4

simply a matter of increased volume, but also of a ‘sense of distance’ and ‘spatial expansiveness’ [4]. These observations suggest that, for Mahler, timbre could function simultaneously as a means of articulating musical structure and as a means of shaping the listener’s perception of musical space. 

Let us examine these ideas through several examples. In the present study, we focus on the brass instruments, and particularly on the trombone, in order to investigate the effects of timbral transformation from the perspective of mute usage. 

Figure 1 shows the passage in the first movement of Mahler’s Symphony No. 3 (1895–96) in which muted trombones make their first appearance. 

The full score contains detailed performance instructions in Mahler’s own hand, including such directions as ‘attach the mute’ and ‘remove the mute’ (these instructions were, of course, subsequently copied into the individual orchestral parts by the copyists). As a conductor with extensive practical experience, Mahler was meticulous about such performance details. At this point in the movement, the three trombones play in unison at ff with mutes, causing their combined sound to stand out distinctly in terms of timbre. 

Immediately before the B ♭-major ff chord that follows, Mahler specifically instructs the players to remove their mutes [7]. The abrupt change in instrumentation thus produces a corresponding change in timbre at a structurally significant moment. 

The use of brass mutes took a significant new direction the following year, in 1897. Richard Strauss, who was also active as a conductor, commissioned instrument makers in Germany to produce high-quality mutes designed specifically to minimize, or even eliminate, intonation problems. He subsequently employed these mutes in Don Quixote (1897), using them in the brass section, including the tubas, in ways that altered the pitch relationships among the instruments.

Assuming that the same pitch is produced by a sine wave, its frequency would be approximately 370 Hz. However, a pure sine wave cannot exist as a perfectly sustained physical sound in the external environment; any audible sound inevitably exhibits fluctuations in its temporal, spectral, and phase characteristics. The total spectral displacement produced by fitting a mute to the trombone is 326 Hz, which is of the same order of magnitude as the centre frequency itself. The resulting spectral change is therefore by no means negligible. 

Furthermore, a spectrum in which all power is concentrated at a single frequency corresponds to a Dirac delta function, for which the Shannon entropy [12] is zero. Since real instrumental sounds contain information distributed across multiple spectral components, their Shannon entropy, when represented as physical sound spectra, can be expressed in bits. 

At the same time, timbre is not merely a physical property of a sound spectrum but a perceptual phenomenon arising through auditory and cognitive processes. For this reason, we also evaluate sensory dissonance [14] using Sethares’ Sensory Dissonance model [13]. For the normalized spectrum 

of the trombone F♯, the change in physical information content, as measured by the change in Shannon 

entropy, is relatively small: both the muted and unmuted sounds have entropy values of approximately 11 bits. By contrast, the corresponding values of Sethares’ Sensory Dissonance differ by approximately a factor of 72, indicating a substantially greater sensory dissonance for the muted sound. 

In other words, the comparison suggests that subjective sensory dissonance and physical information content, as measured by Shannon entropy, are only weakly correlated in this case.

As mentioned earlier, the frequency of F♯ is approximately 370 Hz when represented as a sine wave, while that of C♯ is approximately 277 Hz. Therefore, if both tones were represented as sine waves, 

their Wasserstein distance would be approximately 93 Hz. In contrast, for the actual trombone sounds, 

the Wasserstein distance between the F♯ and C♯ is 403 Hz without a mute and 390 Hz with a mute —both substantially greater than the value obtained for the corresponding sine waves. This is to be expected, since actual instrumental timbres contain and therefore require the transport of many more spectral components than sine waves, which have no timbral complexity. 

If we subtract the 93-Hz Wasserstein distance of the corresponding sine waves from each of these values, the resulting timbral gaps are 310 Hz and 297 Hz, respectively. Although the numerical values differ, they remain of the same order of magnitude. 

A markedly different result emerges, however, when we consider the ‘Sethares dissonance due to the difference in pitch’ associated with the presence or absence of the trombone mute. According to 

Sethares’ model, the interval between C♯ and F♯ —a perfect fourth—produces a dissonance of approximately 2.0 arbitrary units when the trombone is played without a mute. By contrast, the same perfect fourth, when the trombone is played with a mute, produces a dissonance of approximately 16.3 arbitrary units. Thus, despite the interval being identical, the dissonance differs by a factor of approximately eight as a result of the change in timbre. 

Returning to this perspective and reconsidering Fig. 1-b, we can see that the change in timbre produces a substantial change in the perceived dissonance of the interval, despite the pitch relationship remaining unchanged. This illustrates a fundamental point: ‘even though the intervals are the same, they are not necessarily consonant to the same degree.’ In other words, whereas conventional harmonic theory generally treats intervallic relationships independently of timbre, a timbre-sensitive approach to harmony reveals that the consonance or dissonance of an interval cannot always be determined from its pitch relationship alone. The present analysis therefore suggests that the consonance and dissonance of an interval should be evaluated in conjunction with the timbral characteristics of the sounds that constitute it.

With regard to the French horn, it is worth noting that, in contrast to the trombone, the degree of Sethares’ dissonance is significantly reduced when using articulation techniques. 3. ‘Klangfarbe Null Grades (The Zero degree of Timbre)’ … Instrumental accord from the Perspective of Gaussian Trial Timbre 

In the previous section, we indicated the frequency of a sine wave—which produces the same pitch— as a reference for the pitch of instrumental sounds. W. Sethares also chooses the sine wave as a reference point, regarding it as the ‘origin’ of timbre. 

If we assume that timbre is something intended to be heard by the human ear, then the ‘sine wave’ becomes something akin to absolute zero—an unattainable ideal. This is because a perfect sine wave cannot perform any physical work [18]; consequently, it cannot cause our eardrums to vibrate, nor can our auditory system perceive it.

4. The Serial Integration of  Klangfarbenmelodie and Sprechgesang 

The years 1924–25, when Berg’s Wozzeck was first performed, coincided with the introduction of the Harmon mute in Chicago, Illinois, USA. 

This period also coincided with the premiere of George Gershwin’s Rhapsody in Blue (1924), performed by Paul Whiteman’s jazz band. Ferdie Grofé, who arranged the work, simply marked ‘Wha-Wha mute’ in the trumpet part, leaving the precise tonal variations to the performers’ interpretation. 

The ‘Wha-Wha’ mute used here was originally a simple cleaning device known as a ‘plunger’. The ‘Harmon’ mute, however, soon became the standard. George Schlusselburg, a Chicago resident, applied for a patent for the Harmon mute in 1924. The patent was granted in 1925, after which the mute came into use and rapidly gained popularity. 

This mute has a hole in the center, and the “tone color” is altered by opening and closing this aperture by hand. Whilst Harmon mutes are available for trumpets and trombones, and are also known for the French horn, they are not generally manufactured for euphoniums or tubas, as it is difficult to manipulate the bell by hand on these instruments. By opening and closing this aperture with the hand, the Harmon mute can produce a ‘wow-wow’ effect in the tone. 

“Closing the opening of the Harmon mute completely” with the palm of the hand produces a timbral change similar to [u], whilst opening it produces a sound similar to [a]. Interestingly, if one shifts just a few finger(s) to create a gap—resulting in a ‘half-open’ position—the timbre changes to a sound resembling the vowel [o] in spoken language.

Just as Wagner was unable to use straight mutes for the trombone during his lifetime, the rise in pitch and the resulting instability in playability caused by the insertion of mutes were serious problems. Whilst custom-made products were used in Germany for R. Strauss’s “Don Quixote”, in the United States from the 1920s onwards, mutes for brass instruments saw rapid development and widespread adoption, originating in jazz bands. 

When the Nazis seized power, Schönberg, who was Jewish, went into exile outside Austria, whilst Berg and Webern remained in Europe. Whilst mutes for string and brass instruments were heavily utilised in the works of G. Mahler and the Second Viennese School to achieve ‘timbral changes’, approaches such as the Harmon mute—which alter the mute itself—cannot be found in the works of this ‘pioneering generation of timbral melody’. 

Not only in the scores of Berg, who died prematurely in Austria in 1935, and Webern, who was accidentally shot and killed in 1945, but also in those of Schönberg—who lived in the United States until the 1950s—no specifications for mutes other than the orthodox straight mute can be found.

A. Schönberg, who left Austria in 1933, took up a professorship at the University of California, Los Angeles, in 1936, took up residence in Beverly Hills, and became friends with his neighbor, G. Put simply, it is highly likely that Schönberg himself was aware, in terms of sound, that the ‘timbre’—or ‘vowel’—changes produced by the opening and closing of the harmon mute, as commonly used in jazz, could be achieved on brass instruments. However, Schönberg himself did not think along these lines, and there is no evidence within his body of work that he viewed the opening and closing of the plunger or Harmon mute as relating to ‘spoken language’. The same applies to Alban Berg and Anton Webern; indeed, the use of the wow-wow mute itself remained far from commonplace in orchestral music right up until the 21st century. The work of Ernst Ernst Krenek and Hans Eisler, amongst others, was premised on ‘jazz’, and it was not until the post-Second World War generation that an approach emerged which directly engaged with the changes in sound  themselves.

So, has this Viennese approach to music come to an end? We would hesitate to say so. One witness to the contrary is Beat Furrer ( 1954-), the Swiss-born Austrian composer and conductor, who continues to work in Vienna today. His music is distinguished by a style that systematically brings noise into the fabric of musical sound. In the genealogy of musical thought, his work may be seen as a living inheritance: the youthful aspirations of Gustav Mahler and Arnold Schönberg, preserved and carried forward into the twenty -first century. 

It is essential to acknowledge the immense contribution made by Vinko Globokar (1934-) to the exploration of the underlying relationship between musical timbre and spoken language [20]. During the early days of the French National Institute for Music and Acoustics (IRCAM), Pierre Boulez appointed Globokar as director of the ‘Instrument and Speech Research Department’, and practical investigations into the boundary between instrumental sound and spoken language were carried out in collaboration with István Matuz (flute) and Heinz Holliger (oboe), amongst others. 

Boulez had in mind Schönberg’s three problems, which were the subject of vigorous debate in the 1950s, particularly the ‘Sprechgesang problem’ and the ‘Klangfarbenmelodie problem’, and his approach focused on their common ground. However, due to the limitations of knowledge at the time, analogue sound processing, and computer technology in the 1970s, the discussion diverged from the behavior of sound itself towards physicality involving action, literary connotations, and even social and political expression through the ‘voice’, and thus failed to reach sufficient depth.

Although an analysis of ‘vowels’ produced by brass instruments and human speech sounds within the same acoustic space is beyond the scope of this article and will be addressed in a subsequent article, various correspondences can already be identified between syllables and pitch-defined polyphonic structures. 

The digital processing technologies mentioned at the beginning of this article—including Kurzweil’s sampling synthesizer and automatic text-to-speech systems—came to the fore from the 1980s onwards, while the issues that had been explored before the 1970s largely fell outside the scope of these technologies [21]. 

The question raised in this paper is by no means a new one. Rather, it is a problem that has been posed repeatedly over the past two or three centuries, yet has never been adequately formulated. 

Let us take a closer look at the subtitle of Helmholtz’s seminal work, published in 1863: Die Lehre von den Tonempfindungen , or On the Sensations of Tone , whose subtitle reads als physiologische Grundlage für die Theorie der Musik (‘as a Physiological Basis for the Theory of Music’). 

Neither Lord Rayleigh (John William Strutt), Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman, nor György von Békésy showed a sustained interest in elucidating the sense of tonal progression in just intonation—a subject to which Helmholtz, a pianist with absolute pitch, devoted considerable attention. This, however, in no way diminishes the value of these unresolved questions. Rather, it points towards a vast and largely uncharted field in which ‘the theory of music’, in its fullest sense, remains to a remarkable extent unexplored. 

5. Brief Concluding remarks,  Summary of Unresolved Issues and Acknowledgements 

The progress of this thesis corresponds closely to the point reached by Helmholtz in the middle of the 1850s. In establishing physiological acoustics, Helmholtz developed a method for evaluating dissonance through the phenomenon of ‘beating’. By around 1859–61, he had largely completed his analysis of speech sounds and his synthesis of vowels using tuning forks. However, a fully functional version of his vowel-tuning-fork system, which employed electromagnets, was not achieved until 1905, after his death. 

With regard to instrumental timbre, the present research has reached the stage at which the fundamental framework for representing spectra, quantified in hertz, by means of Wasserstein geometry has been established. A detailed examination of the specific treatment of timbre will be presented in a subsequent paper. A separate paper is also being prepared on the theoretical foundations and mathematical formulation of the method. 

With regard to speech sounds, the present study has so far been limited to the classification of a À Vinko Globokar, le maestro 

24 

restricted set of vowels. The treatment of consonants, attack characteristics, and other aspects of speech will require further investigation. 

These issues relate to the three problems posed by Schönberg at Luigi Nono’s seminar in 1987, namely: 

1. The treatment of sound—the serial approach 

2. The distinction between singing and speaking 

3. Klangfarbenmelodie 

After approximately forty years of engagement with these three problem formulations, the present paper demonstrates, in a preliminary manner, that Problems 2 and 3 are essentially manifestations of the same underlying question, and that their treatment can be integrated with Problem 1. More specifically, the serial procedure envisaged here is a sequential mapping of spectral vertices based on the Wasserstein distances between spectra. In this sense, the present study suggests that a unified serial treatment of timbre, speech, and melody is mathematically feasible. 

We should also note that the Wasserstein distance is defined here not in physical real space, but in the probability space of a normalised spectrum. In other words, although we perceive music and speech through our ears as physical phenomena occurring in real space, the present approach can become fully effective only when the problems of spatial acoustic perception—including both auditory and cerebral processes—are properly taken into account. Only then can this framework contribute to an understanding of the ‘wisdom of music’ as it actually resounds in the physical world.
