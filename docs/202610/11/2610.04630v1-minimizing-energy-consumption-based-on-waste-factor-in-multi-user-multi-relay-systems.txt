# Minimizing Energy Consumption based on Waste Factor in Multi-user Multi-relay Systems 

Nurullah Sevim, Mostafa Ibrahim, Sabit Ekin, Senior Member, IEEE, and Theodore S. Rappaport, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract The escalating energy demands of modern wireless networks make energy-efficient system design a central challenge for future 6G deployments. Relay-assisted communication has long been recognized as a powerful tool for improving coverage and throughput, tracing its roots to early repeater communication systems and the foundational relay channel models in information theory [1]. Meanwhile, modern relay selection and routing strategies are primarily driven by rate-based or transmit-power-centric metrics, which often neglect hardware-level losses that is independent of the transmitted signal. As networks grow denser and more heterogeneous, signal-independent hardware inefficiencies and circuit power consumption increasingly dominate the total energy consumption and fundamentally alter the optimality of relay assignment decisions. To address the challenge of modeling signal-independent power consumption in relay-assisted systems, the Waste Factor (WF) framework is adopted: a hardware-aware, unifying metric that explicitly captures signal-independent power losses across cascaded transceivers and wire-less channels. The optimal relay–user assignment problem is formulated as an energy-minimization problem subject to communication feasibility and quality-of-service constraints, ensuring that each user achieves a minimum required received power (equivalently, a target link reliability). The formulation jointly accounts for component inefficiencies, propagation losses, and relay-selection constraints. The proposed framework demonstrates that the WF formulation provides a fundamentally new way to model relay-assisted communication, enabling hardware-aware energy-per-bit minimization and a structured representation that enables computationally efficient analysis. Simulation results confirm that WF-based relay assignment reduces total energy per bit while improving the ability of users to satisfy minimum received-power requirements, outperforming conventional formulations across both metrics. I. Introduction The global demand for digital communication continues to grow at an unprecedented pace as wireless networks evolve to support increasingly dense deployments, higher data rates, and a vast ecosystem of connected devices [2]. Emerging sixth-generation (6G) communication services, including immersive extended reality, autonomous systems, and massive machine-type communication, will require orders-of-magnitude improvements in both throughput and coverage. However, this rapid technological expansion is accompanied by escalating energy consumption across information and communication technologies (ICT) [3]. Recent estimates suggest that ICT already accounts for a measurable share of global electricity consumption, a trend expected to intensify as networks become more heterogeneous and computation-intensive [4]. Improving the energy efficiency of communication systems has therefore become a central research priority, both to reduce operational expenditures and to ensure the environmental sustainability of future wireless infrastructures. While conventional wireless design emphasizes spectral efficiency and coverage reliability, modern base stations, access points, relays, and user equipment incur substantial signal-independent power consumption from local oscillators, data converters, digital processing, and control circuitry, while power amplifiers and RF front-ends add losses that can dominate 3

total power in low-SNR or low-rate regimes. Classical link-level metrics such as energy per bit or transmit power alone fail to capture this interplay between hardware inefficiency, non-ideal signal chains, and multi-hop routing. Widely used efficiency metrics such as Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) share this blind spot, as they do not relate energy to useful signal output and can mislead system-level assessments [5]. To address the limitations of rate-based and transmit-power-centric metrics in capturing hardware inefficiencies, a recently introduced metric known as the Waste Factor (WF) -also called Waste Figure when in dB units- provides a unified framework for modeling power dissipation in cascaded wireless systems [6–8]. WF aggregates the multiplicative and additive losses incurred across transmitters, receivers, channels, and relays, yielding a hardware-aware representation of the actual energy cost associated with reliable communication. By distinguishing between useful radiated energy (e.g. energy consumption along the signal path between source and sink) and signal-independent consumption (e.g., energy consumption off of the signal path), WF enables thorough system-level analyses for energy used both in information transmission as well as total energy consumption in the communication system. Relay-assisted wireless communication has been widely investigated as an effective mech-anism to improve coverage probability, link reliability, and energy consumption per bit in networks where direct transmission is limited by propagation loss or power constraints. A growing body of work focuses on optimizing relay operation through joint resource allocation and system-level optimization. For instance, [9] studies unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)– relayed wireless systems and formulates joint power and trajectory optimization problems to either maximize the minimum achievable rate or minimize total power consumption under rate constraints. While such formulations enable flexible system design, they are primarily based on rate maximization or transmit-power minimization and often result in highly coupled non-convex optimization problems. In addition, signal-independent hardware inefficiencies are not explicitly accounted for, thereby limiting the accuracy of energy-consumption modeling. In contrast, a WF-based formulation provides a unified, hardware-aware characterization of energy consumption, offering a more structured representation of relay-assisted communication and potentially reducing modeling complexity by directly operating on end-to-end energy metrics. Another line of research focuses on selecting which relay assists each source–destination transmission and allocating transmit power across the corresponding links, with the goal of 4

reducing computational complexity and signaling overhead. In [10], a semi-distributed user relaying algorithm is proposed for amplify-and-forward relay networks, where each relay independently evaluates whether it can support a given source–destination transmission by comparing its local channel conditions against a predefined threshold. A central controller then selects one relay from the set of feasible candidates to forward the signal. This approach emphasizes practicality and low complexity by avoiding full global channel state information exchange, while still achieving achievable-rate performance close to that of fully centralized relay selection schemes. In contrast, coordinated relay transmission strategies for multi-user cellular systems are investigated in [11], where joint transmission and interference cancellation principles are used to serve direct and relayed users simultaneously. Optimal scheduling is shown to require exhaustive search, motivating suboptimal yet efficient co-ordination algorithms that achieve near-optimal system throughput. While these studies demonstrate the benefits of coordination and relay selection, they primarily optimize rate or capacity metrics and do not explicitly model hardware inefficiencies or signal-independent power consumption. Cooperative transmission from multiple geographically separated transmitters has also been extensively studied in the context of Coordinated Multi-Point (CoMP) communication. In CoMP systems, multiple base stations or transmission points jointly serve a user in order to improve coverage, reduce outage probability, improve received signal power, and boost cell-edge performance. Such coordination can transform otherwise interfering signals into useful contributions at the receiver and significantly improve system throughput and coverage in dense deployments [12, 13]. This paper investigates the design of energy-efficient multi-user and multi-relay wireless systems under the WF framework. Conventional relay-assignment approaches are typically based on rate maximization, transmit-power minimization, or shortest-path metrics, which implicitly assume idealized transceivers or treat hardware-related power consumption as a secondary effect. Such formulations often fail to capture the dominant signal-independent power losses arising from practical RF front-ends, baseband processing, and cascaded transceiver chains. These losses become particularly significant in short-range relaying and dense multi-hop deployments. As a result, relay assignments that appear optimal under rate- or power-based metrics can be energy-inefficient when realistic hardware constraints are taken into account. 5

Unlike prior WF studies [8, 14] that identify analytical conditions for favorable relaying on a single link, this work extends the framework to the network level, jointly addressing relay selection and user assignment. This shift from link-level to network-level minimization yields a fundamentally different problem structure that couples multi-hop cascades, hetero-geneous hardware, relay capacity limits, and spatial user distributions, tradeoffs invisible to conventional rate- or shortest-path formulations. Operating frequency also shapes feasibility. This work considers millimeter-wave (mmWave) communication, a cornerstone of 5G and 6G for its large bandwidths and high data rates. mmWave links suffer severe path loss, limited diffraction, and strong blockage sensitivity, forcing dense deployments, short-range links, and relay assistance to maintain coverage. These same characteristics make mmWave especially sensitive to hardware inefficiency and signal-independent power, amplifying the value of hardware-aware metrics such as WF. To focus on the structural implications of hardware inefficiencies and relay assignment un-der the Waste Factor framework, the channel model in this work is treated deterministically through distance-dependent large-scale path loss. This choice isolates first-order propagation effects and enables clear interpretation of the resulting energy trends without the additional variability introduced by stochastic channel fluctuations. Extensions to probabilistic and site-specific propagation models, including log-normal shadow fading, are natural and are left for future work. Although the WF framework allows several structured special configurations to be ex-pressed in closed form, the general relay-assignment problem exhibits inherent combina-torial complexity. In particular, when the number of users and relays grows, the optimal assignment becomes computationally intractable due to the exponential growth of possible configurations. To overcome the combinatorial complexity and computational intractability of the relay-assignment problem, a suite of methods is employed with complementary strengths: greedy strategies for decoupled regimes, min-cost flow (MCF) reformulation for exact polynomial-time optimization under single-link capacitated assignment, and construc-tive/Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP)-based methods for scalable optimization in multi-relay cooperative regimes. Exhaustive search is included as a benchmark when the network dimensions permit, enabling explicit quantification of the optimality–complexity tradeoff. The relay-assisted architecture considered in this work bears conceptual similarities to 6

joint-transmission CoMP, where multiple transmission points cooperatively deliver data to a user. However, while the CoMP literature primarily focuses on spectral efficiency and interference mitigation [15, 16], this work addresses a complementary problem: the energy-efficient selection of cooperating transmission points under a hardware-aware energy model based on the Waste Factor framework. This energy-centric perspective, based on the WF framework, is particularly relevant for future dense mmWave deployments, where energy consumption and hardware inefficiencies play a critical role. The major contributions of this work are summarized as follows:  

> •

WF-based system modeling: The WF framework is extended to multi-user, multi-relay networks, with explicit end-to-end energy-per-bit expressions for candidate relay paths capturing component inefficiencies, propagation losses, and signal-independent power.  

> •

Energy-minimizing relay assignment: The relay-selection and user-assignment problem is formulated as total energy-per-bit minimization under communication feasibility constraints, including minimum received power requirements and relay capacity limits.  

> •

Exact solutions for structured cases: Several structured special cases are shown to admit exact solutions, enabling optimal relay assignment in polynomial time.  

> •

Scalable algorithms with quantified tradeoffs: For general large-scale systems, scalable sub-optimal algorithms are developed that achieve near-optimal energy-per-bit perfor-mance at low computational complexity.  

> •

Comprehensive evaluation: Simulations quantify total energy per bit and the fraction of users failing the minimum received power requirement, showing that WF-based assign-ment reduces energy consumption while improving coverage feasibility over conventional approaches. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section II reviews the WF model and develops the WF-based energy formulation for multi-hop links. Section III introduces the multi-user multi-relay system model, and Section IV formulates the relay-assignment energy-minimization problem. Section V develops scalable sub-optimal algorithms for the general case. Section VI provides simulation results and performance evaluations. Finally, Section VII concludes the paper and outlines directions for future research. 7

III. System Model A downlink cooperative wireless network is considered, composed of a single base station (BS), a set of R heterogeneous decode-and-forward (DF) relays, and K stationary user equipments (UEs). Each UE is allocated an orthogonal bandwidth B, eliminating inter-user interference; this FDMA-like operation isolates energy-efficiency effects from interference management, focusing the analysis on relay selection and hardware-aware energy modeling. The BS and relays use multiple transmit antennas while UEs have a single receive antenna. The number of effective transmit chains at each relay sets its serving capacity, limiting the number of users it can simultaneously support. All relays operate in half-duplex mode and, unless otherwise stated, employ decode-and-forward operation, regenerating a clean copy of the signal before forwarding. The system operates in the mmWave regime. Although mmWave links incur higher free-space path loss than sub-6 GHz systems, the higher achievable antenna gains can offset much of this loss; regardless of frequency, the WF framework enables a true energy-consumption analysis while highlighting the role of relay-assisted transmission for coverage and energy efficiency. Conclusion This paper developed a Waste Factor (WF)–based framework for hardware-aware energy minimization in multi-user, multi-relay wireless networks. Unlike conventional rate- or transmit-power-based formulations, the proposed framework explicitly accounts for propa-gation losses, cascaded transceiver inefficiencies, and signal-independent circuit power in a unified end-to-end energy-per-bit metric. The main result of this work is that WF is not only an energy-efficiency metric, but also a structured modeling framework that makes relay-assignment problems more transparent and computationally tractable. Several structured special cases were shown to admit exact polynomial-time solutions, while the general multi-relay problem remains combinatorial and requires scalable approximations. To address this regime, greedy, min-cost flow, and MILP-based methods were developed to achieve strong energy performance at practical complexity. 30 

The simulation results provide clear evidence for two main system-level outcomes. First, WF-based relay assignment reduces the total energy per bit consumed relative to conven-tional approaches that rely on proxy objectives, such as rate or transmit power. Second, under minimum received-power constraints, multi-relay cooperation significantly improves coverage feasibility by enabling power aggregation across spatially distributed relays. Overall, the results show that WF enables both more faithful energy modeling and more effective optimization of relay-assisted wireless systems. This makes the framework partic-ularly valuable for dense and heterogeneous future networks, where hardware inefficiencies and feasibility constraints play a dominant role.
