## Abstract 

Datacenter services must meet tight tail latency service-level objectives. Research on improving tail latency focuses on reducing queuing delay by approximating optimal request scheduling policies. As request scheduling nears optimality, the primary way to further improve tail latency is to reduce the service time of requests. Unfortunately, datacenter services lack obvious hotspots for general service time optimization. We point out a new optimization opportunity in targeting the tail service time of services with dispersive service times, which are common in datacenters. To show the feasibility and benefit of this approach, we present PonyTail, a performance analysis methodology and toolkit for analyzing service-level outlier behaviors associated with tail service time. PonyTail helps performance engineers identify control-flow and mi-croarchitectural patterns responsible for service time outliers, as well as their root causes. We apply PonyTail to analyze five latency-critical services, including an in-memory database system and an online search engine. Based on PonyTail’s analysis, we optimize some of the services, improving their 99th percentile service time and latency by 10.7%–46% and 21%–5 ×, respectively. 

## 1 Introduction 

Datacenter services, from storage and data retrieval systems to machine learning applications, increasingly face tight tail latency service-level objectives (SLOs), such as the 99.9th percentile latency being a few milliseconds [12, 18, 99] or microseconds [8, 47, 69, 109]. These services form the back-end of large-scale online platforms like social networks, web search, and e-commerce, which must respond to user requests quickly to improve user experience [18] and revenue [3, 31, 79]. Minimizing datacenter service tail latency is crucial, as each user request triggers hundreds of concurrent service calls [19, 76] and the slowest service response determines the request’s overall latency [9, 18, 26, 54, 70]. Tail latency is usually dominated not by the time it takes the service to process a request—its service time —but by queuing delay in the operating system or service runtime system [42, 52, 82]. Queuing delay occurs when the request arrival rate momentarily exceeds the service’s service rate, e.g., due to bursts of requests or service of a long-latency request blocking short-latency requests [21, 42, 47, 52, 82]. Most efforts to improve tail latency therefore target reduc-ing queuing delay. Queuing delay can be avoided by overpro-visioning servers so individual server load is kept low, but this approach is wasteful and not cost-effective [20, 66]. Tail latency can be minimized in a resource-efficient manner by effective request scheduling (queue management). Queuing theory has identified optimal work-conserving request sched-uling policies 1 for minimizing tail latency [102]. Systems research has explored efficient mechanisms for implementing (or approximating) these policies—even for microsecond-scale service times—using work stealing [80, 82] and request preemption [42, 47, 63]. Compared to alternative (non-work-conserving) policies that leverage knowledge of the service time distribution [21], the near-optimal policies sustain higher throughput for the same tail latency SLOs. With request scheduling subsystems tightly approximating optimal policies, however, the importance of improving ser-vice time grows. The primary way to further reduce queuing delay under an optimal scheduling policy is to reduce request service time, as the time a request spends enqueued depends on the service times of all other pending requests [34, 102]. The problem is that service performance is already heav-ily optimized, with hyperscale datacenter operators even per-forming automatic profile-guided optimization [14, 28, 58, 81, 111] based on datacenter-wide profiling [6, 48, 86]. Moreover, datacenter services typically do not have obvious hotspots that can be optimized to improve common-case service time [48]. Driving tail latency improvements by improving service time will thus entail new optimization strategies. 

Opportunity: Tail optimization We point out a general op-timization opportunity in targeting the tail service time of services with dispersive (variable) service times, which are common in datacenters [10, 13, 54]. In such services, it is meaningful to distinguish slow from average service times and to optimize the slowest requests—rather than optimizing hotspots that broadly impact a large fraction of requests. Tail service time optimization is appealing for services with dis-persive service times, as improving just the tail service times can have an outsized impact on queuing delay (§ 3). Tail optimization targets service-level factors, not external factors such as (1) hardware behaviors, e.g., resource competi-tion from co-running programs [20, 53, 67, 107, 114], power limit-related throttling [37, 52, 66, 98], and energy manage-ment [72, 73, 103]; (2) operating system sources like inter-rupt management [25, 38, 61, 62, 80]; or (3) runtime system activities such as garbage collection [49, 104, 106]. While variability in service time is often attributed to such exter-nal factors, many latency-critical services exhibit dispersive service times even when external performance fluctuations

1 PonyTail (§ 4) We present PonyTail, a performance anal-ysis methodology and toolkit for developers to explore tail service time optimization possibilities. PonyTail’s goal is to assist performance engineers in analyzing service-level out-lier behaviors responsible for tail service time in cases where hotspot-oriented profiling is less applicable. Example PonyTail use cases include analysis of service time variability in a specific request type and identification of slow requests in a mix of request types. 2

PonyTail’s methodology seeks to attribute outlier service times to the following service-level causes, which together characterize all possible reasons for a tail request being slower than typical requests. The relevant requests might have a unique architectural (code) footprint —i.e., they take a distinct execution path, either statically (different code) or dynam-ically (e.g., executing more loop iterations than in average requests). Alternatively, outlier requests might have a distinct 

microarchitectural footprint , such as a certain load instruction incurring a cache miss instead of a hit. PonyTail’s toolkit enables developers to identify outlier footprints and their root causes. It provides a familiar profiler workflow, consisting of online recording of every request’s footprints, followed by offline analysis that identifies unique aspects of slow request footprints. To help subsequent anal-ysis of these unique aspects’ root causes, PonyTail supports request-level collection of user-specified performance counter measurements with a similar workflow. PonyTail’s data collection leverages modern capabilities of Intel processors, such as Processor Trace [40] and top-down microarchitecture analysis performance counters [40]. It also requires minimal service source code modification to demarcate request boundaries by invoking PonyTail methods. Our evaluation (§ 6) shows that PonyTail’s data collection has moderate overhead—increasing mean service time by 9%–24%, depending on the service time scale—but does not change the shape of the service time distribution. While this overhead precludes datacenter-wide, 24/7 deployment of PonyTail data collection, we believe that it is acceptable for intermittent or manual data collection for performance analysis. PonyTail data collection can also be run on multiple server machines (i.e., service instances), thereby minimizing disruption to each individual instance while still collecting sufficient data to capture tail events.

PonyTail successfully identifies that service time outliers in these services are correlated with distinct microarchitectural and code footprints (both static and dynamic). Based on in-sights obtained from examining these footprints and their root causes, we optimize some of the services, each in a different way—from low-level optimization to configuration tuning. Our tail optimizations improve the 99th percentile service time of TPC-C transactions in the in-memory database sys-tem silo [97] by 10% and its maximum service time by 4.3×. Improving the cache’s hit rate (e.g., via warmup) results in reductions of 53 × and 22 × in the 99th percentile service time and latency, respectively. In the online search engine xapian [105] and the speech recognition service sphinx [100], we find that tail service time is due to factors such as input-dependent loops which dominate service time, and thus cause service time outliers for certain inputs. In these cases, PonyTail’s unique tail-oriented analyses lead to discovery of optimizations that improve ser-vice times across the board. Some of these optimizations may be independently interesting, as they target under-explored microarchitectural mechanisms. Our optimizations improve 

xapian and sphinx service times by 46% and 10.7%, re-spectively, yielding improvements of up to 5 × and 2 × in latency (across the board). Overall, this paper shows that opportunities to further re-duce tail latency exist and that tail service time optimization— facilitated by PonyTail—can unlock this potential, thereby complementing existing optimization approaches. 

## 2 Background 

This section provides background on performance analysis on Intel CPUs. Familiar readers can skip this section. 

CPU structure Like all modern high-performance CPUs, In-tel CPUs employ a superscalar out-of-order (OoO) microar-chitectural design [89]. “Superscalar” means that the pro-cessor can initiate execution of multiple instructions (from a single instruction stream) per clock cycle [36, 45]. “Out-of-order” (or dynamic ) means that the processor determines instruction execution order according to the dynamic avail-ability of instruction inputs and hardware execution units [96], 

> 2

executing instructions possibly out of program order while retaining the appearance of a sequential execution. A pro-cessor core consists of a frontend , which fetches instructions from memory, and a backend , which executes instructions. The frontend decodes fetched instructions into internal micro-operations ( μ-ops), which are subsequently dispatched to the backend, where they await execution. The backend is respon-sible for executing a fixed-size sliding instruction window of the latest fetched μ-ops. μ-ops retire in program order, exiting the instruction window once they have executed and committing their results to architectural state. The backend computes dependencies between the μ-ops and schedules their execution (by issuing them to an execution unit) regard-less of their position in the instruction window, based only on availability of hardware execution resources and dependency constraints. The CPU relies on branch prediction [36] to avoid stalling instruction fetching when encountering a branch instruction. Once the branch executes, the prediction’s correctness is validated. If the prediction was correct, the branch instruc-tion can eventually retire. Otherwise, on a misprediction, the backend’s state is rolled back to its last known valid state (which was checkpointed upon making the prediction), thereby squashing the mispredicted branch and all subsequent (mispredicted) μ-ops in the instruction window 

Top-down microarchitecture analysis [108] TMA is a method-ology for identifying hardware-related bottlenecks. It uses per-formance monitoring events to measure utilization of pipeline slots , which represent the CPU resources required to process a 

μ-op. Each pipeline slot available during the program’s execu-tion is classified into one of four categories. If the slot is empty (stalled), it is classified according to the stall reason: (1) fron-tend (unable to fill the slot, e.g., due to an instruction cache miss) or (2) backend (unable to process the slot, e.g., due to an unavailable resource). If the slot is non-empty (utilized), it is classified according to whether it (3) was mispredicted or (4) eventually retired. TMA can further break down each category into finer-grained metrics. The following performance monitoring mechanisms are provided by the performance monitoring unit (PMU) in Intel CPUs. They can be enabled by a profiler without cooperation from the analyzed program. 

Processor event-based sampling [40, § 20.9] PEBS enables efficient, precise sampling of hardware events. In contrast to traditional sampling, recording the execution context of each sample (e.g., timestamp and program counter) is done by the CPU. This information is recorded in an in-memory buffer, with an interrupt raised only when the buffer fills up. 

Processor Trace [40, § 33] PT captures a control-flow trace of the program into an in-memory buffer in compact for-mat. For every executed branch (except unconditionally taken branches), it records whether the branch was taken or not, its target (for an indirect branch), and (optionally) a timestamp. 

## 3 Motivation 

Service tail latency SLOs are specified with respect to re-sponse time , which is the sum of a request’s queuing delay and service time. Queuing delay dominates response time un-less the system operates well below capacity, and is therefore the main target for optimization. As a result, implementa-tions of tail-optimal request scheduling policies have seen continuous efficiency improvements, to the point that further tail latency reductions will require improving service times— but at the same time, it is becoming harder to find software optimizations that broadly improve service times (§ 3.1). To address these trends, we argue for optimizing tail service time, as it can have an outsized impact on response time (§ 3.2). 

3.1 Need for tail service time optimization 

Queuing theory has established an optimal configuration for minimizing tail latency: a single request queue managed with a tail-optimal scheduling policy, which depends on the nature of the service time distribution [102]. For low-to-moderately dispersive service times, the optimal policy is first-come-first-served (FCFS) [84]. For highly dispersive distributions, an optimal policy is processor sharing (PS) [77], where each request receives an equal fraction of CPU capacity. Implementations of these optimal configurations must ad-dress problems that are abstracted away in the queuing theory model, such as multi-core synchronization of a global queue and how to time-share cores between requests in PS. Over time, implementations have steadily addressed the overheads caused by these problems [21, 25, 42, 47, 63, 80, 82]. State-of-the-art request schedulers can now efficiently implement (or closely approximate) the optimal configurations even for microsecond-scale service times. For example, the overhead of the Concord [42] preemptive scheduler (which approxi-mates PS) on 500 μs requests is only 1.5%–5% (depending on the hardware) even for a scheduling quantum of 2.5 μs. Overall, request scheduling overhead is now negligible, ex-cept perhaps for single- or double-digit μs requests. And even there, scheduling overhead is continuously decreasing. Given an optimal request scheduling policy implemented with minimal overhead, the primary way to further improve tail latency is by improving service times , as queuing delay depends on the service times of pending requests. In FCFS, a request’s queuing delay is the sum of the service times of the requests in front of it in the queue. Clearly, reducing the pending requests’ service times will reduce the request’s queuing delay. In PS, a request’s response time depends on the fraction of CPU capacity it receives, which depends on the number of pending requests. If some pending requests finish faster, the request’s CPU share will increase and it will complete sooner. 

> 3

While it would be ideal to improve service times across the board by speeding up a large fraction of requests, it is becoming harder and harder to find software optimizations with such an effect. Services often have no obvious hotspots to optimize, to the point that optimizing individual services is considered not cost-effective [48]—which has driven dat-acenter operators to perform automatic profile-guided op-timization [14, 28, 58, 81, 111]. Recent research on broad optimization of datacenter services has turned to proposing hardware-based optimizations [48, 55, 57, 91], which cannot be used today. We conclude that a new software optimiza-tion strategy is needed: tail service time optimization—i.e., optimizing the slowest requests instead of most or all requests.

## 4 PonyTail 

PonyTail is a performance analysis methodology (§ 4.1) and toolkit (§§ 4.2–4.3) for assisting performance engineers in an-alyzing service-level outlier behaviors responsible for tail service time and exploring tail optimization possibilities. 

> 4

Hotspot-oriented profiling is less applicable for these tasks, because the relevant behaviors may occur infrequently (e.g., 1-in-1000 for the 99.9th percentile service time) and so get missed or ranked as insignificant. 

4.1 Methodology and workflow 

PonyTail’s methodology seeks to attribute outlier service times to one or more of the following three cases, which together characterize all possible reasons for a tail request being slower than typical requests. 

1 Different static code ( STCO) In addition to typical code paths, a tail request might exercise other code paths whose execution slows request processing. Examples of this sce-nario include invocation of garbage collection code or distinct request types, some of which are slow. 

2 Same static code, different dynamic behavior ( DYCO)

Tail requests might execute exactly the same static code as average requests, but in a different dynamic way which slows down the request. An example of this case is a loop whose it-eration count depends on a request parameter x, which results in high service times for requests with high values of x.

3 Same static and dynamic architectural behavior, dif-ferent microarchitectural behavior ( UARCH ) Tail requests might be slowed down by microarchitectural factors. For example, certain memory load instructions might hit in the last-level cache (LLC) in average requests but miss in tail requests. 

Workflow PonyTail’s toolkit is designed to facilitate identi-fying which of the above cases affect the service and deter-mining their root cause using the manual workflow described in §§ 4.1.1–4.1.3. 

4.1.1 Characterization. To determine which of S TCO, D YCO,and UARCH are present, we first characterize service times in terms of the code and microarchitectural footprints of their respective requests. A code footprint specifies which code lo-cations (basic blocks) a request executes and how frequently. A microarchitectural footprint is a request-level TMA break-down of pipeline slot usage. Comparing request footprints allows classifying requests into homogeneous groups of requests with similar footprints, i.e., similar code flow or microarchitectural bottlenecks. This classification reveals which of S TCO, D YCO, and UARCH oc-cur at each service time percentile range: S TCO occurs if the range is associated with a code footprint-defined group; other-wise, D YCO occurs. UARCH occurs if the range is associated with a microarchitectural footprint-defined group. PonyTail assists characterization by providing a graphi-cal representation of code and microarchitectural footprints across service times, which enables visually identifying re-quest groups ( 1 in § 4.2.2). PonyTail describes code foot-prints in terms of dynamic basic blocks (DBBLs). A DBBL is a sequence of consecutive retired instructions τ = i1, . . . , ik

in a program execution E such that no branch in E jumps to any instruction in τ but i1 and no taken conditional branches occur in τ except for ik.3 These conditions are the dynamic analogues of the “no branches in” and “no branches out” defi-nition of a static basic block. PonyTail uses DBBLs because their boundaries can be efficiently extracted from Processor Trace data, whereas basic blocks require disassembling the service binaries (§ 4.3.2). PonyTail visualizes code footprints by plotting a heatmap of request-level (ordered by service time) DBBL hotness ,which is the fraction of time the request spends in this DBBL and any function it calls, with DBBLs identified by their location in the program’s address space (with black horizontal lines separating different modules). The user can plot only DBBLs with hotness of at least h% and that execute at least 

n times in each request. We refer to such a plot as a ⟨h, n⟩-DBBL heatmap, or a ⟨h, n, k⟩-DBBL heatmap if it also zooms in on the slowest k requests. Figure 4a shows an example with several groups (e.g., below and above the 45th percentile). PonyTail visualizes microarchitectural footprints by plot-ting request-level TMA breakdowns, ordered by service time. Figure 4b shows an example, again with noticeable groups. 

4.1.2 Exploration. This step aims to pinpoint the root causes of tail service time. If the relevant service time percentile range is uniquely associated with a request group identified above, we need to identify the reasons for software (S TCO) or hardware ( UARCH ) inefficiency. PonyTail assists this process by enabling standard performance analysis—such as sam-pling hardware events and identifying hot code locations—of only that group ( 5 in § 4.2.2). Limiting to a specific group sidesteps the problem a standard profiler would have. Its prob-lem is not that events in tail requests are not sampled—they are, albeit with lower probability. But because these samples are infrequent, they are ranked as insignificant. PonyTail’s zooming in only on relevant samples thus creates a request-level profile analysis. A different problem must be solved if tail service times are not defined by a group (D YCO). Here, we are not look-ing for bottlenecks but rather dynamic behaviors (typically loops) that cause slow service time. To this end, beyond Pony-Tail’s group-level performance analysis, PonyTail can also identify key DBBLs whose execution time or count is corre-lated with service time and their call stacks ( 2 in § 4.2.2), as well as generate plots depicting the correlation across all requests (see Figure 9 for an example). Exploring such DBBLs is typically prioritized by their call stack depth, to allow for root cause detection in higher code levels. PonyTail also helps to explore loop behavior by providing request-level loop iteration counts ( 4 in § 4.2.2).

4.1.3 Optimization. Insights from the analysis hopefully lead to optimizations that improve tail latency. Importantly, optimizations made in the D YCO case might impact the en-tire request group (but not necessarily). If the optimization addresses a loop-related hardware bottleneck, for example, then every request in the group benefits. However, if the opti-mization parallelizes loop iterations only for certain request arguments, only that part of the group benefits. 

4.2 Design 

PonyTail’s toolkit provides a familiar profiler interface, con-sisting of online recording of performance monitoring data (§ 4.2.1) followed by offline analysis of the data to help the user iden-tify unique aspects of slow requests (§ 4.2.2). Figure 2 depicts the toolkit’s design. It consists of a profiler-like tool, ponyrun ,which can run in “record” mode for data collection or in “re-port” mode to analyze collected data. PonyTail data collection relies on request boundaries being dynamically demarcated by the service via calls into ponylib , a dynamically linked library. 

4.2.1 Data collection ( record mode). Most of the data PonyTail collects is identical to that collected by existing pro-filers, such as the Linux perf tool [64] and Intel’s VTune [41]. Data collection has three standard modes: (1) tracing , which collects a control-flow trace using Processor Trace (PT); (2) sampling , which samples user-specified hardware events using processor event-based sampling (PEBS); and (3) statis-tics , which collects request-level performance counter data for user-specified counters, which can be useful during the exploration step (§ 4.1.2). PonyTail’s main unique aspect is its analysis, which breaks the program-level data into request-level data, even in sam-pling mode. To enable this, PonyTail requires the analyzed service to dynamically demarcate (with ponylib calls) the periods in which a request executes, i.e., when it starts/com-pletes and stops/resumes due to service-level scheduling, if any (Table 1). 4

The analysis tracks the recorded control-flow. Every time the “start” taken branch is encountered, it starts counting “iteration” taken branches until the “termination” branch (if specified) or an unspecified branch is encountered. Note that an inherent limitation of the described implementation is that loops whose body include function calls or other branches are not supported. 

5 Profile This analysis summarizes data recorded in sam-pling or statistics mode in the form of sampled event counts or per-request counts, respectively. It considers only data rele-vant to the specified request group (if any), i.e., samples taken during group requests or statistics collected for them. 

4.3 Implementation 

We implement ponyrun by extending Linux’s perf tool. For data collection, perf can essentially be used as-is. Recording with perf saves engineering effort, as it takes care of man-aging the PMU during context switches, decoding recorded events and augmenting them with timing information, map-ping addresses to symbols, etc. For efficient analysis, we use the dlfilter interface of perf , which allows processing the recorded data through a custom filter provided by a shared ob-ject file. PonyTail’s analyses are implemented as such a filter in C++ in 1450 lines of code (LOC). The rest of ponyrun is implemented in Python (2090 LOC). Ponylib is implemented in C++ (670 LOC), using the jevents library [59]. 

4.3.1 Data collection. 

Control-flow tracing PonyTail uses Intel PT (supported by 

perf record ) to capture the service control-flow trace. We configure Intel PT to record all taken branches (both condi-tional and indirect branches) and to additionally log timing information, but at a reduced rate, so the overall recording rate is tens of MBs/second. To attribute control-flow events to specific requests, ponylib injects entries demarcating request activity into the PT log by issuing PTWRITE instructions. 

Event-based sampling PonyTail uses PEBS via perf record 

to sample CPU context and call stack for every predetermined number of occurrences of the specified hardware events. 

4.3.2 Analysis. The analysis begins with DBBL extraction .This task is complicated by the fact that perf configures PT not to log non-taken branches, to curb the PT data volume. PonyTail uses the sources and targets of taken branches to “fill in the gaps”—i.e., to distinguish between a dynamic in-struction sequence forming one DBBL and a DBBL that falls through to another. § A shows the algorithm. Next, we describe key implementation aspects. 

DBBL-Info PonyTail determines the hotness of DBBL τ for request r based on periodic samples of stack traces Sr captured during r’s execution, as follows:

The analysis synthesizes the stack samples from the PT control-flow trace, as the trace contains the data required to obtain the call stack at any point in time. 

Profile To enable the analysis to associate samples with re-quests, we match the monotonic timestamp and thread ID 

perf provides for each sampled PEBS event with the infor-mation recorded by ponylib about request activity. 

4.4 Limitations 

PonyTail assists manual optimization instead of automatically addressing performance issues [14, 43, 56, 81, 111]. This is a conscious design choice, whose rationale is discussed in § 7. Due to the complex environment of datacenter services, it is difficult to profile their real-world behavior outside of the production deployment [14, 48, 56]. PonyTail’s control-flow tracing is not suitable for “always on” recording of production systems, due to its run-time overhead and data volume (§ 6.1). However, we believe the overhead is acceptable for inter-mittent or manual data collection over short time periods. Disruption due to data collection can be further reduced by recording on many machines for very short time periods. PonyTail’s tracing analysis takes about 4.5–6 minutes per single-core second of service execution (§ 6.2). Analysis time is mitigated by the fact that a few seconds of execution suffice for capturing tail events at microsecond-to-millisecond scale service times. It is also possible to implement a PonyTail “server” that analyzes data once and answers queries about it. Our PonyTail prototype targets C/C++ binaries and does not support services with self-modifying or just-in-time (JIT) compiled code (e.g., Java services). Supporting JITed code is a matter of engineering effort, as perf can support JIT symbols with cooperation from the JIT compiler [83].

Platform We use a server with an Intel Xeon Gold 6442Y (Sapphire Rapids) processor with 24 2.6 GHz cores, 256 GB DDR5-4800 memory, and a datacenter-class NVMe Samsung PM9A3 [88] solid-state drive. Execution harness We use the common harness TailBench provides for statistically sound load testing of each service. The client and server measure response and service time, respectively. We first attempt to replace the binary search branches with conditional move ( CMOV ) instructions, which set the left/right boundaries of the search based on the current key comparison. We therefore perform a different optimization. We use the fact that the key array contains 15 8-byte values to execute all comparisons with a constant number of AVX-512 [40, § 15] vector instructions. We load the array into two AVX-512 reg-isters, obtain a 15-bit mask indicating the comparison result of each index, and return the index of the first set bit (using the TZCNT instruction). 5.5.1 Code footprint exploration. We examine the source code associated with the key DBBL to understand its execu-tion context. The DBBL executes once per document found for the searched term, as part of a loop that iterates over a list of objects, each of which represents a document indexed by the searched term. For each such document, the loop invokes a get_weight() method, part of which looks up the docu-ment in xapian ’s database backend. The backend maintains fixed-size chunks , each of which describes a number of docu-ments, consecutive by their IDs. To retrieve information for a document, the backend first locates the relevant chunk and then traverses it to find the desired document’s data. Locating the relevant chunk consists of checking if the last inspected chunk is a match, and moving to the correct chunk otherwise. PonyTail’s DBBL-Info report shows that, on average, mov-ing to a different chunk is done for ≈ 166 documents out of the ≈ 230 scanned and takes 11% of service time, while locating the correct position inside the chunk takes 74% of service time. We hypothesize that it will be profitable to reduce the backend’s chunk size, as (1) searching inside a chunk is more expensive than locating a chunk, and (2) locating chunks is already done frequently. To test this hypothesis, we evaluate 

xapian on the same database built with 1 KB chunks instead of the default 2 KB. This change reduces mean and tail service times by ≈ 30%, improving mean and tail response times at 87% load by ≈ 3×.

Figure 8b shows that the majority of pipeline slot stalls in xapian re-quest handling are caused by CPU frontend issues: branch mispredictions (26%) and fetch bandwidth underutilization (31%), which refers to the frontend delivering fewer μ-ops to the backend than its capacity. Intel CPUs provide numerous performance counters for tracking events responsible for fetch bandwidth underutilization. Optimization Using the same process as in silo ’s microar-chitectural footprint exploration (§ 5.3.2), we pinpoint the sources of branch misprediction in sphinx . The culprit is a loop evaluating a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) [23] which computes the likelihood that an observation vector belongs to one of several clusters represented as Gaussian distributions. The likelihood is computed incrementally over the vector dimensions, with the loop terminated early if the likelihood crosses some threshold. The resulting loop termi-nation condition requires multiple branches to evaluate. Each loop iteration computes over 4 dimensions, in an apparent attempt to encourage compiler vectorization. PonyTail’s Loop-Follow report shows that this loop iterates over at most 13 dimensions at run-time. We thus change the unrolling factor to 13. This change significantly reduces the number of branches (and mispredictions) during the loop and improves service time by ≈ 10%, despite performing more wasted work (as the early termination condition is now checked less frequently).

5.8 Reflections 

Given that PonyTail records the same data as Linux’s perf ,it is natural to ask whether our optimization effort could be carried out using perf . Here, we pinpoint PonyTail’s unique aspects that enable the analyses. Improving service time in 

silo and moses required identifying code and microarchitec-tural behaviors that occur only in outlier requests. These do not show up (or are ranked low) on a standard perf report. In contrast, in xapian and sphinx we improve service time across the board by addressing a global hotspot. However, successful optimization requires more than identifying the bottleneck. In xapian , PonyTail’s DBBL-Info report is nec-essary for the control-flow optimization (§ 5.5.1). It shows that locating chunks is already done for 72% of the docu-ments yet only takes 11% of request time, which leads to the conclusion that trading off more chunk-locating for fewer chunk-searches will improve service time. For the microar-chitectural optimization (§ 5.5.2), PonyTail’s Loop-Follow is crucial. It enables determining that each execution of the hot loop with poor DSB utilization runs for at most 4 iterations, which leads to the unrolling optimization. Loop-Follow is similarly crucial for the sphinx optimization (§ 5.6). We also consider Intel’s VTune Profiler [41]. It has recently introduced a preview Anomaly Detection analysis feature, which targets analyzing performance outliers—it allows the user to group requests by latency and observe their perfor-mance data for these groups. However, VTune’s analysis is orders of magnitude slower than PonyTail’s (§ 6.2), taking minutes per millisecond. In addition, data collection is limited to 5000 requests of at most 1 second each. This limitation restricts the scope of analysis, especially for services with high request volume and variety, such as silo and shore .PonyTail has no similar limitation. VTune only allows com-paring performance metrics of two specific requests at a time, which hinders broad characterization such as PonyTail’s code and microarchitectural footprint visualization. Finally, VTune cannot perform analysis at request group level, which is a PonyTail functionality heavily used in our analyses. ## 6 Evaluation 

We evaluate PonyTail’s online overhead, during program ex-ecution (§ 6.1), and offline (analysis) efficiency (§ 6.2). We use the setup described in § 5.1. We report averages of 3 runs; if variance is not negligible, we include error bars showing the standard deviation. 

6.1 Run-time overhead 

Impact on service time Figure 11 shows the relative increase in mean service time of xapian , sphinx , and silo for each PonyTail data collection mode. For sampling mode, we sam-ple every 100 K cycles. PonyTail’s overhead varies across both services and modes, with tracing and sampling imposing a geometric average slowdown of 16.5% and 13.7%, respec-tively. Tracing overhead mainly consists of PT recording overhead (on all requests) and PT buffer-full interrupts (on interrupted requests). Similarly, sampling overhead consists of PEBS overhead and interrupts, whose frequency depends on the sampling parameters and program execution. For both modes, PonyTail’s relative overhead decreases with service time, but not in direct proportion. PonyTail’s relative overhead is only 2 .4× higher in silo than in sphinx ,despite silo requests being orders of magnitude faster than 

sphinx requests. Because PonyTail uses perf ’s data collec-tion mechanism, its overheads are comparable to those of standard profiling with perf ’s analogous modes. 

Impact on service time distribution We verify that service time distributions remain similar in shape (only shifted due to the overhead) when PonyTail data collection is running. This ensures that PonyTail does not misclassify non-tail requests as tail requests or vice versa. 

6.2 Analysis efficiency 

Time Figure 12 shows PonyTail’s per-request analysis time of both trace and sample data for xapian , sphinx , and silo .Tracing analysis time scales linearly with service time, reflect-ing a rate of 4.5–6 minutes per second of service execution. Total analysis time depends on the amount of data collected per request—so trace analysis is 60 × slower on average than sampling analysis—and on the number of requests recorded.

## 7 Related work 

We first place PonyTail’s conceptual contribution in context and then discuss its technical aspects. 

Profilers General-purpose profilers report performance prob-lems—such as execution hotspots [29, 41, 64], high data ac-cess latency [27, 113] and bandwidth [35], or useless work [90, 94]—but do not identify their root causes. Specialized pro-filers locate root causes of specific issues, e.g., slow exe-cution [101] or lock contention [5, 17, 112]. PonyTail sits midway between these approaches: it does not identify root causes, but has features to assist root-cause analysis. 

Automated profile-guided service optimization Several sys-tems profile datacenter services in production to perform au-tomated profile-guided optimization, whether online [111] or offline [14, 28, 43, 56, 57, 65, 78, 81]. They do not target tail service time or performance outliers. More importantly, Pony-Tail is complementary to this line of work. The design of an automated optimization system starts with humans character-izing a class of performance issues and developing techniques to automatically fix them. PonyTail targets this first step for the novel problem of specifically improving tail service time. 

Tail service time optimization Several works proposed tech-niques for improving tail service time in key-value stores [7, 22, 85]. PonyTail’s goal is to assist such manual, service-specific efforts. PonyTail is complementary to run-time sys-tems techniques for speeding up slow requests, e.g., using parallelism [32, 44] or voltage/frequency boosting [33, 37]. 

Execution time variance Prior work on analyzing execution time variance does not target PonyTail’s more demanding problem of capturing any program- or CPU-level cause of tail service time. For example, Hindsight [110] traces high-level program-defined events to troubleshoot tail latency in distributed systems, and is therefore not appropriate for under-standing CPU or control-flow factors. FVSampler [93] and Akiyama et al. [4] synchronize profiling samples with func-tion or request boundaries to profile their execution time vari-ance, but they rely exclusively on performance counters and so cannot provide fine-grained control-flow data. NSight [30] reconstructs network-message lifetimes from CPU and NIC hardware traces. It targets network/OS latency, not tail service time, and cannot reveal intra-function factors. 

Cross-execution comparisons Comparison of multiple recorded executions is done by many systems for root-cause diagno-sis or reproduction of software bugs [11, 50, 51, 115]. Per-spect [87] pinpoints the root cause of performance problems by comparing execution with and without the issue, but it reasons exclusively about control-flow and so cannot reveal hardware-related issues. Coz [16] pinpoints promising code locations for optimization by running performance experi-ments during program execution. However, while it supports average-latency as an optimization goal, it has no concept of tail optimization. 

## 8 Conclusion 

This paper shows the opportunity to improve datacenter ser-vice tail latency by improving tail service time. To unlock this potential, we present PonyTail, a performance analysis methodology and toolkit for analyzing service-level outlier behaviors associated with tail service time. PonyTail helps performance engineers to identify control-flow and microar-chitectural patterns responsible for service time outliers, as well as their root causes. We demonstrate PonyTail’s merit by optimizing several latency-critical services, improving their 99th percentile service time and latency by 10.7%–46% and 21%–5 ×, respectively.
