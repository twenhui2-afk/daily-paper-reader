# Defense Feedback and Global-Game Selection 

## Georgy Lukyanov ∗

Toulouse School of Economics, University of Toulouse Capitole 1 Esplanade de l’Université, 31080 Toulouse Cedex 06, France 

## Yaroslav Yosifov

Abstract 

Global games select a unique cutoff when a player’s incentive moves monotonically with private information. We show that this logic can fail when a privately informed participant can take a costly, simultaneous, and unobserved action that defends the status quo. In a currency-attack model, the defender acts only at intermediate signals. Along the traders’ cutoff equation, this interval response creates an endogenous defense-loss term that vanishes at the endpoints of an activation component but can dominate the decreasing benchmark payoff margin in between. We establish a primitive region in which defense is inactive and uniqueness survives, and a finite-CDF overshoot condition under which two active-defense cutoff roots coexist with the inactive benchmark root. Because marginal indifference need not imply global incentives, we also give sufficient conditions for a genuine monotone cutoff best response. Finally, outward-rounded interval arithmetic proves that a proper-prior parameter vector has exactly three symmetric monotone-cutoff equilibria and that this topology persists locally. The result isolates a payoff-based boundary of global-game selection: hidden intervention can restore multiplicity without public signalling or a perturbation of the information hierarchy. 

Keywords: global games; regime change; active defense; private information; equilibrium multiplicity; computer-assisted proof. 

JEL classifications: C72; D82; F31. 

# 1 Introduction 

Private signals select a unique cutoff in the benchmark currency-attack global game because the marginal trader’s payoff moves monotonically with her signal (Morris and Shin, 1998). We identify a payoff-based boundary of this selection argument. One privately informed participant can take a costly binary action that subtracts capacity from aggregate attack pressure. The action is simultaneous and unobserved: it changes the small traders’ success probability but conveys no public information.

Strict inequalities establish local persistence while holding the prior fixed. Corsetti et al. (2004) provide the closest large-participant benchmark. In their simul-taneous currency-attack game, a privately informed large trader’s action is also hidden from the small traders, but the large trader joins the attack and uses a lower-signal cutoff; equilibrium is unique and dominance solvable under an improper uniform prior interpreted as a diffuse-prior limit. Here the large participant instead values peg survival. His costly action is effective only in pivotal states, generating an interval response and the nonmonotone defense-loss term. Corsetti et al.’s sequential extension concerns public action and signalling; their opposite-exposure discussion does not add costly defense capacity or derive multiplicity. The result is distinct from multiplicity generated by observed policy actions and their informational content (Angeletos et al., 2006; Lukyanov, 2025). Jann and Schottmüller (2021) study finitely many attackers and a costly, continuous, secret defense investment; their equilibrium is unique with sufficiently many potential attackers. Mitkov (2025) uses private, payoff-irrelevant sunspots to pin down a unique coordination probability without intrinsic fundamental uncertainty. Our mechanism instead combines payoff-relevant global-game information, simultaneous hidden defense, and locally persistent cutoff multiplicity. Nor is the claim that uniqueness is robust to arbitrary belief perturbations. Weinstein and Yildiz (2007) show that refined predictions are generally sensitive to higher-order-belief perturbations. We keep a proper Gaussian information structure fixed and locate a 2finite-dimensional, payoff-based boundary of selection. The result is complete only within symmetric monotone small-trader cutoffs; asymmetric and non-cutoff equilibria remain outside its scope.

# 6 Interpretation and scope 

The result depends on three distinct forces. Strategic complementarity among small traders creates the usual cutoff logic. The defender’s finite capacity makes intervention pivotal only on a state interval. His private signal then turns that state interval into an intermediate-signal defense band. Because his choice is contemporaneous and hidden, the band changes payoffs but not beliefs. It is this payoff effect—not policy signalling and not an exogenous perturbation of higher-order beliefs—that bends the residual. The cost comparison is equally important. If defense is too expensive, Proposition 1 applies and the benchmark survives. If defense is active at pessimistic cutoffs but inactive at b0, the intervention loss can generate a pair of additional equilibria. This gives a simple economic interpretation of the three roots: limited and selectively deployed defense need not stabilize expectations; it can create two self-consistent views about how much protection the market will face. The currency-attack labels are not essential to this logic. The analytical object is a decreasing benchmark residual minus an intervention loss supported on an endoge-nous activation component. Whenever hidden action is valuable only for intermediate fundamentals, the loss can vanish at the component’s boundaries and peak inside it. Proposition 2 states the resulting overshoot test explicitly; the Gaussian structure makes the test finite and computable. Applications outside currency crises would require their own verification of the global-incentive and activation conditions. The word “local” is substantive. The exact-count neighborhood in Proposition 3 is topological and unquantified. To report a numerical scale without conflating existence 9and exactness, a separate certificate holds ( τ, m 0, α, β, λ ) fixed and establishes at least three qualified equilibria throughout the independent rectangle (cL, c S ) ∈ [0 .70 , 0.72] × [0 .909 , 0.929] .

This rectangle does not carry an exact-count claim. The paper does not claim a general characterization of every Bayesian equilibrium. Proposition 3 is complete only within symmetric monotone small-trader cutoffs. Asymmet-ric and non-cutoff equilibria remain outside its scope. The parameter vector is constructed rather than calibrated, and the result is not evidence about the empirical frequency of currency crises. Its purpose is sharper: to identify a transparent intervention technology under which the familiar global-game selection argument changes, to state the condition responsible for that change, and to verify one locally persistent exact count without relying on a plot or a floating-point grid.
