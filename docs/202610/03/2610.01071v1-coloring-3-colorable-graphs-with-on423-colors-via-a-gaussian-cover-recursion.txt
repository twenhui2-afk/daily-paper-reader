# Coloring 3-colorable graphs with O(n4/23 ) colors via a Gaussian-cover recursion 

## Emile Anand ∗

Abstract 

We give a randomized polynomial-time algorithm that colors any promised 3-colorable graph on n vertices with O(n4/23 ) = O(n0.17391 ... ) colors, improving on the recent bounds of 

O(n0.19539 ) by Bansal, Huang, and Lee [BHL26] and Narang and Tang [NT26] who obtained 

O(n(13 −√97) /18+ ϵ) = O(n0.17506 ··· +ϵ) colors for every fixed ϵ > 0. To prove our result, we start from a fixed-level semidefinite relaxation, where we use a finite-depth recursion on Gaussian covers. Fixing a root vertex, we group vertices by correlation with the root vector. Here, each step extends a cover of directions by one edge and transfers it to a successor group. Our key analytic ingredient is a variance bound for Gaussian maxima: for a maximum of 

m ≥ 2 centered linear forms with coefficient norms at most r, mean μ, and variance v, we prove 

v ≤ r2 −μ2/(2 log m) using Chen’s Gaussian convexity theorem [Che24]. Together with a variance-scale lower-tail estimate, this controls the threshold loss at each extension, which shows that root-conditioned vector colorings can either extract a large independent set from a group or bound its size, forcing a contradiction after constantly many steps. The resulting sparse-case guarantee combines with the dense progress bound of Kawarabayashi, Thorup, and Yoneda [KTY24], and the recursion’s numerical inequalities are verified via rational interval arithmetic.

# 1 Introduction 

Given an n-vertex graph promised to be 3-colorable, how many colors suffice in polynomial time? The promise guarantees a partition into three independent sets without providing it. Deciding 3-colorability is NP-complete, and the promise does not remove the difficulty: coloring a 3-colorable graph with five colors is already NP-hard [KLS00, GK04, BKO19], and hardness for every constant number of colors is conjectured. The problem is a canonical promise problem: a global property of the input is guaranteed but not given, so algorithms must exploit structure they cannot directly inspect. It has been a test case for semidefinite rounding and convex hierarchies, and the gap it leaves openn, which is constant-factor hardness against polynomial-color algorithms, is among the largest in approximation theory. Approximation algorithms therefore seek a proper coloring with a controlled number of additional colors. Historically, progress on this problem has relied on combining combinatorial structure with semidefinite programming. The two approaches apply in complementary regimes: combinatorial methods exploit a large minimum degree, whereas semidefinite rounding benefits from an upper bound on the maximum degree. In the latter regime, the basic task is to extract an independent set large enough to make progress toward a coloring. The Gaussian rounding algorithm of Karger, Motwani, and Sudan [KMS98] provides the starting point: they show how to convert a vector coloring into a random vertex set by thresholding Gaussian inner products, removing conflicts via a maximal matching. Notably, the geometry left behind when this procedure has small expected output provides additional information, since, at each selected vertex, the matching gives a family of edge directions whose Gaussian maximum remains large. In this work, we develop a finite-depth recursion for exploiting that geometry. We fix a root and group vertices into correlation shells according to their inner product with its vector, as in the neighborhood analyses of [KMS98, BHL26]. A family in one such shell is described by its correlation and a Gaussian threshold that it reaches with inverse-polynomial probability. The recursion extends its endpoints by one graph edge, projects away the root direction, and transfers the cover property to a successor shell. Through this process, large shells yield independent sets through rounding, and small shells impose cardinality bounds that constrain the loss in the next extension. These alternatives produce a scalar recursion whose continuing states lie in a compact domain (see Figure 1). The cover-composition arguments of Bansal, Huang, and Lee [BHL26, Lemma 4.14] and Chlamtáč [Chl09, Theorem 3.4.4] use Borell’s Gaussian isoperimetric enlargement inequality [Bor75]. In those bounds, the enlargement cost is multiplied by the radius of the inner family. We instead derive a composition estimate from the variance of the inner Gaussian maximum. For a maximum of m ≥ 2 centered Gaussian linear forms with coefficient norms at most r, mean μ, and variance v, we prove 

v ≤ r2 − μ2

2 log m .

Together with Chen’s lower-deviation inequality [Che24], this replaces the full-radius factor by a variance-dependent factor. The resulting composition inequality gives the neighborhood lift in Lemma 16. Section 1.1 compares this proof with the concurrent approach of Narang and Tang [NT26]. The remaining finite inequalities are verified by exact rational interval arithmetic over an adaptive subdivision of the compact domain. Our main theorem is an improved coloring bound of O(n4/23 ) = O(n0.17391 ... ), as stated in Theorem 1, which improves on the bound of Bansal, Huang, and Lee [BHL26] and concurrent work of Narang and Tang [NT26], where the exponent we present has a slightly smaller value by setting α = 1 /(5 + 3 c∗), where 

c∗ = 1 /4 + 10 −6, to absorb the subpolynomial losses in the dense–sparse reduction. For our analysis, only a fixed number of neighborhood extensions is needed, since the algorithm rounds the full root-correlation shells and does not have to enumerate the paths or construct the pruned graph used in the proof. 

Theorem 1. Every promised 3-colorable graph on n vertices has a proper coloring with O(n4/23 ) colors that can be found in randomized polynomial time, with success probability at least 1 − n−10 .

## 1.1 Related work 

Combinatorial and semidefinite algorithms. Progress on this problem has alternated between com-binatorial and semidefinite tools. Wigderson [Wig83] gave an O(n1/2)-coloring algorithm by using the 

2Coloring 3-colorable graphs via a Gaussian-cover recursion bipartite neighborhoods of high-degree vertices and greedily coloring the remaining graph. Blum [Blu94] introduced the general notion of progress toward a coloring and improved the guarantee to ˜O(n3/8) by looking one step further out, exploiting distance-2 neighborhoods where earlier arguments had used only immediate ones. Karger, Motwani, and Sudan [KMS98] brought in semidefinite programming, following Goemans and Williamson [GW95], to reach ˜O(n1/4) colors; Blum and Karger [BK97] then combined the two approaches for ˜O(n3/14 ). Arora, Chlamtáč, and Charikar [ACC06] brought the bound to O(n0.2111 )through a geometric analysis of semidefinite rounding, and Chlamtáč [Chl07] pushed it to O(n0.2072 ) with a hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations [Las01]. Kawarabayashi and Thorup [KT17] returned to combinatorial methods, which, combined with the semidefinite analysis, give ˜O(n0.19996 ) colors. For general graphs without a colorability promise, Halldórsson [Hal93] achieved an O(n(log log n)2/ log 3 n) approximation ratio. Kawarabayashi, Thorup, and Yoneda [KTY24] subsequently developed a combinatorial progress algorithm for graphs of minimum degree ∆ > √n that colors such graphs with no(1) √n/ ∆ colors; this idea, combined with semidefinite rounding, gives their ˜O(n0.19747 ) bound. We make use of their dense-side guarantee in our work. Related structural parameters for dense graphs include rank-expansion, a well-linkedness measure defined through the cutrank of balanced separations [Ana25]. 

Hardness. Khanna, Linial, and Safra [KLS00] showed that coloring a 3-colorable graph with four colors is NP-hard; Guruswami and Khanna [GK04] gave a new proof that does not rely on the PCP theorem, and Bulín, Krokhin, and Oprśal [BKO19] extended the hardness to five colors. Dinur, Mossel, and Regev [DMR09] studied conditional hardness for approximate coloring under variants of the Unique Games Conjecture. Indeed, without the 3-colorability promise, the chromatic number is hard to approximate within n1−ε for every 

ε > 0 [FK98, Zuc07]. 

Neighborhood covers and root-conditioned rounding. Bansal, Huang, and Lee [BHL26] obtained an O(n0.19539 )-coloring algorithm by extending the neighborhood analysis to a third step and constructing a vector 5 /2-coloring for the resulting third-neighborhood sets. We adopt their root-conditioned vector-coloring constructions and their dense-sparse formulation. The cover machinery itself, which comprises of Gaussian covers, packings, and the composition and pruning steps, was assembled across these earlier SDP analyses, and the statements we use appear in Chlamtáč’s dissertation [Chl09], whose Theorems 3.4.4 and 3.6.9 establish cover composition and the packing spread theorem. Whereas the neighborhood analysis in [BHL26] uses three levels, our proof combines the norm-sensitive composition of Lemma 14 with root-conditioned shell rounding and extends the recursion to a fixed depth of 404. 

Concurrent work. Concurrently, Narang and Tang [NT26] obtain O(n(13 −√97) /18+ ε) colors for every fixed ε > 0, also via a fixed-depth Gaussian-cover recursion. Their Lemma 2.5 yields the same leading composition loss as Lemma 14 here for a common inner norm bound, using Ehrhard’s inequality. An explicit potential then establishes their guarantee for modified KMS rounding. Our proof derives the composition estimate from Chen’s Gaussian convexity theorem and a variance bound, combines the recursion with root-conditioned shell rounding from a three-round SoS relaxation, and verifies its drift by exact rational interval arithmetic, to obtain the exponent 4 /23. 

Gaussian convexity and finite verification. Gaussian isoperimetry controls the measure of Euclidean enlargements of sets [Bor75]; Ehrhard’s inequality describes Gaussian measure under convex combinations of convex sets [Ehr83]. The cover-composition proof in Bansal, Huang, and Lee [BHL26, Lemma 4.14] uses the isoperimetric enlargement inequality. Here the inner maximum is a convex Gaussian functional, so its variance provides additional information about the lower tail, so the resulting variance bound is an instance of Gaussian superconcentration [Cha14]. Appendix B gives a weighted composition statement that retains individual witness masses and a precise entropy truncation bound, and the recursion itself uses the cardinality-based estimate of Lemma 14. Chen [Che24] proves convexity of the normalized logarithmic moment-generating function of a convex Gaussian functional, together with a lower-tail estimate at the scale of its variance, and we apply both of these tools to a maximum of linear forms. A finite exact computation verifies the initialization and the uniform positive drift of the resulting recursion, stated as Proposition 17.

## 2.1 Vector colorings and Gaussian rounding 

Definition 2 (Vector coloring [KMS98]) . For a real number κ ≥ 2, a vector κ-coloring of G = ( V, E ) assigns unit vectors ( xj )j∈V such that ⟨xj , x k⟩ ≤ − 1/(κ − 1) for every jk ∈ E. The coloring is strict if equality holds on every edge. A proper integer κ-coloring gives such vectors by placing its colors at the vertices of a regular simplex. Karger, Motwani, and Sudan [KMS98] round a vector coloring by selecting vertices above a Gaussian threshold and keeping the isolated vertices.

## 2.2 Gaussian covers and packings 

On an edge ij of a strict vector 3-coloring, let vij = (2 vj + vi)/√3. This is a unit vector perpendicular to 

vi. If both endpoints pass threshold t, then ⟨g, v ij ⟩ ≥ √3t. If a matching rounding fails, this forces large Gaussian maxima among these tangent directions.

## 2.4 Dense–sparse progress and parameters 

Definition 10 (Progress toward a coloring [Blu94]) . # 4 Conclusion 

We provide a randomized polynomial time algorithm to color a graph that is promised to be 3-colorable with O(n4/23 ) colors. The proof reduces the sparse coloring problem to a scalar recursion on root correlation and cover threshold. The variance bound for Gaussian maxima controls the loss at each step, while shell rounding limits the number of available endpoints. The resulting positive drift is verified on a compact domain by exact interval arithmetic. The number of steps is an absolute constant, and the algorithm rounds full correlation shells.
