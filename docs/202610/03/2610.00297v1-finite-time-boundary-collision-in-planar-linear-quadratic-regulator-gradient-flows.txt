# Finite-time boundary collision in planar linear quadratic regulator gradient flows 

Kang Liu ∗

> School of Future Technology, Xi’an Jiaotong University, China

Abstract 

Policy gradient methods for the linear quadratic regulator optimize feedback gains using a cost over an infinite horizon. When this cost is evaluated at one fixed initial state, it need not diverge near every part of the stability boundary. We study whether the Euclidean gradient flow can reach this boundary in finite optimization time. For controllable planar systems with one input and positive definite quadratic weights, an exact representation of the cost yields a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of such a collision. The condition reduces to a scalar root calculation and includes the critical case, where a double direction root attracts an open set of stabilizing gains. For a triangular family, the criterion gives an explicit algebraic parameter region, and every trajectory either converges to the Riccati gain or collides with the stability boundary. A sharp threshold for compact cost sublevels provides a convergence certificate. Along every colliding trajectory, the stability margin and the smallest eigenvalue of the accumulated state Gramian vanish linearly, although the Gramian remains positive definite before collision. Numerical experiments examine the dependence on initialization, slow passage near a critical direction, and the effect of adding excitation in a second state direction. 

Keywords: Linear quadratic regulator, Gradient flow, Stability boundary, Finite-time collision, Global dynamics

1. Introduction 

Policy gradient methods for the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) optimize a feedback gain over the open set of stabilizing controllers. Their convergence requires the optimization trajectory to remain in this set. When the objective averages performance over initial states with a positive definite covariance, the cost diverges as the gain approaches the stability boundary, so cost descent prevents a trajectory from reaching it. This property can fail when performance is evaluated from one fixed initial state. A mode approaching instability may receive progressively less excitation, allowing the cost to decrease as the stability margin vanishes. The resulting issue is whether the gradient flow can reach the stability boundary in finite optimization time. Classical LQR theory characterizes an optimal stabilizing gain through an algebraic Riccati equation [1, 2, 3]. Kleinman [4] developed an iterative method for solving this equation. Direct policy optimization instead updates the gain using derivatives of the cost. Global convergence analyses in discrete time [5, 6] and continuous time [7, 8, 9] combine gradient bounds with control of cost sublevels. This framework also supports accelerated methods [10] and robustness analyses of perturbed gradient flows [11]. Hu et al. [12] survey the broader development of policy optimization for control. The choice of optimization metric also affects the gain dynamics, as illustrated by the Riemannian approach to constrained feedback optimization of Talebi and Mesbahi [13]. Here we consider the Euclidean gradient with respect to the original feedback gain. The limitations of evaluating a single initial state are already recognized. Bu et al. [7, Sections 3.2–3.3] show that the corresponding cost can remain finite at a gain that does not stabilize the whole system, and recover coercivity by evaluating a spanning set of initial states. Razin et al. [14] examine restricted initial state coverage in relation to implicit bias and extrapolation in finite horizon, discrete-time LQR. For the infinite horizon gradient flow studied here, finiteness of the boundary cost alone does not determine whether a descent trajectory approaches that boundary or whether the approach takes finite time. These questions depend on the direction of the gradient near the boundary. Accumulated excitation also enters recent results on the geometry of the LQR objective. Watanabe and Zheng [15] refine conditions for strong duality, hidden convexity, and gradient dominance. Their unified analysis [16] allows a semidefinite initial covariance under an additional condition on the 2accumulated state Gramian and establishes uniform gradient dominance on compact subsets of the stabilizing domain. Our analysis concerns the behavior as a trajectory approaches the boundary of that domain. In particular, the accumulated Gramian can remain positive definite before collision while its smallest eigenvalue tends to zero. Positivity along the trajectory therefore does not by itself prevent a finite-time loss of stability. We establish a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of finite-time boundary collision in every controllable planar system with one input, positive definite quadratic weights, and a fixed nonzero initial state. An exact representation of the cost identifies the only possible collision gain and reduces the local dynamics to a scalar equation for the direction of approach. The resulting criterion is computed from the system data. Whenever it holds, including the critical equality case, a nonempty open set of stabilizing initial gains leads to collision. Along every colliding trajectory, the stability margin and the smallest eigenvalue of the accumulated Gramian vanish linearly in the remaining optimization time. For a triangular family, we obtain an explicit polynomial description of the collision region and classify every stabilizing initialization. Each trajectory either converges to the stabilizing Riccati gain or reaches the stability boundary in finite optimization time. A sharp threshold for compact cost sublevels provides a convergence certificate for an individual gain. The collision region is a bounded interval in the squared coupling strength when the second parameter is fixed. Numerical experiments illustrate the dependence on initialization, slow passage near critical parameters, and the effect of adding excitation in a second initial state direction. Section 2 states the general collision criterion. Section 3 proves it and derives the collision asymptotics. Section 4 presents the explicit parameter region and global classification for the triangular family. Section 5 reports the numerical experiments. The appendices provide algebraic calculations and reproduction details.

6. Conclusions 

For LQR costs evaluated at a fixed initial state, the Euclidean gradient flow can reach the stability boundary in finite optimization time while the cost decreases and the accumulated state Gramian remains positive definite. This work gives a necessary and sufficient condition for such collisions in controllable planar systems with one input, including the critical case. For a triangular family, an explicit parameter region identifies when collisions occur, and every trajectory either converges to the Riccati gain or collides with the boundary. The analysis links collision to vanishing excitation of a marginal mode and gives a cost threshold that certifies convergence. Extending these results to higher dimensions and discrete policy updates remains an open direction.
