# Positivity-preserving scalar auxiliary variable schemes for gradient flows via a quadratic reformulation 

Qiong-Ao Huang · Zhi-Hao Liu · Ying-Wei Wang · Li-Na Yan 

Received: date / Accepted: date 

Abstract The scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) method replaces the nonlinear part of the free energy by a positive scalar r(t) = √EN [φ] + C > 0, thereby yielding lin-ear, unconditionally energy-stable schemes for gradient flows. At the discrete level, however, the standard backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson discretizations provide no guarantee that the computed rn+1 remains positive, an inconsistency with the continuous definition that contradicts the square-root ansatz and may compromise long-time robustness. Although the SAV method has been widely applied, this sub-tle but consequential issue has received little attention. We first characterize this failure quantitatively by deriving a sharp criterion and a sufficient condition on the time step size, and construct an explicit counterexample showing that sign loss occurs for parameters of practical relevance. Rather than modifying the definition of r as in existing positivity-preserving variants, we retain the square-root form and reformulate the discrete evolution from rt to ( r2)t, which converts the scalar equation into a convex quadratic with a strictly negative constant term, always yielding a unique positive root. For the Crank–Nicolson scheme, the product-form discretization rn+1 rn preserves this quadratic structure, while conventional alter-natives do not. The resulting schemes incur the same computational cost as the original SAV method and are proved unconditionally energy-stable. Numerical ex-periments for the Cahn–Hilliard equation confirm the predicted positivity, energy stability, and convergence rates.

A comprehensive account of the SAV framework appears in [23]; broader surveys of phase field modelling and numerical approximations can be found in [4]. After time discretization of the original SAV system, two issues arise: 1. the computed rn+1 is not constrained to equal √EN [φn+1 ] + C, and the dis-crepancy can become significant when the time step is “large”; 2. rn+1 > 0 cannot be guaranteed theoretically, contradicting the continuous definition r(t) = √EN [φ] + C > 0. The first issue has been effectively addressed by relaxed and weighted SAV vari-ants [16, 19, 15, 11]. The second issue, namely guaranteeing positivity while retain-ing the square-root definition r = √EN [φ] + C, has received surprisingly little attention despite the widespread use of the original SAV method. The exponential SAV [18] guarantees rn+1 > 0 through a logarithmic change of variable, yet the lower bound of ln rn+1 is not directly controlled, which complicates convergence analysis. The generalized SAV [14, 13] also preserves positivity, but its auxiliary variable evolution does not explicitly involve the associated nonlinear energy term. To date, guaranteeing positivity within the original square-root definition has re-mained an open problem. Preserving the original definition is of interest beyond aesthetic consistency. The original SAV scheme remains the most widely implemented member of the SAV family, serving as the foundation upon which relaxed, weighted and other variants are built. The relation r = √EN [φ] + C is the simplest algebraic link between the auxiliary variable and the nonlinear energy; replacing r by a different function of EN [φ] would alter the structure of the scalar equation at the discrete level, generally leading to a more complex algebraic problem than the compact quadratic form derived in Section 3. Moreover, relaxed SAV variants take the original SAV solution as the starting point for a post-processing correction; if the base scheme already produces rn+1 ≤ 0, the subsequent correction step operates on a value that contradicts the very definition of the auxiliary variable. Fixing the sign-indeterminacy at the source, within the original formulation, therefore strengthens the entire SAV framework at no additional computational cost. In this work, we resolve this question in two steps. First, we diagnose the prob-lem: a sharp criterion for rn+1 > 0 is derived, a sufficient condition on time step size is established via an operator-norm bound, and an explicit counterexample demonstrates that sign failure is not a pathological edge case but a structural phenomenon that can occur for practically relevant parameters and is particularly acute for solutions with significant high-frequency content. Second, we resolve it at the structural level: rather than altering the definition of r, we retain the classical square-root form and reformulate the discrete evolution from rt to ( r2)t. This sim-ple algebraic change converts the scalar auxiliary equation from a linear equation with a sign-indefinite constant term into a convex quadratic whose constant term is strictly negative, thereby always yielding a unique positive root rn+1 at every time step. We construct positivity-preserving SAV schemes for both the backward Euler and Crank–Nicolson discretizations, prove their unconditional energy stabil-ity, and show that they incur exactly the same computational cost as the original SAV method. 2.3 Structural nature of the sign-indeterminacy The failure analysed above is not a numerical artefact of large time steps or poorly chosen parameters; it is a structural consequence of discretizing rt directly. In the original SAV formulation (2.2), discretizing rt directly by backward Euler (or Crank–Nicolson) yields a linear scalar equation An+1 rn+1 − B n+1 = 0 whose constant term Bn+1 is the sum of the positive quantity rn and a pertur-bation driven by N (φn). As the sharp criterion (2.10) shows, the sign of rn+1 is controlled by the competition between rn and this perturbation, which depends nonlinearly on the spatial structure of φn through the operator ( I + τ GL )−1GL .Nothing in the algebraic structure of the linear equation prevents this perturbation from overcoming rn.The root cause is evident from the structure of h(rn+1 ) = An+1 rn+1 − B n+1 =0: while the slope An+1 ≥ 1 is strictly positive, the intercept −B n+1 has no fixed sign. Geometrically, this means the line h(·) can cross the horizontal axis at any point, positive, zero, or negative, depending on the data φn.The resolution, developed in Section 3, is to reformulate the discrete evolution from rt to ( r2)t. This converts the algebraic structure completely: from a linear 

equation whose constant term has an indefinite sign, to a convex quadratic whose constant term −(rn)2 is strictly negative . Geometrically, a convex quadratic with 

h(0) < 0 and h(+ ∞) = + ∞ must cross the positive r-axis exactly once, irrespec-tive of the data. This structural change is the key to the unconditional positivity guarantees proved in Theorems 1 and 3. We close this section with a remark on why the sign-indeterminacy has largely escaped attention despite the widespread use of the original SAV method. In the ODE reduction, the sign failure requires |φn| > 1 for the double-well potential, a condition that may not arise when the solution stays within the metastable Positivity-preserving SAV schemes 9

wells. In PDE simulations, the sign of the perturbation in (2.10) depends on the interplay between the amplitude of φn and the spectral distribution of the operator (I +τ GL )−1GL : even a perfectly smooth function can trigger the failure if it carries sufficient amplitude at wave numbers for which the eigenvalues of GL are large relative to τ −1.

3 Positivity-preserving SAV schemes 

As analysed in the previous section, the sign-indeterminacy of the original SAV scheme originates from discretizing rt directly, which yields a linear scalar equation whose constant term is not sign-definite. The key observation is that rt = ( r2)t/(2 r)for r > 0; reformulating the continuous equations in terms of ( r2)t and discretizing afterwards converts the scalar equation into a convex quadratic with a strictly negative constant term −(rn)2.In contrast to the sharp criterion (2.10), where positivity must be verified at each step and may fail, the quadratic reformulation guarantees rn+1 > 0 uncondi-tionally by its algebraic structure: a strictly negative constant term together with a strictly convex leading coefficient forces a unique positive root regardless of the data. Thus the sign-indeterminacy, which Section 2 traced to the linear structure of the original discretization, is resolved here not by tuning numerical parameters but by a structural change in the algebraic form of the scalar equation: the discrete evolution is rewritten from rt to ( r2)t, a change that is mathematically trivial at the continuous level yet decisive at the discrete level. 5 Conclusion 

This work has resolved a long-standing but overlooked issue of sign-indeterminacy of the auxiliary variable in the original SAV method while preserving its square-root definition. We first gave a quantitative analysis of the original scheme: a sharp criterion and a sufficient condition for rn+1 > 0 were derived, and an explicit coun-terexample demonstrated that sign failure is structural rather than a numerical pathology; a single-step PDE comparison further illustrated the contrast between the original and the new scheme on a Cahn–Hilliard problem. The central idea is to reformulate the discrete evolution from rt to ( r2)t, which transforms the scalar auxiliary equation from a linear equation with an indefinite constant term into a convex quadratic whose constant term −(rn)2 is strictly negative, thereby al-ways admitting a unique positive root obtained from the quadratic formula. For the Crank–Nicolson scheme, the product-form discretization rn+1 rn is essential: it preserves the quadratic structure, whereas the arithmetic or squared means lead to cubic equations that admit no comparable guarantee. The resulting SAV-BE and SAV-CN schemes incur exactly the same computational cost as the original SAV method and are rigorously proved to be unconditionally energy-stable. Several directions merit further investigation. First, the proposed reformula-tion can be combined with relaxed [16, 19, 15] or weighted [11] SAV frameworks to simultaneously improve consistency between rn+1 and √EN [φn+1 ] + C while re-taining the unconditional positivity guarantee. Second, higher-order extensions via diagonally implicit Runge–Kutta (DIRK) methods are natural: each DIRK stage reduces to a BE-type quadratic, so positivity carries over directly. In contrast, any linear multi-step method beyond the one-step case, including BDF-k (k ≥ 2) and the general multi-step schemes studied in [12], introduces more than two time levels into the ( r2)t discretisation, which breaks the quadratic structure of the con-16 Q.-A. Huang et al. 

stant term and precludes an unconditional positivity proof. Third, rigorous error estimates analogous to those for the original SAV method [21, 2] would complete the theoretical foundation. Finally, although the present analysis focuses on gradi-ent flows, the ( r2)t reformulation is purely algebraic in nature and may also apply to SAV discretisations of Hamiltonian and other energy-conserving systems.
