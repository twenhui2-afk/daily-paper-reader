Abstract Bioacoustics and ethology encompass a wide range of audio understanding tasks, many of which stand to benefit from recent advances in large audio-language models. However, progress in the field has so far been assessed on a narrow set of tasks, primarily centered on label-centric biological category recognition, such as species and call-type classification. In this work, we introduce BEANS-Next, a benchmark grounded in a taxonomy of bioacoustics tasks spanning acoustic perception, biological category recognition, scene understanding, and in-context learning. Using BEANS-Next, we show that existing models exhibit limited performance beyond the task families emphasized by existing evaluations, constraining their usefulness for broader bioacoustic applications. To support progress on this broader task space, we also introduce ROOTS, a large-scale training resource built from expanded curated real-world data and previously underused behavioral and acoustic metadata, supplemented by audio-derived information and scalable synthetic generation where labeling is insufficient. We demonstrate that training on this dataset yields substantial progress across all task groups of BEANS-Next, moving audio-language models closer to their potential as general-purpose assistants for bioacoustics and ethology. To accelerate progress in the field, we open-source our benchmark, dataset, and data pipelines. aaProject page: https://earthspecies.github.io/beans-next/. 

## 1 Introduction 

Bioacoustics examines how animals produce, perceive, and use sound. Animal sounds carry information about species identity, behavior, social dynamics, and ecological context, making bioacoustics central to animal behavior research, biodiversity monitoring, conservation, and the study of animal communication (Bradbury and Vehrencamp, 1998; Fischer, Noser, and Hammerschmidt, 2013; Rutz et al., 2023). Machine learning has become a standard tool for analyzing these sounds, with applications including species identification, individual recognition, call-type classification, and behavioral annotation (Stowell, 2022). However, the practical needs of ethologists—researchers who study animal behavior—are broader: researchers often measure acoustic structure (Jaeyeon Kim et al., 2026), identify biological categories, count and compare events (Sueur et al., 2008; Holt et al., 2009; Briggs et al., 2012; Marques et al., 2013), reason over temporal patterns (Kershenbaum et al., 2025; Culhane et al., 2025), and adapt analyses to new taxa or study-specific sound classes (Brown, Montgomery, and Garg, 2021; Nieto-Mora et al., 2023; Cauzinille et al., 2024; Kate and Neethirajan, 2025; Kyalo et al., 2025). This breadth makes bioacoustics a compelling domain for large audio-language models (LALMs), which promise a single interface for diverse audio-understanding tasks. Current LALMs for bioacoustics lack the breadth required to address the full needs of practitioners. Existing benchmarks primarily evaluate classification into a limited subset of biologically relevant categories,

especially species or a narrow set of call types (Hagiwara et al., 2023; Rauch, Schwinger, et al., 2025). Many core bioacoustic abilities remain untested: acoustic measurement, call counting, temporal reasoning, call comparison, and adaptation to new taxa or task-specific categories (Sueur et al., 2008; Holt et al., 2009; Briggs et al., 2012). At the same time, standard training datasets provide limited supervision for these abilities. Bioacoustic archives are often weakly labeled and semi-structured, making them difficult to incorporate into audio-language model training without substantial processing into scientifically meaningful audio-language tasks (Vellinga and Planqué, 2015; Pérez-Granados et al., 2026; Chasmai et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024). This bottleneck means we currently lack both the evaluation data to measure missing abilities in audio-language models and the training resources needed to make progress. To address the evaluation side of this bottleneck, we introduce BEANS-Next, a benchmark for measuring broad bioacoustic audio-language abilities needed for ethology research. BEANS-Next is organized around a taxonomy of tasks that reflects both scientific workflows and groups of audio-language model capabilities, spanning acoustic perception, biological category recognition, scene understanding, and in-context learning. It builds on established benchmarks BEANS-Zero (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025) and BirdSet (Rauch, Schwinger, et al., 2025), while broadening them across all task groups. Evaluating on BEANS-Next reveals that existing bioacoustic models are narrow, performing competitively on familiar tasks but poorly on broader ones, while general-purpose audio-language models remain poor across most tasks. To address the training side of this bottleneck, we introduce ROOTS, a large-scale bioacoustic training dataset with 44 million audio-language pairs from 6.5 million distinct audio clips. ROOTS is built from curated bioacoustic archives, acoustic and behavioral metadata that are underused in computational research, scalable synthetic audio pipelines, and audio-derived metadata. We convert semi-structured and unstructured metadata, such as field notes, into instruction-following tasks for species recognition, taxonomy prediction, call-type classification, life-stage prediction, acoustic description, and other biologically meaningful outputs. Where archive labels are weak or incomplete, we use scalable synthetic audio generation pipelines that provide strongly labeled examples for detection, event counting, temporal reasoning, and multi-audio in-context learning. Together, these sources cover the same task groups as BEANS-Next. We use ROOTS to train bioacoustic audio-language models and evaluate them on BEANS-Next. Starting from a shared NatureLM-audio base model, we fine-tune a separate model on the ROOTS supervision for each tier, isolating the effect of each type of supervision to each ability. Tier-specific training substantially improves performance across all tiers of BEANS-Next, including abilities that are weak or absent in existing models. Because of the aligned taxonomy and training recipe, we show that curated metadata improves biological category recognition, LLM-based text synthesis supports richer description and acoustic perception, and synthetic audio enables scene understanding and in-context capabilities.

Outline and Contributions 

(1) We introduce BEANS-Next , a benchmark for evaluating bioacoustic audio-language models across 

acoustic perception , biological category recognition , scene understanding , and in-context learning .(2) We introduce ROOTS , a large-scale bioacoustic audio-language training dataset built from curated archives, underused and audio-derived metadata, LLM-based text synthesis, and synthetic audio pipelines. (3) We characterize a broader bioacoustic ability space required by ethology and bioacoustics, and show that existing bioacoustic and general-purpose LALMs cover only a limited portion of this space. (4) We demonstrate that training on ROOTS improves performance across BEANS-Next , including ca-pabilities that are weak or absent in existing models, while also improving performance on established bioacoustic tasks. (5) We release BEANS-Next and an evaluation library along with ROOTS and its synthetic audio subsetsto support reproducible research on audio-language model evaluation for bioacoustics and ethology. 

## 2 Related Work 

With the exception of NatureLM-audio (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025), most prior work on LALMs has focused on human speech, music, and general environmental sound. Models such as LTU, SALMONN, Qwen-Audio, Audio Flamingo, and GAMA connect pretrained audio encoders to large language models in order to support instruction following, question answering, captioning, dialogue, and audio reasoning over broad audio inputs (Gong et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2024; Ghosh, Goel, Jayakumar, et al., 2026; Ghosh et al., 2024). Complementary benchmarks such as MMAU evaluate increasingly complex audio understanding and reasoning tasks (Sakshi et al., 2025). However, these models and benchmarks are largely centered on human-centric audio distributions, and general audio competence does not directly imply the fine-grained acoustic perception, biological knowledge, or study-specific adaptability required for bioacoustic research. Bioacoustics has also seen rapid progress in representation learning. Early work showed that large bird-trained embeddings can transfer beyond birds to other taxa and acoustic domains (Ghani et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2025). Self-supervised models such as AVES and animal2vec adapt speech-inspired pretraining objectives to animal vocalizations, improving transfer to downstream bioacoustic classification and detection tasks (Hagi-wara, 2023; Schäfer-Zimmermann et al., 2026). More recent models, including Bird-MAE and TweetyBERT, fur-ther specialize masked prediction objectives for avian soundscapes and fine-grained birdsong parsing (Rauch, Heinrich, et al., 2025; Vengrovski et al., 2026). While these approaches provide strong acoustic representations, they remain primarily encoder-based and are usually evaluated through fixed-output tasks such as species classification, detection, clustering, or individual identification (Schwinger et al., 2026; Miron et al., 2026). Language supervision has only recently been introduced into bioacoustic foundation models. BioLingual uses contrastive audio-language pretraining to support zero-shot classification and retrieval from natural language descriptions (D. Robinson, A. Robinson, and Akrapongpisak, 2024). NatureLM-audio extends this direction to instruction-following audio-language modeling, enabling zero-shot prediction of species, call type, life stage, and other biologically meaningful labels (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025). These models demon-strate that language-conditioned bioacoustic models are feasible, but their evaluation remains concentrated on biological category recognition. Broader ethological abilities such as acoustic measurement, event counting, call comparison, temporal reasoning, and adaptation to study-specific sound categories remain underexplored. Datasets and benchmarks in bioacoustics have similarly focused on recognition problems. BEANS provides a multi-task benchmark for classification and detection across animal sounds (Hagiwara et al., 2023), BIRB evaluates retrieval and generalization in bird vocalizations (Hamer et al., 2023), and BirdSet standardizes large-scale avian soundscape classification under domain shift (Rauch, Schwinger, et al., 2025). Recent audio-language benchmarks have begun to expose the limitations of general LALMs in bioacoustically relevant settings. In particular, WOW-Bench evaluates fine-grained acoustic perception and cognition using marine mammal vocalizations, highlighting the difficulty of low-level listening in unfamiliar acoustic domains (Jaeyeon Kim et al., 2026). However, existing benchmarks remain limited in taxonomic scope, task structure, or coverage

Recent synthetic-data pipelines address part of this limitation by generating strongly labeled examples for sound event detection and few-shot detection (Hoffman et al., 2025; Mahon et al., 2025). These resources support recognition, detection, and data scaling, but are not organized around the broader capabilities needed for general-purpose bioacoustic audio-language models. BEANS-Next and ROOTS address this gap with a benchmark and an aligned training corpus built on the same four-tier taxonomy. 

## 3 Data construction 

3.1 A taxonomy of bioacoustic audio-language abilities While machine learning in bioacoustics has focused primarily on species identification, a broader range of tasks is required by real-world ethology workflows. To structure evaluation and measure progress, we cluster ethological tasks into groups of related tasks we refer to as ‘tiers’. These tiers align not only with scientific usage but also with categories of capabilities that have been proposed as relevant to develop and evaluate in LALMs (Ma et al., 2025; Z. Liu et al., 2026; Ghosh, Goel, Jaehyeon Kim, et al., 2025). Each has multiple task families, which are comprised of multiple tasks. 

Tier 1: Acoustic perception. These tasks measure whether a model can recover the low-level properties of animal sounds, such as fundamental frequency, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and acoustic description, without requiring biological category recognition. We instantiate this tier with task families for acoustic-property estimation, acoustic description matching, and acoustic captioning. Scientifically, acoustic measurement is a key part of the workflow of characterizing, interpreting, or comparing acoustic variation (Odom et al., 2021). 

Tier 2: Biological category recognition. These tasks ask what biological or ecological categories are present, such as species, broader taxonomic groups, call types, life stage, or behavior. We instantiate this tier with task families for species and taxonomy recognition, taxon presence detection, call-type recognition, life-stage and behavior prediction, and semantic captioning. These are closest to established bioacoustic benchmarks (Hagiwara et al., 2023; Rauch, Schwinger, et al., 2025; D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025) and support biodiversity monitoring, repertoire analysis, and other analyses of communication. 

Tier 3: Scene understanding. These tasks measure whether a model can interpret longer, structured recordings containing multiple vocalizations, species, or individuals. We instantiate this tier with task families for event and species counting, temporal localization, co-occurrence, order reasoning, acoustic comparison across events, species listing, and structured scene description. This setting is especially relevant for passive acoustic monitoring, where recordings often contain overlapping calls, repeated events, multiple individuals or species, and long stretches of background sound. Scientifically, these tasks support questions about not only what occurred, but also when. 

Tier 4: In-context learning. These tasks measure whether a model can adapt to a task specified at inference time using text descriptions or audio examples. We instantiate this tier with task families for audio-example-conditioned classification, few-shot detection, unseen-species recognition, and call-type matching. For example, a researcher may provide examples of an unknown call type, a rare species, or a project-specific sound category and ask the model to identify matching sounds in new recordings. Because bioacoustic data are long-tailed across taxa, tasks, and recording conditions, in-context abilities are critical for learning new tasks at runtime without training a separate classifier for every study. 3.2 Text creation methods Both BEANS-Next and ROOTS require converting bioacoustic annotations into instruction-following audio-language examples. We use two complementary text creation methods: templated construction and LLM-based construction. These are shared across the benchmark and the dataset. However, BEANS-Next is exclusively grounded on human-annotated metadata, while ROOTS scales supervision by additionally relying on audio-derived metadata, such as acoustic measurements and spectrogram-based descriptions, and synthetic audio.

Templated construction 

We construct prompt-response pairs from structured fields such as species identity, taxonomy, locality, call type, life stage, and other available metadata. Each source dataset is mapped into a unified schema, transformed in a task-dependent way, and then converted into multiple task variants using prompt and response templates and algorithmic transformations. The full set of templated tasks and source datasets is provided in Appendix B.1. 

LLM-based construction 

Templated construction provides reliable supervision when metadata are already structured, but it cannot capture the full range of information available in bioacoustic archives. Many recordings are also accompanied by free-text or semi-structured annotations, such as field notes, that contain scientifically useful information but are difficult to convert into fixed templates without losing nuance. We therefore use an LLM-based pipeline to transform such evidence into natural-language question-answer pairs and descriptions (overview in Appendix B.2.1). For each task, we write a dedicated prompt specifying the available evidence, the intended task, the output format, constraints on what information may be used, and skip conditions for when the evidence is insufficient or ambiguous. Compared with broad multi-task generation prompts (Ghosh, Goel, Jayakumar, et al., 2026), this single-task design gives finer control over task definitions, their alignment with bioacoustic use cases, and their relative frequency, and narrows each generation problem enough to use a smaller, faster LLM, Gemma 4 26B MoE (Farabet and Lacombe, 2026). Where acoustic supervision is scarce, we also use Gemma’s vision capabilities to describe vocalizations characteristics from spectrograms, prompting with human-written examples and restricting descriptions to acoustic properties. Generated text may contain unsupported claims, inaudible information, references to information that should not be available to the model, or other errors. We therefore filter outputs with regex-based checks for format, keyword, and schema compliance, and with LLM-as-a-judge scores for instruction following with respect to ground truth, hallucination, audibility, and difficulty. One author manually reviewed samples of pre- and post-filtered outputs for each task category as a quality-control step. LLM-based tasks and source datasets are listed in Appendix B.2. 3.3 BEANS-Next benchmark construction BEANS-Next is constructed as a held-out evaluation suite aligned with the four-tier taxonomy in Section 3.1. Evaluation examples are built from real recordings and human- or dataset-provided annotations. Templated and LLM-based text construction are used only to convert supported annotations into prompts and responses, not to introduce new labels. All BEANS-Next benchmark recordings, and any derivatives of those recordings, are strictly disjoint from ROOTS. For tasks where species identity is central, we additionally follow the held-out species protocol introduced in NatureLM-audio (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025): BEANS-Next uses the same held-out species split where applicable, excluding those taxa from the corresponding ROOTS/NatureLM-audio training mixture. We list the held-out taxa in Appendix D.2. Per-tier data sources, task formats, sample counts, and metrics are detailed in Appendix D. 3.4 ROOTS training data construction ROOTS is an instruction-following training corpus built from curated bioacoustic archives, task-specific annotated datasets, audio-derived metadata, and the synthetic audio pipelines described below. Each source is mapped into a shared schema. Structured fields such as species, taxonomy, call type, life stage, sex, and locality are converted with templates, while free-text notes, acoustic measurements, and spectrogram-derived descriptions are converted with the LLM-based pipeline (Section 3.2). Together, these sources provide supervision across all four tiers of the taxonomy. Source-specific tasks and counts are given in Appendices B and C.5. 

Synthetic Audio Pipelines 

For most species, bioacoustic data with temporally strong labels are unavailable (Nolasco et al., 2023). In addition, archives used to train bioacoustic foundation models often contain label noise and missing labels (Michaud et al., 2023). To address these gaps, we use heavy augmentation pipelines to synthesize strongly labeled bioacoustic recordings at scale, which are then passed to the text construction pipelines. We construct two single-audio synthetic datasets. Strong detection converts weakly labeled bioacoustic audio into synthetic, strongly labeled recordings with precise timestamps for each species. These recordings are used by the text synthesis pipeline to generate scene understanding tasks. Detect-diarize creates synthetic recordings with temporally strong labels and a proxy speaker ID for each call. Starting from BirdMixIT source-separated tracks (Denton, Wisdom, and Hershey, 2022), we extract call events and add them to synthetic scenes while preserving their original order and timing and placing them at a consistent amplitude scale. We also construct synthetic multi-audio datasets. Few-shot multi-label classification gives audio examples of up to four target classes and the model must predict which of those classes are present in a query audio clip. Call-type tasks use sound clusters to construct synthetic call-type tasks in multiple formats, including multiple choice, binary presence prediction, and same–different prediction between two sounds. Positive examples are sampled from the same species and acoustic cluster according to our processing pipeline, while negatives are sampled from random clusters outside the target cluster. 

## 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Models We evaluate the bioacoustic audio-language model NatureLM-audio v1.0 (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025) and two strong open general-purpose LALMs, Audio Flamingo Next (AF-Next) (Ghosh, Goel, Jayakumar, et al., 2026) and Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025). We focus on generative LALMs because most BEANS-Next tasks require open-ended or multi-audio answers that encoder-only and contrastive models cannot produce; proprietary API models were excluded due to high failure rates and cost (Appendix E). We additionally train NatureLM-audio v1.1 as the base model for our ROOTS experiments; it retains the v1.0 architecture but adds expanded training data and support for longer and multiple audio inputs. All models are queried through the same inference, parsing, and scoring pipeline. 4.2 Training NatureLM-audio v1.1 is trained on an expanded version of the NatureLM-audio v1.0 semantic task mixture, using curated metadata and primarily templated supervision. From this checkpoint, we fine-tune a separate model on the additional ROOTS supervision for each tier (NatureLM-audio T1–T4), with the audio encoder and Q-former unfrozen and the LLM fine-tuned with LoRA (E. J. Hu et al., 2022) (Appendix C.2). Holding architecture and initialization fixed isolates the effect of the training data: because the base model has seen primarily templated semantic supervision, comparisons against it measure the contribution of LLM-based text synthesis, synthetic audio, and audio-derived metadata. We do not train a single joint model in this work; joint multi-tier training, possible interference between tiers, and architectural changes are left to future work.

## 5 Results 

On the established benchmarks (Tables 1 and 2), NatureLM-audio models are far stronger than general-purpose LALMs at species-level classification. On BirdSet, Audio Flamingo Next and Qwen3-Omni score near zero, mostly answering ‘Bird’ or ‘None’. NatureLM-audio v1.1 improves over v1.0 on CBI, call type, and BirdSet, but regresses on ESC-50, HumBugDB, life stage, and individual counting. 5.1 Tier 1: Acoustic Perception Table 3 shows that ROOTS Tier 1 training adds signal-level capabilities that are weak in the NatureLM-audio v1.1 base model, improving acoustic descriptions, SNR estimation, and mean f0 prediction. The improvement on unseen-species f0 estimation is particularly important because it suggests that the model is not only exploiting taxon-specific priors, but has learned a more transferable mapping from audio to quantitative acoustic attributes. The main exception is crow vocalization description, where the general-purpose LALMs remain better; this likely points to residual sensitivity to domain and annotation format, rather than uniform transfer across all acoustic-description settings. 5.2 Tier 2: Biological Category Recognition Table 4 shows that ROOTS Tier 2 training also improves biological category recognition. The gains across presence, call-type, and behavior prediction suggest that mined metadata provides useful supervision beyond species labels alone. This supports the broader claim that improving bioacoustic LALMs is not only a matter of adding more labeled recordings, but also of expanding the kinds of biological supervision available to the model. Captioning remains weak in absolute terms, indicating that open-ended biological description remains harder than discriminative metadata prediction. 5.3 Tier 3: Scene Understanding Table 5 shows that ROOTS Tier 3 training mainly improves the parsing of scenes into events and relations. The largest gains occur when species identities must be bound to event-level attributes, such as which species vocalized first, which vocalization was highest or lowest in pitch, and whether vocalizations overlapped, with consistent gains across all multiple-choice characteristic tasks. The supervision is nonetheless incomplete: counting remains difficult, with a species-count error slightly worse than the base models, and open-ended duration-based identification does not improve. One hypothesis is that the architecture limits fine-grained temporal perception, consistent with recent moves toward denser audio embeddings and timestamp-based positional encodings (Ghosh, Goel, Jayakumar, et al., 2026). 5.4 Tier 4: In-Context Learning Table 6 shows the strongest evidence that ROOTS can change how the model uses its inputs. Audio Flamingo Next and the NatureLM-audio v1.1 base model remain near chance on the matching tasks, suggesting that they do not use the support audio as task-defining context. Qwen3-Omni performs well above chance on call-type and unseen-species matching, indicating that general-domain in-context learning partially transfers to bioacoustics. Tier 4 training yields the best results on all five tasks, showing that synthetic multi-audio supervision teaches the model to compare a query against in-context examples rather than rely only on labels seen in training. Gains are smallest on Gibbons, a low-SNR dataset, suggesting limited robustness to recording conditions and to the domain shift from synthetic data.

## 6 Conclusion 

Existing audio-language models, both general-purpose and bioacoustic, cover only a limited portion of the capabilities ethologists and bioacousticians actually rely on. By organizing evaluation and training around a shared capability taxonomy, we are able to measure these gaps and show that targeted data construction— curated metadata, LLM-based text synthesis, and synthetic audio pipelines—can begin to close them. We see this as a step toward audio-language models that support the full range of bioacoustic workflows rather than only naming what is present. Substantial headroom remains, and we hope the released benchmark and dataset help the community make progress on these. These capabilities also raise concerns worth flagging. Both BEANS-Next and ROOTS inherit taxonomic and geographic biases from public bioacoustic archives, which may limit reliability for underrepresented taxa and ecosystems. Stronger detection of endangered or vulnerable species could be misused when combined with geolocation metadata, a risk we partially mitigate by excluding endangered species from our Xeno-canto sources. And as audio-language models are deployed more widely in ecological workflows, errors—including hallucinated species, behaviors, or counts—could propagate into downstream scientific or conservation decisions. We therefore recommend these models be used as assistive tools, with predictions validated by domain experts. AI use statement We used generative AI tools (Gemma 4 26B MoE) as a core component of our data construction pipelines, specifically synthesizing question-answer pairs and descriptions from semi-structured and unstructured metadata, generating spectrogram-derived acoustic descriptions, and scoring candidate examples via an LLM-as-a-judge filtering step (instruction following, hallucination, audibility, and difficulty), as described in Section 3.2 and Appendix B.2. One of the authors manually reviewed a sample of pre- and post-filtered LLM outputs for each category of data as an additional quality-control step. We also used AI coding assistants to help implement portions of the training, evaluation, data processing, and data synthesis code, and to assist with routine cluster job management and orchestration. All code was reviewed and tested by at least one of the authors before use in reported experiments. Generative AI was used to refine some of the writing in the paper, after which it was carefully reviewed for accuracy. It was also used to spot any obvious omissions or mistakes before submission, to generate visual elements for Figure 1 and 2, and to edit the format of tables, in which case a manual review of table values was conducted. We take responsibility for the final content of this work, including text, claims, and artifacts produced with the aid of generative AI. Reproducibility statement We release the following artifacts to support the reproducibility of our results. BEANS-Next and ROOTS are publicly available on Hugging Face, together with the synthetic audio subsets Synthetic Strong Detection, Synthetic Detect-Diarize, and AnimalSpeak-PseudoVox. For ROOTS, we do not redistribute audio. However, all audio used is publicly available, and we provide guidance for retrieving and preprocessing it accordingly. The BEANS-Next evaluation library is publicly available on GitHub and implements data loading, model-server interfaces, answer parsing, and metric computation. Benchmark construction, task definitions, sample counts, metrics, and held-out taxa are described in Appendix D, including Table D.1.1 and Appendix D.2. ROOTS construction, covering templated and LLM-based tasks, filtering, and synthetic audio generation, is described in Appendix B. Templates are provided on Hugging Face with ROOTS, as well as their mapping to specific samples. Training and fine-tuning hyperparameters, augmentations, and compute are given in Appendices C.1 and C.2. The evaluation protocol, post-processing, and metric definitions are in Appendix E, and the text-only and noise controls are in Appendix E.6.

B.3 Synthetic Audio Creation Synthetic scenes were constructed by mixing extracted vocalizations (“pseudovox”) into real environmental backgrounds. Pseudovox were obtained by source-separating Xeno-canto recordings using BirdMixIT (Denton, Wisdom, and Hershey, 2022) and extracting short activity windows exactly as proposed in DRASDIC (Hoffman et al., 2025); we used a filtered subset of these clips, each tagged with a species label (predicted by sl-efficientnet-bio (Miron et al., 2026) and kept where it matched the metadata), an acoustic cluster ID reused from DRASDIC, and a duration. Background recordings were drawn from SilentCities. To construct a scene, we sample a background, optionally mix two backgrounds for acoustic diversity, and insert a configurable number of pseudovox events at target SNRs sampled from a Gaussian mixture (range −20 dB to +15 dB), with random resampling, optional time reversal, and randomized inter-event gaps. All audio is rendered at 16 kHz. Four datasets were generated from this pipeline. Call type MCQ and Binary call type detection present the model with one or more example pseudovox clips of a target call type and ask it to identify or detect that call type in a separate audio clip. Same/different call type presents two pseudovox clips and asks whether they share a call type. For these three tasks, “call type” is operationalized as the intersection of species and acoustic cluster (“fine mode”); negatives are sampled from unrelated species–cluster combinations. Few-shot detection is a multi-label variant: the model is given example clips for 1–4 candidate sounds plus a query scene of 3–10 s and must identify which (if any) of the candidates appear in the scene; queries optionally include a short clip of the scene’s background environment to help the model distinguish target events

## C Training 

C.1 Training Hyperparameters The stage-1 LALM was trained on only open-ended species classification data, with a global batch size of 128 for 350k steps, at a peak lr of 1e − 4 with 25,000 steps of warmup and cosine decay to a minimum lr of 3e − 5.The stage-2 LALM was trained for 1.5 million steps and a global batch size of 128 at a peak lr of 1e − 4 with 25,000 steps of warmup and cosine decay to a minimum lr of 3e − 5. Audio was limited to 10-second clips. This completed stage-2 formed the base model for fine-tuning. C.2 Fine-tuning Hyperparameters The per-tier fine-tunings were instantiated from the stage 2 checkpoint for 2 epochs, using a global batch size of 128. We used an initial learning rate of 1e−6, 15,000 warmup steps to a peak learning rate of 1e−4, and cosine decay to a minimum learning rate of 1e−5. LoRA fine-tuning was applied to the query, key, value, and output projection layers, using rank 32, α = 32 , and dropout 0.1. Audio was limited to 30-second clips. C.3 Augmentations We apply audio augmentations during stage-2 training to improve robustness to background noise and over-lapping vocalizations. With probability 0.4, we add background noise sampled from a pool of environmental and general-audio noise corpora, including DEMAND, IDMT, TUT, UrbanSound, OrCA/ship-noise datasets, WHAM, AudioSet, and SilentCities, at an SNR sampled uniformly from [−10 , 25] dB. Noise datasets were curated in advance, including SilentCities being processed with sl-efficient-all model (Miron et al., 2026) to filter clips with animal vocalizations. For detection-style tasks, we additionally apply a noise-masking variant with probability 0.15, replacing the target signal with background noise and training the model to output the corresponding negative label. We also use task-aware mixup with probability 0.3, mixing up to three compatible partner recordings into a primary example with Beta-distributed weights ( α = 0 .4). For open-ended detection tasks, partner labels are unioned into the target answer; for option-based detection tasks, mixed-in labels are added only when they appear among the provided candidate options, otherwise they serve as distractor background. Noise augmentation is applied across all training stages, while MixUp is only applied in stage-2 onward and only for detection tasks. C.4 Computational resources For each fine-tune, 8 NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs were used. For Tier 1 and Tier 4, the fine-tuning time was approximately 1 day. For Tier 2 and Tier 3, the fine-tuning time was approximately 3 days. Thus, the total train time was approximately 64 H100 days. These follow ISO 226, and Xeno-canto provides a guide for their computation, making them likely consistent. 1 We create an open-ended regression SNR evaluation, as well as an MCQ-based evaluation, that provides 4 bins with indicative SNR ranges as options. For acoustic description, we use the LLM-based pipeline to mine acoustic descriptions from Xeno-canto sample field notes, and reformat them into captions or MCQs. The synthesis LLM retains only directly acoustic information such as pitch, texture, frequency movement, and rhythm, while excluding species names, call-type labels, behavioral context, and other semantic cues. 

Tier 2 benchmark processing. For taxon presence detection, we used held-out Xeno-canto validation recordings and constructed balanced binary yes/no tasks for bird, mammal, insect, and amphibian vocalizations. Positives contain the target taxon according to focal label; negatives are sampled from other taxa in the held-out pool, with additional filtering on background species and field notes in the metadata. For call-type evaluation, we derived binary alarm-call, flight-call, and begging-call presence tasks, plus a fixed-vocabulary multi-label task over alarm call , flight call , begging call , song , and call . These examples are based on BEANS-Zero call-type variants linked to Xeno-canto metadata. We additionally include two LLM-synthesized Tier 2 semantic evaluation splits from Xeno-canto and iNaturalist BEANS-Zero/new held-out pools. Semantic captioning asks for natural-language descriptions of audible biological content, including call type, behavioral context, acoustic character, life stage when acoustically relevant, and background species when supported by the metadata. Behavior MCQs ask which audible behavior or context best matches the recording, using plausible distractors generated from the same metadata regime. These tasks differ from Tier 1 acoustic captions because semantic labels and behavioral meaning are allowed when supported by fieldNotes , occurrenceRemarks , and species metadata. We removed generations judged insufficiently audible, interesting, or difficult using an LLM-judge filter. 

Tier 3 benchmark processing. For single-audio scene understanding, we used recordings from Birdeep, Powdermill, NocturnalBirdMigration, and BirdVox full-night, cropped randomly to between 1 and 30 seconds. Their ground-truth selection tables provide species labels, temporal extents, and, for Birdeep, frequency ranges; synthesis prompts convert these annotations into natural questions while excluding ambiguous rows such as ties, boundary-clipped events that could change the answer, or cases with insufficient annotation support. Tier 3 includes LLM-based text synthesis for species counting, total vocalization counting, per-species vocalization counting, temporal referring (start, middle, or end of recording), vocalization co-occurrence, species by vocalization order (e.g. which species vocalizes third), species by highest pitch, species by lowest pitch, species by longest vocalization, species by vocalization frequency or dominance, and structural caption-ing, which provides an overview of vocalizations in the recording. We include both open-ended and MCQ variants where appropriate, the latter of which provide reference species as options, with a random number of them being other species that are present in the recording. We also create tasks with templates, namely listing all species in the recording, listing the frequency range, and listing the species, frequency range, and vocalization count for each species. 

Tier 4 benchmark processing. For gibbon few-shot detection, we used Hainan gibbon recordings and held out fixed support examples for three call categories: multiple-pulse calls, single-pulse calls, and duets. Each query asks which support sound, if any, appears in the target recording. We include both the full naturally imbalanced split and a balanced split for headline evaluation.

## E Evaluation Pipeline and Metrics 

E.1 Models 

NatureLM-audio v1.0. NatureLM-audio v1.0 (D. Robinson, Miron, et al., 2025) is an audio-language foun-dation model specifically designed for bioacoustics, combining a BEATs audio encoder with a Llama 3.1 8b instruction-tuned language model backbone. The model was trained on paired audio-text data spanning animal vocalizations, speech, and music, and supports a broad range of zero-shot bioacoustic tasks, including species classification, vocalization detection, call-type recognition, and audio captioning. NatureLM-audio was introduced alongside the BEANS-Zero benchmark and demonstrated strong transfer performance on unseen taxa and tasks. 

NatureLM-audio v1.1. We train NatureLM-audio v1.1 for the first time in this work. It retains the architecture of NatureLM-audio v1.0 but incorporates infrastructure improvements supporting longer audio and multiple audio files as input, together with newly ingested training data from the same underlying bioacoustic archives. NatureLM-audio v1.1 serves as the common base checkpoint for the tier-specific ROOTS experiments. Project resources and model weights are publicly available through the associated web page. 2

Audio Flamingo Next (AF-Next). Audio Flamingo Next (Ghosh, Goel, Jayakumar, et al., 2026) is a large open audio-language model for unified understanding of speech, environmental sounds, and music. AF-Next extends the Audio Flamingo family with substantially larger-scale training data, long-context audio processing (up to 30 minutes), and temporal chain-of-thought reasoning mechanisms grounded in timestamps. The model achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance across a wide range of audio understanding and reasoning benchmarks. AF-Next builds upon earlier Audio Flamingo models that introduced few-shot and conversational audio-language reasoning capabilities for open-ended audio understanding tasks. Model checkpoints and project resources are available through NVIDIA research releases and Hugging Face. 3

Qwen3-Omni Instruct. Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025) is a unified multimodal foundation model supporting text, image, audio, and video understanding and generation within a single architecture. E.2 Evaluation library Our evaluation is fully decoupled from model serving: models run behind an HTTP predict endpoint, while the BEANS-Next runner performs post-processing and metric computation locally. For each dataset example, the runner sends the audio (and task prompt) to the model server, receives a raw text prediction, applies a task-specific post-processing pipeline, and then computes deterministic metrics (or routes to an LLM judge for non-deterministic tasks). The evaluation writes both per-example artifacts (raw, processed, and scored predictions) and an aggregate summary with per-task metrics. GPT-4o audio-preview and Gemini 3.8 Flash were excluded due to high rates of API-level failures and cost, respectively. GPT-4o refused to process approximately 30% of examples citing playback restrictions. For Gemini 3.8 Flash, we sampled 500 samples from all tasks in BEANS-Zero and BEANS-Next, and estimated the total cost for evaluating on the two benchmarks with ‘high’ thinking mode to be $7,500. E.3 Post-processing: parsing and label matching Let ˆyraw be the raw model text output for an example. Post-processing produces a segment list S = [ s1, . . . , s k]

and a canonical joined string ˆy obtained by concatenating segments with “ , ”. The pipeline is explicitly split into two phases: 

Parser phase (structural extraction). Parser steps only reshape text into label-like fragments without normalization. For classification and detection tasks, we apply comma-based parsing: • parse_labels_comma : splits each segment on commas into multiple segments, while avoiding split-ting numeric thousands separators (e.g., “ 1,654.75 ”). 

Cleaner phase (normalization and label alignment). Cleaner steps normalize segments and align them to the task label vocabulary: • normalize_whitespace : collapses runs of whitespace and strips leading/trailing spaces per segment. • strip_eos : truncates any text following a model-specific end-of-sequence marker (default “ <|end_ of_text|> ”) per segment. • fuzzy_match_to_labels : maps each segment to the closest vocabulary label by minimum Levenshtein distance; optionally, a distance threshold can reject low-confidence matches (replacing them with a default label). • conservative_match_to_labels : a higher-precision variant intended for multi-label detection, using (i) case-insensitive exact match, (ii) case-insensitive longest substring match, then (iii) bounded Levenshtein matching; unmatched fragments are typically dropped. • extract_label_from_text / extract_mcq_choice_from_text / extract_scientific_ name_from_text / extract_hz_bucket_from_text : specialized extractors used by tasks whose model outputs are verbose (e.g., multiple-choice explanations, scientific-name outputs with timestamps, or numeric frequency estimates). Fuzzy label matching is applied only when a task has a fixed label vocabulary (e.g., classification/detec-tion). For captioning and other free-form tasks (open-ended, counting, QA), post-processing is limited to whitespace/EOS cleanup to avoid corrupting natural-language outputs.

E.4 Task types and metrics Each evaluation task declares a task type, which determines both the post-processing configuration and the metric family.  

> E.4.1 Classification

Classification tasks produce a single label from a finite vocabulary. After post-processing and label alignment, we compute: • Accuracy : fraction of examples where the (post-processed) predicted label exactly matches the ground-truth label. • Macro precision , macro recall , macro F1 : computed by averaging per-class precision/recall/F1 equally across classes.  

> E.4.2 Detection (multi-label presence)

Detection tasks allow multiple labels per example (presence/absence). After comma parsing and label matching, we compute: • Macro Average Precision (mAP) : average precision computed per label from the precision–recall curve and then macro-averaged across labels. • Macro precision , macro recall , macro F1 : computed from predicted vs. target label sets (per example), then aggregated per task.  

> E.4.3 Captioning

Captioning tasks produce free-form text descriptions. Because single-sample CIDEr is degenerate, we compute captioning metrics at the corpus level: • CIDEr (corpus mean, normalized to [0 , 1] ): reported as the mean CIDEr score over the evaluation set. E.5 Computational resources For NatureLM-audio v1.0 and v1.1 checkpoints, as well as Audio Flamingo Next, an NVIDIA A100 40GB GPU was used for each for approximately 1 day to run all evaluations. For Qwen3-Omni, an NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU was used for approximately 1 day. These estimates are similar for the 3 audio-language models and 6 configurations of language models for the ablations in Sec. E.6. However, only BEANS-Next evaluation was done for these ablations, which accounts for around an eighth of samples. Thus, the total evaluation resources were around 4 A100 days and 1 H100 days for the main evaluations, and 1 A100 day and 0.5 H100 day for the ablations. E.6 Text-only and Gaussian-noise controls MMAU and music question answering have indicated that LALMs can sometimes correctly respond to queries even when the correct audio is not provided (Sakshi et al., 2025; Zang et al., 2025). Complementary experiments find that audio-language models can favor textual cues when text and audio conflict (C. Wang et al., 2025). We thus evaluate the base LLMs of our chosen LALMs, namely Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, and Qwen3 30B-A3B (although, Qwen3-Omni utilizes a modified version of it) without audio. We also measure performance of the chosen LALMs, namely NatureLM-audio v1.0, Audio Flamingo Next (AF-Next), and Qwen3-Omni, when Gaussian noise is provided as input, rather than the correct audio file. We use these experiments as proxies to assess whether textual cues are benefiting models during evaluation in BEANS-Next. 

Conditions. We distinguish two text-only conditions. In the informed condition, all audio inputs and placeholders are removed and the following instruction is prepended to each question: 

> Audio is intentionally unavailable. Answer using only the text and answer choices. Make your best guess and follow the requested answer format. Do not refuse or explain. 29 BEANS-NEXT AND ROOTS: BROADENING AUDIO-LANGUAGE CAPABILITIES FOR BIOACOUSTICS

In the silent condition, audio is removed without this instruction. The question text and answer choices are otherwise unchanged. We use each model’s native chat template and deterministic decoding. For the three audio-language models, we compare real audio with duration-matched Gaussian noise at −20 dBFS RMS. Noise is supplied with the ordinary audio prompt, without an additional informed condition. 

Scoring and answer validity. We apply the same task-specific scoring rules across conditions. Unam-biguous answer prefixes and bulleted call-type lists are accepted; explicit missing-audio refusals are invalid answers rather than negative presence predictions. Invalid MCQ and binary-presence answers receive zero accuracy. Regression MAE is conditional on a parseable numerical answer, with its parse rate (the share of answers with a parseable number) reported alongside the error, as in Tables 3 and 5. For per-species counting, validity requires a parseable species–count mapping or explicit absence. Table 14(b) separately reports parse rates for MCQ and label-set tasks, pooling accepted answers over attempted examples. 

Results. Tables 11–14 report the control results. Informing text models that audio is unavailable substantially changes their response behavior. Across Tier 1 MCQs, the three LLMs produce valid answers on 99.5–99.6% of examples when informed, compared with 1.3–5.8% in the silent condition. For Tier 3 MCQs, the corresponding ranges are 91.6–97.5% and 0.3–1.6%. Thus, an informed–silent performance difference reflects both answer validity and the usefulness of textual priors. Conversely, a valid answer can be uninformative: silent Qwen3 and Llama answer “Yes” on all 2,000 bird-presence examples, obtaining 50% accuracy. Informed Qwen3 predicts all five call types on every example, yielding 46.4% macro-F1 but zero exact-set accuracy. This baseline explains why a substantial multi-label score need not indicate sensitivity to the recording. Real audio nevertheless provides a clear advantage on several tasks. Qwen3-Omni bird and amphibian presence accuracy falls from 90.0% and 86.5% with real audio to 50.0% for both with noise; Audio Flamingo Next shows similar reductions, from 88.3% and 80.4% to 50.0% and 49.5%. Other comparisons are less decisive. Conditional regression errors can conceal low answer coverage: Audio Flamingo Next provides numerical estimates for only 1.8% of held-out f0 examples with real audio, while several no-audio conditions provide none. Tier 3 results also depend on output compatibility: only 0.05% of NatureLM-audio v1.0 real-audio MCQ responses are parseable under the single-answer protocol. Its near-zero scores should therefore not be interpreted as a direct ranking of scene-understanding ability. Together, these controls show why task performance, answer validity, and simple constant-answer baselines should be considered jointly when assessing reliance on audio.
