(Dated: October 9, 2026) Replica symmetry breaking (RSB) is the organizing principle of mean-field spin-glass theory, yet whether a continuous Parisi hierarchy can arise in a genuinely short-range system in finite spatial dimension remains unresolved. Here we introduce a disordered model of N -component vector spins on a D-dimensional hypercubic lattice, with random matrix couplings on nearest-neighbor links and random tensor couplings on elementary plaquettes. In the large-N limit, the model supports thermodynamically stable one-step, full, and one-full RSB phases, alongside replica-symmetric paramagnetic and ferromagnetic phases. We find that spatial locality constrains these ordered phases through two distinct infrared mechanisms. ( i) Quenched random anisotropy destroys uniform ferromagnetism for D ≤ 4 through an Imry-Ma mechanism. (ii ) A continuous Parisi hierarchy gives rise to replica Goldstone modes whose gaplessness is protected by a Ward identity. At leading order in 1 /N , these modes behave as a band of free spin waves, producing infrared-divergent fluctuations in D ≤ 2 and a Mermin–Wagner instability of the continuously broken phase. Remarkably, the Parisi hierarchy also acquires a direct real-space interpretation. For two pure states with mutual overlap q, a characteristic length ξ(q) describes how far the differences between their frozen magnetization patterns remain correlated. Throughout a continuous sector of the Parisi hierarchy, ξ(q) is independent of temperature and decreases monotonically with q. The ultrametric organization of pure states is therefore accompanied by an ultrametric hierarchy of real-space cor-relation lengths: states that separate near the root of the Parisi tree differ through long-wavelength frozen patterns, whereas states that separate farther from the root differ only on progressively shorter scales.

I. INTRODUCTION 

One of the central open questions in spin-glass the-ory is whether the Parisi picture of replica symmetry breaking (RSB) describes the low-temperature phase of finite-dimensional spin glasses with strictly short-range interactions [1, 2]. In the fully-connected Sherrington– Kirkpatrick (SK) model [3, 4], this picture is firmly es-tablished. Early studies of the mean-field equations [5–7] exposed the limitations of the replica-symmetric solution, which predicts a negative zero-temperature entropy [3] and is unstable below the de Almeida–Thouless (AT) line [8, 9]. Parisi’s RSB ansatz [10–14] resolves these pathologies by replacing the unstable replica-symmetric saddle by a marginally stable hierarchical solution [15]. Moreover, its physical content is striking: in the spin-glass phase, the equilibrium Gibbs measure decomposes into infinitely many pure states whose mutual overlaps are organized ultrametrically [18–27]. Beyond the SK model, studies of the random-energy model [28–30], p-spin Ising glasses [31–33], spherical spin glasses [34–40], Potts glasses [41, 42], finite-connectivity glasses [43, 44], and hard-sphere structural glasses [45– 48] have revealed a much broader landscape of mean-field phases, with one-step, full, and mixed patterns of RSB. The corresponding nonequilibrium dynamics is equally rich, exhibiting aging, dynamical arrest, and violations of fluctuation–dissipation relations [49–64]. In finite dimensions, however, spatial fluctuations can disrupt the mean-field organization of states, and exact analytical results are scarce. The principal alternative to the Parisi picture is the droplet theory [65–72]. In its canonical form, this picture holds that a zero-field short-range Ising spin glass supports, for each disorder realization, only a single pair of pure states related by a global spin flip, and that its low-energy excitations are compact droplets of linear size L whose characteristic free-energy cost scales as Lθ . The theory further pre-dicts that any nonzero magnetic field destroys spin-glass order, in contrast with the de Almeida–Thouless (AT) transition of mean-field theory [8, 72]. Independently, the self-averaging constraints of Newman and Stein rule out a direct transplantation of the standard SK state picture to short-range systems [73–75], without exclud-ing more general RSB descriptions formulated through metastates [76]. These competing descriptions agree on the presence of frozen order: within a pure state, the lo-cal magnetizations form a spatially random pattern with no conventional long-range order, but with a nonzero Edwards–Anderson order parameter qEA [1]. However, they differ in how many pure states carry this order and how their mutual overlaps are organized. Extensive numerical studies [77–111] and a comple-mentary field-theoretic program [112–128] have clarified several aspects of spin-glass ordering. Simulations of the three-dimensional Edwards–Anderson (EA) model strongly support a continuous transition at finite tem-perature and zero field into a glassy phase with qEA >

0 [77, 78, 80, 83]. Domain-wall and interface calculations place the lower critical dimension near dℓ ≃ 2.5 [79, 129], while the replicated Landau–Ginzburg theory of the over-3lap field has upper critical dimension du = 6 [112, 114]. These results do not, however, determine how the pure states are organized below the transition. That organization remains disputed. Numerical stud-ies of low-temperature overlap distributions and ex-citations have been interpreted in favor of both pic-tures [86, 87, 92, 93, 98–100], and direct tests of ultra-metricity have also reached opposing conclusions [101– 103]. The fate of the AT line is equally unsettled. In a magnetic field, the one-loop renormalization group ad-mits no stable perturbative fixed point below six di-mensions [113]. This obstructs a weak-coupling descrip-tion of the transition but does not establish its absence. Higher-order calculations, alternative expansions, and simulations continue to support different scenarios [109– 111, 123, 125, 126]. This impasse motivates the search for analytically tractable models that retain essential features of short-range systems. Early work emphasized the gauge-theoretic structure of frustration and led to exact identi-ties for local spin-glass models on the Nishimori line [130– 140]. Other approaches introduce controlled limits. Kac models recover mean-field thermodynamics as the inter-action range diverges [141–143]. Bethe-lattice models re-tain finite connectivity but lack a finite-dimensional Eu-clidean geometry [44, 126]. Another route preserves the lattice and local interactions while enlarging the num-ber of degrees of freedom per site. The short-range p-spin model of Campellone, Parisi, and Ranieri, for ex-ample, places M spins at each site. Its site-factorized 

M → ∞ saddle recovers the mean-field p-spin equations, with spatial structure entering through 1 /M fluctua-tions. Their analysis focuses on the 1RSB phase, leaving the lower-temperature continuous-RSB regime unexam-ined [144]. Large-component vector glasses offer a related approach [145–151], although infinite-component solu-tions of scalar and anisotropic random-exchange models tend to remain replica symmetric 1.These approaches motivate two related questions for a microscopic lattice model with strictly local interactions. Can a Parisi hierarchy be thermodynamically stable? And if so, how do pure states that separate at different levels of the hierarchy differ in real space? The latter has so far been approached only numerically through cluster-ing analyses of the EA model that suggested a hierarchy of states generated by correlated spin domains [157]. In this work, we introduce a model that gives analytic access to both questions. It consists of N -component spins on a D-dimensional hypercubic lattice, coupled through random matrices on nearest-neighbor links and random rank-four tensors on elementary plaquettes. At a translation-invariant Parisi saddle point, the off-diagonal overlaps are encoded in a nondecreasing func-tion q(x), where x ∈ [0 , 1] labels the levels of the replica hierarchy at which two replicas separate and q(x) is their overlap. A k-step ansatz produces a q(x) with k finite jumps, one for each level at which replica symmetry is further broken. When the number of breaking levels be-comes infinite, these jumps merge into a continuous rise, and replica symmetry is broken continuously over an in-terval of the hierarchy. We call a phase continuously bro-ken when q(x) is continuous and strictly increasing over a nonzero interval. Representative profiles for one-step (1RSB), full (FRSB), and one-full (1FRSB) glassy phases are shown in Fig. 2. In this sense, 1RSB is discontinuous, since replica symmetry is broken through a single finite jump, whereas both FRSB and 1FRSB contain a contin-uously broken sector.  

> B. Summary of Results

The large-N solution of our local model connects three aspects of glassy order: the hierarchy of equilibrium states, the spatial patterns that distinguish them, and the fluctuations that threaten their stability. The re-sults below refer to the translation-invariant saddles of the N → ∞ theory and, where stated, to their leading 1/N fluctuations. 

• Stable hierarchical glasses with strictly lo-cal interactions. The equilibrium phase diagram [Fig. 1(b)] contains thermodynamically stable 1RSB, FRSB, and 1FRSB glassy phases. FRSB consists of a continuously broken Parisi sector that terminates at the Edwards–Anderson plateau, whereas 1FRSB contains a continuous sector followed by an interme-diate plateau and a final finite jump to the Edwards– Anderson plateau. For example, in D = 3 at JP =

Jl = 1, cooling drives the system from the param-agnet into FRSB and then 1FRSB, while a 1RSB dome appears at strong frustration and low temper-ature. No other Parisi structure is possible since the saddle-point equations together with replicon stabil-ity permit at most one continuously broken sector, followed by at most one jump, in every integer dimen-sion (see Sec. IV E). Plaquette disorder is essential for replica symmetry breaking. With link disorder alone, the glass remains replica symmetric and marginal, whereas any nonzero 

JP destabilizes this solution. At fixed link-disorder strength Jl, the relative strength of plaquette disorder then controls the character of the broken phase: weak plaquette disorder favors continuously broken FRSB and 1FRSB phases, while stronger plaquette disor-der favors one-step order. Accordingly, the phase dia-gram evolves from predominantly FRSB and 1FRSB at weak JP to predominantly 1RSB at strong JP . In the latter regime, the paramagnet can transition di-rectly into 1RSB through a random first-order tran-sition, with a finite jump in the Edwards–Anderson overlap but no latent heat. 5

• Imry–Ma destruction of ferromagnetism for

Replica symmetry breaking does not remove this in-frared obstruction, so magnetized RS, 1RSB, FRSB, and 1FRSB phases are all excluded for D ≤ 4. 

• Coexistence of ferromagnetism and hierarchi-cal glass order. For D > 4, where uniform ferromag-netism is allowed, the replica-symmetric ferromagnet loses replicon stability along a de Almeida–Thouless line while retaining a finite magnetization. The equi-librium continuation is therefore a mixed phase in which ferromagnetic order coexists with a nontrivial Parisi hierarchy, realized as FM–1RSB, FM–FRSB, or FM–1FRSB depending on parameters. Thus, hierar-chical glass order can emerge within an already mag-netized phase, rather than only after ferromagnetic order has disappeared. 

• Gapless replicons and Mermin–Wagner insta-bility in D ≤ 2. Every continuous Parisi hierarchy is accompanied by gapless replicon modes generated by “small permutations” of the Parisi tree. > C. Imry–Ma Instability of Ferromagnetic Order

The absence of ferromagnetic order in D ≤ 4 can be understood as an Imry–Ma instability [158–161]. The essential competition is between ferromagnetic stiffness, which favors a uniform orientation, and the random anisotropic couplings, which favor different orientations in different spatial regions. For a region of size L, the elastic cost of a smooth rotation grows as LD−2, whereas the energy gained by adapting to the local disorder grows as LD/ 2. The latter therefore dominates on sufficiently large scales for D < 4. The marginal case D = 4 requires a logarithmic refinement of this balance [162]. > IV. REPLICA SYMMETRY BREAKING

The stability analysis above shows that the replica-symmetric saddle becomes unstable in part of the phase diagram. We therefore turn to saddle points that break replica symmetry, albeit without breaking translation in-variance. A natural ansatz is to consider k-step replica-symmetry-breaking ( kRSB) matrices, whose entries are organized according to a nested hierarchy of k blocks. The resulting matrices are no longer invariant under the full permutation group Sn, but only under permutations that preserve this nested block structure—replicas may be permuted within the smallest blocks, and blocks may be permuted within larger blocks containing them, at each level of the hierarchy. k-step RSB matrices form a finite-dimensional commutative, associative, unital alge-bra, and therefore provide a natural invariant space in which to seek saddle-point solutions. We begin with the simplest symmetry-broken ansatz, one-step replica symmetry breaking (1RSB), and deter-mine where its solutions are locally stable and thermo-dynamically favored. We then turn to full replica sym-metry breaking (FRSB), in which the discrete hierar-chy becomes the continuous Parisi function q(x) and the pure states organize into an ultrametric tree. A cen-tral result of this work is that, in the present short-range model, this thermodynamic hierarchy acquires a direct spatial interpretation through a corresponding hi-erarchy of correlation lengths. Pure states that sepa-rate near the outer levels of the Parisi tree differ through long-wavelength variations of their frozen magnetization patterns, whereas states that remain together to deeper levels differ only on progressively shorter spatial scales. We also study one-step full replica symmetry breaking (1FRSB), which combines a continuous sector with a single discrete jump. Finally, we compare these com-peting saddle points to construct the full phase diagram and argue that RS, 1RSB, FRSB, and 1FRSB exhaust the replica structures realized by the model. We further show that a continuous Parisi hierarchy leads to Gold-stone modes, whose long-wavelength fluctuations desta-bilize continuous replica symmetry breaking for D ≤ 2through a Mermin–Wagner-type mechanism.

> D. Goldstone Modes and Mermin–Wagner Instability

We now show that the gapless replicon modes asso-ciated with a continuous Parisi profile have a symme-try origin that persists beyond the large-N limit. Al-though the replica-permutation group Sn is discrete, a continuously broken Parisi hierarchy admits permuta-tions whose action on the ordered state becomes arbi-trarily small. These infinitesimal deformations generate Goldstone modes of the continuously broken replica sym-metry [164–168]. At leading order in 1 /N , these modes behave as free spin waves with level-dependent stiffness, producing a Mermin–Wagner-type infrared instability in 

D ≤ 2. We emphasize the absence of O( N ) symmetry in our setup: related Mermin–Wagner-type arguments for short-range vector spin glasses show that conventional XY spin-glass order is absent in D ≤ 2 [169]. Here the relevant soft modes have a different origin: they are Goldstone modes associated with continuous breaking of replica permutation symmetry rather than physical spin-rotation symmetry.

> E. Allowed Parisi Structures

More complicated Parisi profiles could, in principle, contain several continuously breaking sectors or multiple finite jumps. In Appendix. D, we show that within the translation-invariant, large-N theory, the saddle-point equations together with replicon stability restrict the al-lowed structures to RS , 1RSB , FRSB , 1FRSB , (234) together with their magnetized counterparts where al-lowed. This classification holds in every integer spatial dimension and does not rely on a numerical search over higher-step RSB ansatzes. The proof has two main ingredients. First, continu-ous FRSB sectors are highly constrained. On any con-tinuously breaking interval, the local FRSB equations uniquely determine ∆ = ∆( q), x = x(q), (235) so all continuous sectors must follow the same relation between overlap, gap, and Parisi coordinate. In partic-ular, two continuous sectors cannot be separated by a finite plateau. A plateau at fixed overlap q = q∗ would 32 have to leave and rejoin the continuous solution at the same value x(q∗), forcing the plateau to have zero width. To further constrain the continuous solution, it is use-ful to introduce the integrated overlap 

χ(q) =

χ(q) ≤ h(q), (237) with equality on every continuously breaking interval. The key result of Appendix. D is that h(q) can change curvature at most once, from concave to convex. Once 

h(q) enters its convex regime, a concave χ(q) lying be-low it cannot leave and later rejoin the envelope without violating this bound. This strongly restricts the possible arrangement of continuously breaking sectors. Second, finite jumps are also strongly constrained. The jump-matching condition, together with replicon stabil-ity and the curvature property above, implies that any finite jump must be terminal. More specifically, after a jump the profile remains strictly below the envelope 

h(q) at all larger overlaps, so it cannot subsequently enter another continuous sector, which would require 

χ(q) = h(q), or satisfy the matching condition for a sec-ond jump. Thus, once a finite jump occurs, no further hierarchical breaking is possible. It follows that an admissible, replicon-stable profile can contain at most one continuously breaking sector, fol-lowed by at most one finite jump. The only possible structures are therefore RS, 1RSB, FRSB, and 1FRSB, together with their magnetized counterparts where al-lowed. The only lattice-specific ingredient in the proof is a spectral inequality involving the Watson integrals 

wr (D, ∆). We establish this inequality for every in-teger dimension using analytic bounds supplemented by a finite exact-arithmetic verification, as detailed in Appendix. D. The classification is therefore computer-assisted, but does not rely on a numerical search over higher-step RSB ansatzes.

We now compare the RS, 1RSB, FRSB, and 1FRSB saddles, including their magnetized counterparts where allowed. For weak plaquette disorder, D = 5 and JP = 0 .5[Fig. 7(a)], the regions left unresolved by the RS/1RSB analysis are replaced predominantly by FRSB and 1FRSB order. Their magnetized counterparts occupy the lower-frustration region, while the RS ferromagnet sur-vives at small p. A small low-temperature FM+1RSB pocket also remains. Thus continuous RSB dominates much of the glassy regime in this panel, although one-step order can still coexist with ferromagnetism. At intermediate plaquette disorder, D = 5 and JP =1.4 [Fig. 7(b)], one-step order expands substantially. FM+1RSB occupies a broad region at lower p, while the unmagnetized 1RSB glass extends to larger p. Narrow FM+FRSB and FM+1FRSB regions survive near the up-per boundary of the magnetized glass. The magnetized and unmagnetized 1RSB phases join as the condensate vanishes, while at larger frustration the paramagnet tran-sitions directly into the 1RSB glass. For strong plaquette disorder, D = 5 and JP = 3 [Fig. 7(c)], one-step order dominates the ordered region. The unmagnetized 1RSB glass occupies most of the phase diagram, with an FM+1RSB region and a small RS fer-romagnetic phase remaining at low p. No thermodynam-ically selected FRSB or 1FRSB region is resolved in this panel. Finally, for D = 3 and JP = 1 [Fig. 7(d)], ferromag-netic order is absent, so all glassy phases are unmag-netized. Upon cooling, the paramagnet first enters an FRSB phase, followed at lower temperature by 1FRSB. At sufficiently large frustration, the low-temperature phase instead becomes 1RSB, producing the 1RSB dome seen in the figure. Taken together, the D = 5 panels show a clear evolu-tion with increasing plaquette disorder: weak JP favors extended regions of continuous RSB, while stronger JP

progressively favors one-step order. Within the large-N

theory, these results establish that a strictly short-range, finite-dimensional spin glass can support thermodynam-ically stable continuously broken Parisi phases.

> V. CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

In this work, we have constructed a local lattice model of vector spin glasses whose large-N limit supports both magnetized and unmagnetized realizations of one-step, full, and one-full replica-symmetry breaking. Together with the replica-symmetric paramagnetic and ferromag-netic phases, these exhaust the Parisi structures allowed by the saddle-point equations and the replicon-stability conditions in every integer spatial dimension. The proposed lattice model exposes two distinct in-frared mechanisms constraining the structure of phase diagrams, which extend beyond the large-N limit. First, continuous Parisi breaking gives rise to replica Gold-stone modes whose masslessness is protected by a replica Ward identity. At leading order in 1 /N , these modes behave as free spin waves with level-dependent stiff-ness. Their infrared fluctuations therefore diverge in 

D ≤ 2, producing a Mermin–Wagner-type instability of the continuously broken phase. Second, quenched ran-dom anisotropy destabilizes ferromagnetic order in D ≤ 4through an Imry-Ma mechanism, including on replica-symmetry-broken branches. Our central result links the thermodynamic overlap be-tween pure states to the spatial correlation length of their frozen magnetization differences. For two pure states with mutual overlap q, the deficit qEA − q measures the mean-square separation of their frozen spin configura-tions, while the difference-field correlator, conditioned on 

q, describes how these differences are organized in real space. Its characteristic decay length defines a correla-tion length ξ(q), which is independent of temperature, consistent with Parisi–Toulouse scaling, and decreases monotonically with overlap. Pure states that branch near the root of the Parisi tree therefore differ through long-wavelength frozen patterns, whereas states that share a deeper common branch differ only over progressively shorter spatial scales. The ultrametric hierarchy of over-laps is thus inherited by a hierarchy of real-space corre-lation lengths, giving replica-symmetry breaking a direct spatial interpretation. Our analysis opens several directions for refining and extending these results. We highlight four in particular: 

• Finite-N corrections. The framework above pro-vides a starting point for a systematic large-N pro-gram to study the stability of hierarchical glassy or-der in a strictly local spin-glass model. A natural next step is to compute higher-order 1 /N corrections to the saddle-point equations and Hessian eigenvalues, thereby refining both the phase diagram and the hier-archy of spatial correlation lengths. It would also be valuable to determine whether finite-N calculations can access the free-energy cost of imposing different overlaps in spatially separated regions, enabling a di-rect comparison with stiffness-based predictions of the droplet picture. Finally, it is also essential to sup-plement these analytical studies with finite-N Monte Carlo simulations to directly test the predicted phase structure away from the large-N limit. 

• Spatially resolved dynamics . The static phase diagram also motivates a study of dynamical arrest and hierarchical aging. For the 1RSB and 1FRSB ansatzes, candidate dynamical transition lines can be obtained by replacing equilibrium stationarity with respect to the breakpoint m by the upper-plateau marginality condition λ(1)  

> R

(0) = 0, following the ap-proach used for spherical glasses [35, 58]. Deriving the corresponding local Langevin dynamics would then determine whether these marginal lines indeed mark the onset of dynamical arrest and identify the asso-ciated relaxation processes. Furthermore, the aging dynamics of the SK model exhibits an infinite hierar-chy of asymptotically separated timescales [54]. Our results raise the interesting question of how this tem-poral hierarchy relates to the hierarchy of spatial cor-relation lengths ξ(q). Deriving the large-N equations for local Langevin dynamics would allow us to test for dynamical ultrametricity and determine whether dis-tinct aging time scales can be associated with differ-ent equilibrium length scales. Such a correspondence would connect the spatial organization of pure-state differences to the hierarchy of relaxation times, a re-lation not implied by the static Parisi structure alone. 

• Ultrametricity as an RG scale. Perhaps most strikingly, our results suggest that the Parisi hierar-chy itself may provide an organizing principle for spa-tial renormalization, with the Parisi level x acting as an emergent scale coordinate. The monotonic rela-tion x 7 → ξ[q(x)] associates deeper levels of the hi-erarchy with progressively shorter-ranged differences 35 between pure states. This raises a concrete ques-tion: as the observation scale is increased, can spa-tial coarse-graining be described by a progressive loss of resolution among the deeper branches of the Parisi tree, effectively inducing a flow of the ultrametric hi-erarchy? Related ideas have appeared in earlier work by Oppermann and Sherrington, who formulated an RG-like scaling directly in hierarchical space, with the number of breaking steps playing the role of a decimation parameter [170]. Furthermore, similar re-sults have been discovered in forthcoming work [171], where replica-symmetry breaking resolves a dimen-sionally transmuted RG scale into an ordered hier-archy of constituent gaps, with successive gap ratios fixed by the Parisi level. Together, these results sug-gest a deeper connection between ultrametric organi-zation and the emergence of physical scales. A natural starting point in this direction would be to define over-laps from block-averaged frozen magnetizations and follow their distribution as the block size is increased. This would test whether spatial coarse-graining can be represented directly as a flow of the Parisi tree, so that the hierarchy describes not only how pure states are organized, but also how that organization changes with spatial resolution. 

• Quantum Spin Glasses. A natural extension is to ask whether the hierarchical organization of real-space length scales found here survives in short-range quan-tum spin glasses [172–176]. A useful starting point is an O(N ) quantum-rotor generalization of the present model with random link and plaquette interactions. In an imaginary-time replica formulation, the overlap field becomes bilocal in time, Qαβ (x; τ, τ ′), allowing spatial and temporal correlations to be treated within the same framework. One can then ask whether the overlap-dependent hierarchy of spatial length scales, 

ξ(q), survives quantum fluctuations and whether it is accompanied by a corresponding hierarchy of tem-poral scales, namely ultrametric temporal structure .Coupling this short-range quantum spin-glass sector to itinerant fermions would also provide a setting in which to ask whether different levels of the Parisi hi-erarchy produce distinct channels of singular fermion scattering and, collectively, non-Fermi-liquid metallic behavior.
