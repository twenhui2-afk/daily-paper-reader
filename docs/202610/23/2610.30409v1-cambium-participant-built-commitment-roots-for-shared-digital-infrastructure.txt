## Abstract 

Commitment services, such as timestamping, transparency logs and provenance registries, let a party prove that it bound itself to a record without publishing the record. Today an operator runs them, or a blockchain replaces the operator at the cost of network-wide agreement on every write. We present Cambium, a permissioned peer-to-peer service in which participants build each cycle’s com-mitment root together, so that no single party decides which inputs the root includes. Fixed-size committees certify inputs and use a recursive-doubling merge, in which disjoint groups recompute ev-ery intermediate value, to spread root construction across all partic-ipants. A successful cycle costs each node 𝑂 (𝑐 log (𝑛 /𝑐 )) messages for committee size 𝑐 , and each node stores its own 𝑂 (log (𝑛 /𝑐 )) 

proof per cycle, along with the 𝑂 (𝑛 ) membership roster. A Go pro-totype on AWS with 𝑐 = 4, 8–256 nodes and simulated stragglers completes cycles in 12–148 ms and verifies proofs in under 1.5 ms, and demonstrates logarithmic growth. We show that conflicting certificates identify their signers, that a proof binds a signed input to its root, and that two conflicting roots both become final only if at least a third of the nodes are Byzantine, except with probabil-ity exponentially small in the number of committees. We also set out the membership, proof-availability and exclusion questions a community deploying Cambium must answer.

## 1 Introduction 

Many digital interactions need a commitment rather than a ledger: evidence that a party bound itself to a record at a given point, which it cannot later disown and which others can check. A coop-erative registering dataset contributions, a newsroom committing to a photograph’s hash at capture, and an agent committing to an offer before a counterparty acts all need that evidence across organisations. None needs a total order over every action. Nor do the records need to live in one place: local-first software [13] and per-agent ledgers such as TrustChain [19] keep records with their participants, who disclose a record and its proof only when needed. Timestamping and transparency logs provide compact inclu-sion proofs [9, 14], but an operator chooses the root’s contents. Blockchains distribute that control at the cost of network-wide agreement on each write. Cambium sits between the two. All participants build one root per cycle together: no node proposes it or receives every input, and each keeps only its own proof. Cambium does not aim to beat a sin-gle aggregator on latency or cost; it aims to remove the aggregator’s control over which inputs enter the root. Omitting a committee’s inputs needs a quorum of that committee or shows up as a counted hole, while each node sends only 𝑂 (𝑐 log (𝑛 /𝑐 )) messages per cycle (§4). We establish local certificate uniqueness with accountability, root-relative proof soundness and finality: conflicting final roots mean that at least a third of the nodes are Byzantine, except with probability exponentially small in 𝑛 /𝑐 (§5). Liveness is weaker: with-holding below that bound can stall cycles. Equal protocol roles also do not settle who may join, whose inputs are lost when links fail, or who restarts a stalled network (§8). We contribute a commitment-service interface that separates record custody, proof verification and root acceptance, illustrated by a use case (§2); the cycle trie and banyan trie (§3); the cycle protocol and a security analysis of accountability, proof soundness, finality and availability (§4–5); a Go prototype and a crash simula-tion (§6); and the requirements for operating Cambium as shared infrastructure (§8). 

## 2 Overview and Scope 

We write node for a participating computer, 𝑛 for the number of nodes and 𝑐 for the committee size. Every node holds the roster , the list of all members’ identities and public keys, which the genesis root 𝐶𝑅 0 fixes and only signed membership epochs change (§5, §8). In each cycle every node submits one input, a digest, and com-mittees of 𝑐 nodes certify these inputs (§4) before all nodes jointly compute a cycle root over them. Each root commits to its prede-cessor, so the roots a node accepts form a hash chain. We want five properties: conflicting certificates identify their signers (ac-countability); a proof verifies against a root only if its input was certified for that root (proof soundness); an accepted root omits at most a fixed share of the committees, set by 𝐻 max , and counts each omission as a hole (§4); honest nodes never hold conflicting final roots (finality); and a node’s per-cycle cost does not grow with the length of the chain. Section 5 establishes the first two, and the fourth, while fewer than a third of nodes are Byzantine, with high probability; the acceptance rule and cost bounds of §4 give the third and fifth. A node exposes four operations: submit an input for a arXiv:2610.30409v1 [cs.DC] 21 Oct 2026 Ahmed et al. 

future cycle, export a proof, verify a proof against a root, and report the root it accepts for a cycle and whether that root is final. 

Use case. Consider news organisations that commit to pho-tographs’ hashes at capture, so that a fact-checker can later confirm that a photograph existed, unaltered, by a given cycle. The partic-ipants are hundreds to thousands of organisations, each running one node that batches its photographers’ captures. They need com-mitment within about a minute, any number of commitments per cycle, tolerance of members going offline, and rare roster changes. With 𝑐 = 16 Cambium tolerates 5% of members offline, and with 

𝑐 = 32, 10% (Table 2); more slows progress but does not affect safety. A newsroom node submits the root of its local Merkle batch ( POST /commit ); at publication it exports a proof for that cycle with the photograph’s batch-inclusion claim ( GET /path ). A fact-checker checks the committee signatures, recomputes the cycle root ( POST /verifyProof ), and compares it with the final root that most of several randomly chosen members report ( GET /root ). 

What a proof establishes. A proof binds a signed digest to a cycle root and a key, without attesting to the record’s truth or the key’s authority. The cycle does not establish wall-clock time, and guessable records need a salted digest 𝐻 (𝑟 ∥𝑥 ) with fresh 𝑟 . After rollback (§4), proofs against a discarded root no longer verify, and holders must re-submit. A proof against a root outside the chain the verifier trusts provides no evidence about the claim. 

Batching and absence. A complete committee packet shows that a digest is absent from that packet. A claim that a node made no conflicting commitment over a range of cycles additionally needs a namespace, a rule for unique submissions, and the complete batches for those cycles. A hole (§4) establishes neither an empty input nor the absence of an action. There is no globally readable ledger, and each node keeps the 𝑂 (𝑛 ) roster, this storage cost is the main non-logarithmic growth factor in Cambium. 

## 3 Data Structures 3.1 Cycle trie 

A Merkle tree over the committees’ packets yields a root, but if a few nodes compute its upper levels they control it, and if every node computes every level, every node receives every packet. Cambium instead follows recursive-doubling all-reduce [23]: at each level, distinct values halve as their holders double, so after log 2 (𝑛 /𝑐 )

levels every node holds the root. To tolerate faulty nodes, nodes act in groups of 𝑐 , and a node accepts a value only when a quorum of a group vouches for it. The group assignment below ensures that every level-𝑙 value is recomputed by 2 𝑙 disjoint groups, so a captured group causes divergence that later cycles can expose (§4); that each node contacts 𝑂 (𝑐 ) others per level; and that a node’s co-members change across levels, decorrelating the recomputations (Fig. 1). Let 𝑛 = 𝑐 · 2𝐿 with 𝑐 a power of two; other roster sizes need padding or unequal committees, which we do not analyse. Each node has a cycle index 𝑖 (§3.2). ## 3.2 Banyan trie 

After each cycle, a small-domain pseudorandom permutation [21], keyed by the locally accepted root, maps identity indices to next-cycle indices. This re-seats committees; assignment is unknowable until the preceding root exists. Every packet carries its predecessor root, linking successive cycle tries into the banyan trie . Re-seating also samples global health: nodes that built on different predecessor roots can meet in the next cycle’s committees, where a node can join, as a dissenter, a committee that a foreign root’s assignment places it in (§4). 

## 4 Cycle Protocol 

Committee phase. A committee must turn its members’ inputs into one packet signed by 𝜏 = ⌈2𝑐 /3⌉ members, so that its certifi-cate is unique (Lemma 5.1). Members first exchange signed initial packets carrying the cycle number, sender identity, cycle index, predecessor root and a hash of the sender’s input. Two rounds fol-low. In the hash round members agree on which input hashes the packet includes; this fixes the inputs before anyone reveals them, so no member can choose its input after seeing others’. In the data round they reveal the inputs and agree on the committee signed packet (CSP), the leaf of the cycle trie. ## 5 Security 

Model. Within each membership epoch, the roster and public keys are fixed; a signed future epoch can change the roster at a specified cycle. Hashes are collision resistant; signatures are unforgeable and bind cycle, attempt, predecessor root, level, position, phase and value; honest nodes sign at most one value per context. A static adversary controls 𝑓 of the 𝑛 identities, fixed before a cycle’s permutation is known; adaptive corruption is outside the model. ## 6 Implementation and Evaluation 

We implemented a Cambium prototype in Go. We ran one node per AWS ECS Fargate task (2 vCPU, 4 GiB RAM) with 𝑐 = 4, an EFS-backed proof store and the prototype’s straggler simulation enabled. In Table 1, each merge level adds 6.9–7.1 messages per node, near the 2 𝑐 − 1 peer-and-ally contacts, and cycle time grows by 19–41 ms per level. Cycles cannot overlap, because each cycle’s assignment depends on the previous root, so input throughput rises only from 670 to 1,730 inputs per second as 𝑛 grows from 8 to 256; batching (§2) raises commitments per input.

## 7 Related Work 

Aggregators. A single aggregator receives 𝑂 (𝑛 ) inputs while each client sends 𝑂 (1) messages; the aggregator chooses which inputs enter the Merkle root. Keyless signature infrastructure [3] spreads this aggregation over a hierarchy of servers, but a server can drop the inputs below it without accountability, and witness cosign-ing [22] attests to the root without sharing the choice of inputs. Cambium distributes the load and the choice. Both an aggregator and Cambium need a root-trust rule. Secure in-network aggrega-tion [5] also builds a commitment tree inside the network and lets each contributor check its own inclusion, but relies on a trusted querier. 

Timestamping, transparency, consensus. Timestamping uses hash chains or trees [9]; Bitcoin anchoring [24] could supply a wall-clock reference for Cambium’s roots. Certificate Transparency maintains Merkle logs [14]. PBFT and HotStuff totally order re-quests [4, 25]; interactive consistency and asynchronous common subset deliver vectors or sets [17, 20]. Cambium computes an ag-gregate instead. Algorand samples committees from prior random-ness [8]. Cambium uses its own roots for re-seating and leaves proofs with participants. 

Local-first and accountable ledgers. TrustChain keeps a per-agent chain of interactions without global consensus [19], and ConTrib’s per-participant ledger detects fraud through record ex-change [7]. Cambium binds such evidence to a shared root. It could anchor Secure Scuttlebutt’s single-writer log heads [11]. Byzantine-tolerant version control [18] and secure CRDTs [10] replicate state; Cambium retains digests. Local economies may rely on double-spend detection [16]; per-cycle inputs give range evidence when senders disclose complete batches. 

## 8 Operating Shared Infrastructure 

In a shared-data cooperative, contributors retain records and proofs that auditors check against accepted roots. This leaves three opera-tional questions. 

Membership and recovery. 𝐶𝑅 0 fixes the roster, and Sybil re-sistance is delegated to whoever governs it. That suits institutional ledgers run as commons [1] and closed commons that condition replication on contribution [15], but a roster concentrates authority unless it is accountable: admission and removal rules, authenti-cated roster versions that keep old proofs verifiable, and a record of who authorised each restart. The prototype stages signed future membership epochs, each authorised by a threshold certificate from the current epoch, without breaking the root chain; it changes 𝑛 

between powers of two with 𝑐 and 𝐶𝑅 0 fixed. 

Evidence availability. Local retention avoids copying every record everywhere, but applications then need exportable proof bundles and independent retention, and verifiers must distinguish missing evidence from a negative claim. 

Participation and exclusion. Someone pays for slow members: either everyone waits for them, or timeouts turn their slow links into holes, so low-bandwidth or intermittently connected members may repeatedly miss cycles, and rebuild burdens may fall on them. An adversary can also target holes at chosen committees (§4). Be-cause holes make missing participation visible in the committed structure, a deployment can measure inclusion rate and repeated Ahmed et al. 

holes per participant, and respond by tuning timeouts, subsidising operation or delegating hosting. The operational test for a com-munity is whether participants can keep usable evidence, contest exclusion and change operators without losing the ability to verify their records. 

## 9 Conclusion 

Cambium lets participants build a shared commitment root per cycle, with no aggregator or global ordering, while records and proofs stay with their holders. Number of messages per node, total build latency and proof sizes all grow logarithmically with the size of the network. Its final roots agree, with high probability, whenever fewer than a third of the nodes are Byzantine; its liveness needs fewer than that, and untested hole filling is the main engineering gap. Governance, proof availability and inclusion decide whether, and which, community it would best serve.
