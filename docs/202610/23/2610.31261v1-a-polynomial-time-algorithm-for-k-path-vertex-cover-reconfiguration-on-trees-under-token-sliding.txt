# A Polynomial-Time Algorithm for k-Path Vertex Cover Reconfiguration on Trees under Token Sliding 

# Duc A. Hoang VNU University of Science, Vietnam National University Hanoi, Vietnam 

# hoanganhduc@hus.edu.vn 

Abstract 

A k-path vertex cover ( k-PVC) of a graph is a set of vertices meeting every path on k vertices. In Token Sliding, one token occupies each vertex of a cover; a move slides one token along an edge to an unoccupied vertex, preserving the cover property. We study whether two covers of a tree are joined by such moves. The case k = 2 admits a linear-time decision algorithm. For every integer 

k ≥ 2, we give an O(kn 2)-time decision algorithm on trees of order n, with k part of the input. Two covers are reachable precisely when their rigid tokens, which never move, occupy the same set and every component remaining after their deletion has the same token count. The proof transforms configurations of equal size without rigid tokens into a canonical configuration built on properly rooted subtrees, whose paths on k vertices pass through their roots. A rooted-subtree recurrence identifies movable candidate tokens. We also give a direct recursive construction between the supplied endpoints, returning at most 8 n2 3⌈n/ 2⌉ slides in O(n10 3n/ 2) time. The author takes full responsibility for the correctness and content of the paper. 

Keywords: combinatorial reconfiguration, token sliding, k-path vertex cover, tree, polynomial-time algorithm. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recently, combinatorial reconfiguration has attracted attention from both theoretical and practical viewpoints. A reconfiguration problem asks whether one feasible solution of a source problem can be transformed into another by a sequence of small changes, each of which keeps the solution feasible. For an overview of this research area, we refer readers to the surveys of Nishimura [4] and van den Heuvel [6]. For an integer k ≥ 2, a k-path vertex cover (k-PVC ) of a graph G is a set S ⊆ V (G) such that every path on k vertices of G contains a vertex of S. Brešar, Kardoš, Katrenič and Semanišin [9] introduced the minimum k-PVC problem, proved that it is NP -hard for every fixed k ≥ 2, and gave a linear-time algorithm on trees. A 2-PVC is a vertex cover. The complement of a 3-PVC is a dissociation set , that is, a set of vertices inducing a subgraph of maximum degree at most one [3, 9]. Hoang, Suzuki and Yagita [2] initiated the study of reconfiguring k-PVCs. In the Token Sliding model, a token is placed on each vertex of a k-PVC. A move slides one token along an edge to an unoccupied vertex so that the occupied vertices still form a k-PVC. A sequence of such moves is a TS-sequence . The problem k-Path Vertex Cover Reconfiguration ( k-PVCR ) under Token Sliding asks, given a graph G and two k-PVCs S and S′ of G of the same size, whether there is a TS-sequence from S to S′.For k = 2, taking complements identifies the problem with Token Sliding for independent sets: sliding a vertex-cover token from u to v moves an independent-set token from v to u [2]. Token Sliding for independent sets is PSPACE -complete on general graphs [10, 11], and its complexity on restricted graph classes has been studied extensively. We refer readers to Kamiński, Medvedev and Milanič [8] and to Nishimura [4] for examples. On trees, Demaine et al. [5] gave a linear-time decision algorithm. Their characterization uses the tokens that never move and compares token counts after deleting the vertices with those tokens together with their neighbors. For general k, Hoang, Suzuki and Yagita [2] proved that k-PVCR is PSPACE -complete under each of Token Sliding, Token Jumping and Token Addition/Removal even on planar graphs of maximum degree three and bounded bandwidth. They gave polynomial-time algorithms under all three models 1arXiv:2610.31261v1 [cs.DS] 22 Oct 2026 for paths and cycles, and under Token Jumping and Token Addition/Removal for trees. For each fixed 

k ≥ 2, Hoang, Suzuki and Yagita [2] also construct shortest TS-sequences on paths in O(n2) time and TS-sequences on cycles in O(n2) time whenever the target is reachable, where n is the order of the input graph. They left open the complexity under Token Sliding on trees. For caterpillars and k ≥ 4, Hoang [1] gave a polynomial-time algorithm based on the vertices with rigid tokens, which never move, and the token counts after deleting those vertices. For each fixed k ≥ 4, Hoang [1] also constructs a TS-sequence in O(n4) time between equal-size k-PVCs of an n-vertex caterpillar when neither has a rigid token. For 

k = 3, the same paper points out that verifying one condition of its characterization of rigid tokens in polynomial time may require further ideas [1]. As far as we know, the complexity on trees has remained open for every k ≥ 3. 

# 1.1 Our Results 

In this paper, we give a polynomial-time decision algorithm for every k ≥ 2 and an algorithm for finding a TS-sequence for every yes-instance. The first result treats k as part of the input. 

Theorem 1.1. There is an algorithm that, given an integer k ≥ 2, a tree T of order n, and two k-PVCs 

S and S′ of T , decides in O(kn 2) time whether there is a TS-sequence from S to S′.

When k > n , every vertex set is a k-PVC, and the answer is yes exactly when |S| = |S′|. Section 4 proves Theorem 1.1. For k = 3, we apply the algorithm of Theorem 1.1 to the complements to decide in 

O(n2) time whether one dissociation set of a tree can be transformed into another by sliding its tokens along edges, one at a time, so that the occupied vertices always form a dissociation set. The decision algorithm has two main ingredients. • We characterize reachability by rigid tokens and token counts (Theorem 3.10). For a k-PVC S of a tree T , let R(S) be the set of vertices occupied in every k-PVC reachable from S; the tokens on these vertices never move and are called rigid . Two k-PVCs S and S′ of T are reachable from one another if and only if R(S) = R(S′) and they have the same number of tokens in every component of T − R (S). The condition is necessary because no token can enter or leave a component of 

T − R (S), and it is sufficient by the Key Lemma (Lemma 3.9): two k-PVCs of equal size whose tokens are all movable are reachable from one another. The characterization has the form of the result of Hoang [1] for caterpillars and k ≥ 4, and for k = 2 it describes the same relation as that of Demaine et al. [5] (Remark 3.11). The Key Lemma rests on a canonical form: every k-PVC of a given size whose tokens are all movable can be transformed into one canonical k-PVC determined by the tree, its vertex order and the size. It is built one part at a time on the properly rooted subtrees of Brešar et al. [9] (Section 2.3), that is, subtrees U attached to the rest of the tree by one edge at a root u such that 

T [U ] contains a path on k vertices and T [U − u] does not. While u is occupied, the tokens in U − u

do not interact with the rest of the tree, so tokens moved through u can put a prescribed set on U ,after which the construction continues on the smaller tree. For k = 2, Demaine et al. cut off stars formed by a leaf, its neighbor and the other leaves there; already for k = 3, such small pieces do not suffice (Example 3.8), and properly rooted subtrees take their place. • We compute R(S) with Algorithm 1, which follows the idea of the linear-time algorithm of Demaine et al. [5] for independent sets. It starts with all occupied vertices as candidates and repeatedly removes a candidate whose token can move while the other candidates stay occupied; the candidates that remain form R(S) (Theorem 4.9). What is new for general k is the test whether the token of a candidate can move. A slide from a candidate v creates only unoccupied paths through v, each made of v and at most two unoccupied paths starting at other neighbors of v, and the components of the tree minus the candidates can be rearranged independently. Hence, the test needs, for each such component P and its vertex r next to a candidate, only the least possible number of vertices of a longest unoccupied path starting at r. The integer c(P, r ) is the minimum number of tokens in a configuration obtainable from the fully occupied configuration P by slides in T [P ] and legal removals at r. Among the configurations with c(P, r ) tokens obtained in this way, τ (P, r ) is the least number of vertices of a longest unoccupied path starting at r, with value zero when r is occupied. Since the tokens of these components stay movable, the number needed by the test is τ (P, r ) if P

holds exactly c(P, r ) tokens and zero otherwise (Corollary 4.7), where one recurrence on rooted subtrees computes c(P, r ) and τ (P, r ) for every k ≥ 2. 2Our second result outputs a TS-sequence for every yes-instance. For this construction, running times count array, integer, and slide-record operations after reading the input. A binary encoding of k adds 

O(log( k + 1)) for reading that parameter. 

Theorem 1.2. There is an algorithm that, given an integer k ≥ 2, a tree T of order n ≥ 1, and two 

k-PVCs S and S′ of T such that S′ is reachable from S, outputs a TS-sequence from S to S′ with at most 

8n2 3⌈n/ 2⌉ slides in O(n10 3n/ 2) time. 

The construction in Section 5 connects the two supplied endpoints directly. It fixes the same properly rooted side in both endpoints and recursively connects their remaining restrictions once. Within that side, a single transfer loop maintains occupied roots in nonempty branches, and exchanges along one path adjust consecutive prescribed sets. The resulting quadratic local cost and the potential ( √3) p − 1 give the stated bound on the complete returned list. The running-time bound uses exact tree traversals, the rooted recurrence, and linear assembly of the returned lists, as specified in Section 5.4. The exponential bound is an upper bound; we do not claim that this procedure must return exponentially many slides. The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces notation and basic properties of TS-sequences; Section 3 proves the reachability characterization; Section 4 gives the decision algorithm and proves Theorem 1.1; Section 5 proves Theorem 1.2; and Section 6 presents the remaining questions. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

This section introduces the graph notation, token sliding on k-PVCs, and properly rooted subtrees used throughout the paper. We then prove the basic properties of TS-sequences needed for the characterization in Section 3 and the decision algorithm in Section 4, including a property of restrictions to one side of an edge (Lemma 2.12). 

# 2.1 Graph Notation 

We use V (G) and E(G) to denote the sets of vertices and edges of a (simple, undirected) graph G,respectively. The neighborhood of a vertex v in G, denoted by NG(v), is the set {w ∈ V (G) : vw ∈ E(G)}.The closed neighborhood of v in G, denoted by NG[v], is simply the set NG(v) ∪ { v}. For two sets A and 

B, we sometimes use A − B and A + B to indicate A \ B and A ∪ B, respectively, and we write A − x and 

A + x for A − { x} and A + {x}. For X ⊆ V (G), the subgraph of G induced by X is G[X], and G − X is obtained by removing X and all incident edges; we write G − v for G − { v}. A path on k vertices is a sequence of k distinct vertices in which consecutive vertices are adjacent. Throughout the paper, k ≥ 2 is an integer and T is a finite nonempty tree of order n. A fixed linear order of V (T ) resolves all choices between vertices. A set W ⊆ V (T ) is connected if T [W ] is connected. We state the results for subsets of V (T ) because the proofs apply them to subtrees; the reader may take W = V (T ) at first reading. For adjacent vertices x, y ∈ W , the set W xy is the vertex set of the component of T [W ] − x containing y. When W is connected, W xy and W yx partition W , and the edge 

xy is the only edge joining these two sets. The notation follows Demaine et al. [5], who use T xy when 

W = V (T ). A connected set P together with a vertex r ∈ P is a rooted tree (P, r ), in which parents, children, ancestors and descendants are defined as usual. For a connected set P rooted at r ∈ P , the sets P ry , for y ∈ NT [P ](r), are its branches , rooted at the corresponding vertices y. The rooted height of (P, r ) is the number of vertices of a longest path of T [P ] starting at r. The depth of v ∈ P is the number 

dist (v, r ) of edges of the path from r to v, and sub( v) is the vertex set of the subtree of v, that is, v

together with its descendants; the rooted tree is always clear from the context when sub( v) is used. We sometimes identify a subtree with its vertex set. For a finite multiset X of nonnegative integers, σ(X) denotes the sum of its two largest members, with missing members counted as zero. In particular, σ(∅) = 0 and σ({a}) = a. Deleting or decreasing members of X cannot increase σ(X). All lists inside σ below are multisets, so equal entries are counted separately.

# 3.2 The Key Lemma 

Demaine et al. [5] delete a safe degree-1 vertex, that is, a leaf whose neighbor has at most one neighbor of degree more than one, together with its neighbor and the resulting isolated vertices. Our proof deletes a properly rooted subtree instead, and the next example shows that, already for k = 3, the smaller subtrees deleted by Demaine et al. do not suffice for this argument.

# 4 Algorithm for Trees 

By Theorem 3.10, deciding reachability reduces to computing the vertices with rigid tokens. We first define two integers for every rooted subtree and show how they determine the minimum of ε when all tokens are movable. The proof counts the tokens that can be removed at the root, first from the set in which every vertex is occupied and then from an arbitrary k-PVC whose tokens are all movable. We then give Algorithm 1, which removes vertices from a set of candidates for rigid tokens, and prove its correctness. Finally, we analyze its running time and prove Theorem 1.1.

# 4.5 Running Time and Proof of the Decision Theorem 

We describe how to compute every pair ( c(P, r ), τ (P, r )) in one component in O(k|P |) time, and how to check all slide conditions in linear time per round. These bounds give the claimed running time after at 23 most n + 1 rounds. For each vertex set P of a component, each round of Algorithm 1 roots T [P ] temporarily at an arbitrary vertex and makes two passes. The first pass, from the leaves toward the temporary root, computes the pair for every subtree below an edge. The second pass, from the temporary root toward the leaves, computes the pair on the other side of every edge. At a vertex x, a sum stores the incoming values of c, and a histogram with indices 0 , . . . , k − 1 stores the multiplicities of the incoming values of τ .Initializing the histograms takes O(k|P |) time, and accumulating their entries and the sums takes O(|P |)time. The histogram determines D, m, and η in O(k) time. To find m, the positive indices are processed in increasing order while retaining the largest selected value and the number of selected entries. For an index s of positive multiplicity, its first occurrence is permitted exactly when its sum with the previous largest selected value, or zero if none was selected, is at most k − 2. After that first occurrence, all further occurrences of s are permitted exactly when 2 s ≤ k − 2. Thus, each index takes constant time: either all of its occurrences are included, or the longest permitted prefix ends after zero or one occurrence at that index. After the first failed inclusion, no longer prefix is permitted. The scan therefore computes exactly the value m of Equation (5), without listing every occurrence separately for each edge. In the second pass, all pairs entering a vertex x are known when x is processed. The pair sent toward a neighbor y is obtained by subtracting the incoming value of c from y and one occurrence of its value of τ , and then evaluating the recurrence on the remaining histogram. The subtracted entry is restored before processing another neighbor. Each evaluation takes O(k) time. There are two ordered ends of each edge and one full-root evaluation for each vertex, so the two passes take O(k|P |) time in total. They also compute ( c(P, r ), τ (P, r )) for every possible root r ∈ P . Over all components of T [W ] − B, the work is O(kn ), where n = |W |. Finding the components, counting the original tokens in them, and computing the values λB and the sets HP take O(n) additional time. For a candidate v ∈ B, a scan of its neighbors outside B keeps the three largest values λB (w), with multiplicity and their corresponding vertices. For every proposed target z, removing its occurrence from those three, if present, leaves the two largest values among the other neighbors; missing values count as zero. If z is not among those three vertices, removing it does not change the two largest values. Thus, each pair ( v, z ) is tested in constant time after the scan at v. The sum of the vertex degrees in the tree is 2( n − 1), so finding all candidates for removal takes O(n) time per round. For 2 ≤ k ≤ n, one round of Algorithm 1 consequently takes O(kn ) time and uses polynomial space, where n = |W |.

Proof of Theorem 1.1. The decision algorithm computes the two rigid sets and applies the characterization of reachability. For k > n , every subset is a k-PVC, so Lemma 2.4 gives reachability exactly when 

|S| = |S′|. Suppose that 2 ≤ k ≤ n. The tree condition and the two k-PVC conditions can be checked in 

O(n) time. For the latter checks, a pass through each component of T − S and of T − S′ records the two largest downward path lengths at every vertex and rejects if their sum together with that vertex is at least k. The input satisfies the k-PVC conditions exactly when neither unoccupied forest contains a path on k vertices. The decision algorithm runs Algorithm 1 for S and for S′ with W = V (T ). By Theorem 4.9, the returned first sets are RW (S) and RW (S′). If they differ, it rejects. Otherwise, with their common value denoted by R, it accepts exactly when |S ∩ P | = |S′ ∩ P | for the vertex set P of every component of 

T − R. By Theorem 3.10, this condition is equivalent to reachability. Comparing the rigid sets and the component counts takes O(n) time. There are at most n + 1 rounds per input k-PVC, each taking O(kn )time by the preceding analysis. The total running time is therefore O(kn 2). 

# 5 Finding a TS-Sequence 

We first describe short branch operations and a normalization procedure, then give two procedures for choosing a k-PVC with a prescribed property at one vertex. We then connect the two supplied endpoints by recursion on smaller subtrees. Both endpoints first receive the same prescribed restriction on one side of an edge, and a single recursive call connects their remaining restrictions. Finally, we bound the length of the output and the running time in terms of the order of the tree. Throughout this section, all choices among vertices follow the fixed order from the preliminaries. 24 5.1 Elementary Procedures and Prescribed Targets 

Under the hypotheses of Lemma 2.4, the following explicit routing procedure joins two sets of the same cardinality on a tree with p vertices. This leaf-pruning procedure appears in Kornhauser [12]. The procedure fixes a leaf at a time, always choosing the least remaining leaf. If its occupation differs from the target, a breadth-first search finds a nearest vertex with the desired occupation. Every intermediate vertex of the joining path has the opposite occupation, so successive slides along the path give the leaf its desired occupation. The leaf is then fixed, and the procedure continues on the remaining tree. The token counts in the remaining tree agree with the target counts, so the search always succeeds when the leaf differs from its target. At a stage with j vertices, at most j − 1 slides are needed. Thus, the procedure uses at most p(p − 1) /2 slides. It applies when the tree contains no path on k vertices or when fewer than 

k vertices are unoccupied. The same search occupies the root of a nonempty branch of a properly rooted subtree, or makes the root unoccupied when that branch is not full. In the first case, a nearest token moves toward the root. In the second case, successive tokens move toward a nearest unoccupied vertex, until the root is unoccupied. These moves are legal while the root of the properly rooted subtree is occupied, since the branch contains no path on k vertices. When the root u of the properly rooted subtree itself is unoccupied, a nearest token inside that subtree can move to u by Lemma 3.3. Indeed, at each slide, a path through the newly unoccupied vertex either meets the token at its new position or stays inside one branch, where no path on k vertices exists. This procedure leaves the entire exterior fixed. When two branch configurations differ only by exchanging one occupied vertex with one unoccupied vertex, we route the tokens along the path joining those vertices. For a connected vertex set B and vertices s, t ∈ B, the procedure Exchange (B, S, s, t ) returns the empty list if s = t. Otherwise, it scans the unique path from t to s. Starting at t, it moves each token encountered along that path back to the position vacated by the preceding token, with the first token moving to t. The scan continues until it has moved the token initially at s. The next lemma gives the conditions under which these slides are legal in the ambient tree.

We analyze the algorithm with exact tree computations and explicit lists of slides. Let n = |V (T )| and let L be the length of its actual output, before any shortening. Vertex sets are Boolean arrays. We count unit-cost array accesses, arithmetic operations on integer labels and counts, and reads and writes of slide records. Its length is at most 8 n2 3⌈n/ 2⌉ by Lemma 5.8. When k ≤ n, Equation (25) gives O(n8(L + 1)) operations. Since 3⌈n/ 2⌉ ≤ √3 3 n/ 2, substituting the length bound gives O(n10 3n/ 2) operations. For pairs not known to be valid and reachable, the initial cover checks and the decision algorithm reject precisely the other pairs; their time is also within the stated bound. 

# 6 Concluding Remarks 

In this paper, we have shown that Token Sliding for k-PVCs of trees of order n can be decided in O(kn 2)time for every integer k ≥ 2, and we have given a direct recursive construction with at most 8 n2 3⌈n/ 2⌉

slides and running time O(n10 3n/ 2). The decision algorithm computes the vertices with rigid tokens and uses the token counts in the components remaining after their deletion. It remains open whether TS-sequences of polynomial length between reachable k-PVCs of trees can be found in polynomial time for every fixed k ≥ 3. For k = 2, Demaine et al. [5] proved that a TS-sequence of length O(n2) can be found in O(n2) time whenever the target is reachable, and they gave paths on which every such sequence has length Ω( n2). Their result transfers from independent sets to 2-PVCs by taking complements. The bound proved here for the direct pair construction does not recover that quadratic guarantee. It remains open whether 

O(n2) slides suffice for every fixed k ≥ 3. Another question is whether the O(kn 2) decision bound can be improved to linear time for each fixed k ≥ 3, as is known for k = 2.
