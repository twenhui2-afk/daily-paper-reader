Abstract — Real-time whole-body humanoid teleoperation re-mains challenging due to the computational latency introduced by optimization-based retargeting pipelines and future-frame conditioning. Existing motion tracking policies typically require dense reference targets including full joint positions, body-part transformations, and even future frames, which tightly couples the controller to expensive upstream modules. In this work, we present HuMiT , a whole-body teleoperation system built on a unified tracking policy that requires only a compact reference target, consisting of root height, root velocity, and the SE(3) poses of four sparse keypoints at the current frame, yet achieves competitive tracking fidelity compared to methods relying on more diverse reference states including complete joint positions, dense body-part transformations, and even future frames. Experiments show that our policy achieves a communication-to-inference latency of ∼20 ms measured between headset streaming and policy output and a video-based optical flow latency of 65 ms (median) across different motion types. We validate the system on a Unitree G1 humanoid, showcasing split-second reactive responses such as catching a tossed ball, as well as coordinated loco-manipulation capabilities. Project page: https://xiaohu-art.github.io/Track/ .

I. INTRODUCTION Humanoid robots hold unique promise for operating in human-centric environments due to their morphology that naturally matches the workspaces and tools designed for people. Teleoperation is a practical paradigm for human-in-the-loop robot control. It provides a scalable mechanism for collecting diverse demonstration data to train autonomous policies, and also allows human operators to intervene in high-risk scenarios or situations where autonomous systems are likely to fail. However, building an effective whole-body teleoperation system remains challenging. A primary issue is large tele-operation latency , which arises from two sources. First, optimization-based retargeting [1] introduces substantial per-frame delay and can suffer from numerical instability. Sec-ond, existing learning-based tracking policies rely heavily on full reference states as inputs, including complete joint positions, body-part transformations, and their velocities [2], 

Fig. 1: Real-world teleoperation with HuMiT. Moreover, many of these methods condition the policy on future reference frames to enable anticipatory control [4], [5], which further exacerbates the latency problem. A secondary challenge is limited whole-body coordination ,as many approaches decouple upper- and lower-body control [6]–[8], which is particularly restrictive for dynamic tasks. In addition, hardware-centric solutions based on exoskeletons [9]–[11] or marker-based motion capture [12], [13] further constrain deployment flexibility. In this work, we investigate a simple but consequential question: does high-fidelity whole-body motion tracking truly require a full reference state at deployment? We find that a compact reference target (CRT) , consisting only of a root height, root linear and angular velocity commands, and the SE(3) poses of four sparse keypoints (bilateral wrists and ankles) at the current frame, is sufficient as the deployment-time input of a unified whole-body tracking pol-icy that achieves competitive tracking performance compared to methods relying on full reference states (i.e., complete joint positions, dense body-part states, their velocities, and future frames). This compact reference design dramatically reduces the information that the upstream sensing module must provide, and it removes the retargeting pipeline from the online control loop entirely, resulting in significantly lower end-to-end latency. We present HuMiT , a low-latency whole-body tele-operation system for humanoid robots. HuMiT employs lightweight human motion sensing to produce compact refer-ence targets, which are then tracked by a whole-body policy trained in parallel simulation. As shown in Fig. 1, HuMiT enables a wide range of dynamic whole-body skills that are difficult to achieve with high-latency or decoupled systems. Our contributions are as follows:

Compact reference suffices at deployment. Through cross-method comparison and ablations under identical conditions, we show that a compact reference target matches full-reference policies.

A. Learning-Based Humanoid Whole-Body Control 

Decoupled vs. unified control. Partitioning whole-body control into separate upper- and lower-body policies [6]– [8] simplifies learning but fundamentally limits movements that couple limb motion and base stability, whereas unified policies integrate all degrees of freedom, enabling true loco-manipulation at the cost of increased training complexity. 

Reference target design. Among unified tracking con-trollers, prior methods differ in what reference information the policy observes. BeyondMimic [2] conditions the policy on joint positions and joint velocities as reference targets. BFM [3] augments this with body-part local positions, pro-viding denser spatial supervision. SONIC [4] and OmniClone [5] further extend the reference to include future frames for anticipatory control. Despite these differences in reference richness, all of these methods share the assumption that dense reference targets are necessary for high-fidelity tracking, an assumption that remains largely unexamined. 

B. Humanoid Teleoperation Systems 

Existing teleoperation systems [14] differ mainly in their sensing modality and in the resulting end-to-end latency. 

Hardware-centric sensing. Exoskeleton-based systems [9]–[11] capture the operator’s upper body while the lower body is driven by joysticks or pedals, so sensing leads to decoupled control. Optical marker-based motion capture suits [12] are accurate but expensive, require extensive calibration, and restrict the operator to a constrained workspace. 

Vision-based pose estimation. Camera-based methods estimate human body pose using models such as SMPL [6], [15]–[17], offering greater flexibility and lower hardware cost, but the computational overhead of pose estimation followed by optimization-based retargeting makes the high-frequency, low-latency throughput required for responsive teleoperation hard to reach. HumanPlus [18] and OmniH2O [19] adopt this approach and inherit its latency limitations. 

Direct extremity control. A concurrent work, Extrem-Control [20], operates directly on the SE(3) poses of six links (pelvis, torso, hands, and feet) and incorporates velocity feedforward into the PD controller, reporting latency as low as 50 ms with optical motion capture and 60 ms with a VR-based setup. Its deployable policy, however, is distilled from a privileged teacher that observes up to 32 future reference steps, and its reference is anchored by the operator’s global pelvis and torso poses. In contrast, HuMiT is trained in a single stage without distillation or any future-frame super-vision, and replaces global pose anchoring with root height and velocity commands. 

C. Motion Retargeting 

Motion retargeting bridges the embodiment gap between the human operator and the target robot by converting captured human motion into robot-executable commands. Classical approaches solve per-frame inverse kinematics (IK) or nonlinear optimization problems [1], [21] to map human joint positions onto the robot’s kinematic structure. While geometrically accurate, they introduce two practical draw-backs for teleoperation: (i) the per-frame optimization incurs non-negligible latency that accumulates in the control loop, and (ii) the numerical solvers are prone to instability (e.g., Fig. 2: Method overview. In HuMiT, retargeting is performed offline only during training data preparation. At deployment, the tracking policy directly consumes scaled human keypoints as reference tar-gets, completely removing retargeting from the online loop and eliminating its associated latency and instability. A. System Overview 

Fig. 2 illustrates the HuMiT pipeline. Human motion is captured by a VR headset and converted into a compact reference target (CRT) gcpt through a one-time scale calibra-tion. The CRT is then consumed by a reinforcement-learning-based whole-body tracking policy π that outputs joint-space commands for the Unitree G1 humanoid ( N = 29 DoF). The central design choice in HuMiT is that π observes only the CRT, consisting of root height hrt , root linear velocity 

vrt , root angular velocity ωrt , and the root-relative SE(3) poses 

(Rrt, j, prt, j) of a sparse keypoint subset j ∈ Kc ⊂ K (bilateral wrists and ankles, |Kc| = 4), without requiring joint-level references qrt and ˙qrt , the states of the remaining body parts, or any future-frame information. This reduces the reference generation stage in Section III to two lightweight components: (1) a motion sensing module that captures the operator’s body keypoints via VR head-set (PICO 4 Ultra) with motion trackers, and (2) a scale calibration module that maps human keypoint positions to the robot’s body positions in closed form, in place of an optimization-based retargeting loop. 

B. From Human Motion to Compact Reference Target 

Motion sensing. Our sensing front-end is entirely consumer-grade as in Fig. 2: a PICO 4 Ultra VR headset, two handheld PICO controllers, and two PICO Motion Trackers strapped to the operator’s lower legs. From this setup, the PICO body-tracking runtime infers a full-body skeleton of 24 points with 6 DoF each at approximately 80 Hz and streams it to the control host over Wi-Fi. From this stream we extract the SE(3) poses of the four keypoints in Kc ⊂ K. Putting on the entire wearable set takes less than a minute, and the system imposes no workspace constraint beyond Wi-Fi coverage, in contrast to exoskeleton or optical motion-capture pipelines. 

Scale calibration. To bridge the morphological difference between the human operator and the robot, we perform a one-time calibration following GMR [22]. At the start of each teleoperation session, the operator assumes a reference pose (e.g., T-pose). We compute per-limb scale factors α j by comparing the operator’s limb segment lengths to those of the humanoid robot, and apply these factors to map incoming human keypoint positions into the robot’s body frame, while keypoint orientations require no morphological rescaling and are passed through unchanged: 

prt, j = α j · pht, j, Rrt, j = Rht, j, j ∈ Kc.The root height hrt is obtained as the z-component of the scaled root position, and the root velocity is computed via finite differences of the root transformation. All mappings are closed-form and add negligible latency ( <1 ms). 

C. Whole-Body Motion Tracking Policy 

We train a unified whole-body tracking policy π via reinforcement learning in simulation. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) offline data preparation, in which human motion clips are retargeted to the robot via IK to produce full reference states g f ull , and (2) online RL training, in which 

π learns to track the CRT using PPO with an asymmetric actor–critic architecture. The full reference g f ull is used only to (i) initialize feasible simulator states, (ii) compute dense reward signals, and (iii) serve as an additional critic input. It is worth noting that the actor π itself never observes g f ull .

Training data. We train on motion sequences from AMASS [23], LAFAN1 [24], [25], and BONES-SEED [4] after filtering out physically infeasible clips. Every clip is further augmented with its sagittal-plane mirror, which swaps the left and right joints and reflects the root trajectory about the vertical plane through the initial heading. For each human clip, we solve an optimization-based inverse kinematics problem to obtain a robot-frame reference trajectory

Conditioning a policy on k future reference frames delays at least k ∆t of reference buffering ( ≥200 ms for 10 frames at 20 ms spacing). 

Internal timestamped measurement. We record times-tamps from the arrival of a skeleton packet streamed by the headset to the policy’s action, covering wireless trans-mission, CRT assembly, and policy inference. This yields a communication-to-inference latency of ∼20 ms, excluding the sensing latency and the robot’s actuation response. 

External optical-flow estimation. To capture the full operator-to-robot delay including physical execution, we record the operator and the robot in the same camera frame, compute dense optical flow between consecutive frames within an operator region and a robot region, and reduce each region to one standardized motion signal by averaging its flow along the vertical direction. We then detect prominent peaks in the operator signal and match each to the robot extremum of the same sign, and the per-peak latency is the time offset of the matched pair (Fig. 3). We record three motion types at 200 fps (5 ms temporal resolution): punching, squatting, and waving, yielding median (mean) latencies of 78 (91) ms with n=4 matched pairs, 60 (63) ms with n=5, and 85 (91) ms with n=7, respectively. The pooled median (mean) over all 16 pairs is 65 (83) ms.

C. Real-World Teleoperation ( Q3 )

We deploy HuMiT on a Unitree G1 to evaluate three capabilities that are directly enabled by its low-latency, unified-policy design: accurate real-time tracking, fast reac-tive responses, and coordinated loco-manipulation. Snapshots of all real-world demonstrations are shown in Fig. 1, and Table IV reports the outcome of ten trials per task together with the dominant failure mode we observed. 

Real-time tracking. We first stream live operator motion to the robot and evaluate qualitatively how closely the G1 mirrors the human (Fig. 1a). The robot reproduces a wide range of whole-body poses driven by the compact reference target alone. 

Reactive catching. To validate the low-latency advantage, we conduct a task as shown in Fig. 1b: A ball is thrown toward the robot, which mirrors the operator’s motion in real time and catches the ball with its glove. This task is inherently difficult for high-latency systems, since the robot has to react within the flight time of the ball. HuMiT catches the ball in 2 of 10 trials, whereas SONIC-LL and TWIST2 never succeed, and its remaining failures come from the mismatch between the operator’s and the robot’s arm workspace rather than from a delayed response. 

Coordinated loco-manipulation. We further evaluate four loco-manipulation tasks that require tight coordination be-tween locomotion and manipulation (Fig. 1c–f). In all of them, the unified policy maintains balance during locomotion without degrading manipulation accuracy. SONIC-LL and TWIST2 remain stable on these four tasks, but their higher latency requires the operator to pause and correct after each motion, which makes teleoperated data collection substan-tially slower. These behaviors are difficult to obtain with decoupled upper-/lower-body controllers, where the lower body is typically driven by joystick or pedal commands that cannot be tightly synchronized with arm motions. VI. CONCLUSIONS AND LIMITATIONS We presented HuMiT, showing that a compact reference target is sufficient as the deployment-time input of a whole-body tracking policy with competitive fidelity, achieving a communication-to-inference latency of ∼20 ms and an externally measured end-to-end latency of 65 ms (median). Real-world experiments demonstrate that this latency enables reactive tasks such as catching a tossed ball, as well as coordinated loco-manipulation, validating the approach. HuMiT also has several limitations. First, the training data is dominated by upright locomotion and upper-body gestures, so motions involving ground contact such as kneel-ing or crawling are underrepresented, leading to degraded tracking accuracy or failure on these behaviors. Second, current hardware and teleoperation policy design do not support high-payload loco-manipulation (e.g., carrying heavy objects while walking), as the policy has not been trained under such loading conditions.
