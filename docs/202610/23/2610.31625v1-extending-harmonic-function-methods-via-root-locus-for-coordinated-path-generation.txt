# Extending Harmonic Function Methods via Root Locus for Coordinated Path Generation

Abstract 

We consider the offline generation of coordinated paths for N interchangeable robots and N goals in planar environments with polygonal obstacles. After a conformal map to a circular domain, starts and goals become the poles and zeros of a rational function R, and the paths are the branches of its Generalized Root Locus (GRL), i.e. the streamlines of the logarithmic potential − log R. We show that, for every phase outside a finite set of critical phases and a set of boundary phases, the induced gradient motion drives each robot to a distinct goal along pairwise disjoint paths that never reach the obstacles; the robot– goal assignment is produced by the construction, and with the gain used as a clock all robots move as the closed-loop roots of the GRL and arrive together at a prescribed time. Obstacles become streamlines through successive Milne–Thomson reflections, and for a finite number of reflections a finite test certifies safety for the potential that is actually computed. Reciprocal root-locus planning is recovered as a special case. The method is illustrated on two polygonal workspaces, 100 random instances compared with independent harmonic planning and artificial potentials, and a scene with ten robots and ten obstacles. 

Keywords: Generalized root locus, Harmonic functions, Conformal mapping, Obstacle avoidance, Multi-robot systems, Path planning 

1. Introduction 

Coordinated motion of several robots in a cluttered environment is usually organized in two layers: an 

offline layer that builds feasible global paths, and an online layer that tracks them and handles finite size, actuation limits and close interactions. This paper is about the first layer. We consider N interchangeable robots and N goals in a planar workspace with polygonal obstacles, and look for smooth paths that are collision-free by construction; assigning robots to goals is part of the problem. Potential-based methods encode the whole problem in a scalar field. The artificial potential field (APF) of Khatib [1] may have local minima; harmonic function methods (HFM) [2] remove them, since solutions of the Laplace equation have no interior extrema. Navigation functions [3] also exclude spurious minima, but need a map onto a sphere world, hard to build for polygonal scenes. For several robots, electrostatic analogies have been used without obstacles [4], and root-locus (RL) constructions have produced collision-free formations [5] and single-robot paths among diffusive hazards [6]. The reciprocal root locus (RRL) [7, 8] handles polygonal workspaces through a conformal map, but enforces obstacle avoidance by heuristic corrections, without a proof of non-penetration. The Generalized Root Locus (GRL) bridges these lines of work. If starts and goals are the poles and zeros of a rational function R, the GRL branches are the streamlines of the logarithmic potential − log R

[9]: integral curves of a harmonic field, as in HFM, with the branch structure of the root locus. The

2. Problem formulation and GRL potential 

2.1. Workspace and canonical domain 

Let P ⊂ C be a bounded workspace whose boundary is the union of Nobs pairwise disjoint simple closed polygons Γ 0, . . . , ΓNobs −1, with Γ 0 the outer one. At the planning level the robots are interchangeable point agents. N initial positions and N goals are given in P . For robots of radius ϱ, a margin is introduced by inflating every obstacle and shrinking the outer boundary by ϱ before planning. This handles the clearance between robots and environment only. Mutual safety is not implied by disjoint paths and requires the distance between distinct paths to exceed 2 ϱ; this condition does not depend on time, is checked after planning and can be included in the choice of the phase. Following [7, 8], the workspace is mapped onto a circular domain by a conformal map 

z = f (s), f : P → D, (1) where D is the disk bounded by a circle C0 minus Nobs − 1 closed disks bounded by circles Ci = {| z − ci| =

ri}, with f (Γ i) = Ci. Schwarz–Christoffel formulas are semi-explicit only for simply connected polygons [10, 11]; we compute f and f −1 with the toolbox PlgCirMap [12], based on Nasser’s fast Koebe iteration [13], with cost O(N 2obs NΓ +Nobs NΓ log NΓ) for NΓ nodes per boundary component. The map is normalized by an interior point α ∈ P sent to the origin, taken in a central free region (Fig. 1). All the analysis is carried out in D, and the paths are mapped back by f −1. The images in D of starts and goals are still denoted by p1, . . . , p N and ζ1, . . . , ζ N . The planning problem in D is to find N smooth, pairwise disjoint curves in D joining the poles to the zeros, with a pairing produced by the construction itself.

3. Break points and critical phases 

Distinct branches of the same family can meet only at break points, usually defined by a multiplicity condition [5]; here they have a direct interpretation in terms of the potential. 

Definition 1 (Break point) . A point b / ∈ Σ is a break point if, for some θ and k > 0, it is a root of multiplicity at least two of 1 + ke jθ R(z) = 0. 

Proposition 1 (Critical characterization) . For b / ∈ Σ the conditions ∇Φ( b) = 0 , ∇Ψ( b) = 0 , Ω′(b) = 0 

and R′(b) = 0 are equivalent. Every break point satisfies them, and every point satisfying them is a break point of the family θ = π − arg R(b).Proof. The first three conditions are equivalent by (4). Differentiating (3) gives Ω ′ = −R′/R , and R(b)̸ =0, so Ω ′(b) = 0 if and only if R′(b) = 0. If b is a multiple root of 1 + ke jθ R, differentiating at b gives 

ke jθ R′(b) = 0, hence R′(b) = 0. Conversely, if R′(b) = 0, take θ = π − arg R(b) and k = 1 /|R(b)|: then 1 + ke jθ R(b) = 0 and the derivative vanishes too, so b is a multiple root. Break points are therefore the critical points of the complex potential: the stagnation points of the flow. In the obstacle-free case their number is bounded, as shown in [5]; we restate the bound in the present notation. 

Proposition 2 (Break-point bound) . For R as in (2) , the number of break points is at most 2N − 2.Proof. Let q := n′d − d′n, so that R′ = q/d 2. By Proposition 1, every break point is a zero of q outside Σ. For N = 1, q = ζ1 − p1̸ = 0 and there is no break point. For N ≥ 2, n′d and d′n have degree 2 N − 1and the same leading coefficient N , because n and d are monic. Hence deg q ≤ 2N − 2. With the image singularities of Section 5 the bound no longer applies, and the number of critical points in the domain is verified by the argument principle (Section 6). A phase is critical if its family contains a break point. By Proposition 1, the set of critical phases associated with a domain D is Θb := {π − arg R(b) (mod 2 π) : b ∈ D \ Σ, R ′(b) = 0 }. (8) Each critical point gives one phase, and the critical points are finitely many in D when R is meromorphic near D, so Θ b is finite. 7. Numerical results 

7.1. Reference examples with polygonal obstacles 

The Flagship example has N = 5 robots and Nobs = 5 boundary components, the outer one included (Fig. 1); the map is normalized with α = 2 j. The Warehouse example has N = 3 robots and Nobs = 7, with shelves as obstacles; here α = 6 .5 + 3 .5j. In both cases the obstacle-compatible potential is built by reflections up to depth m (Figs. 4(a) and 5(a)). Table 1 reports the certificate for m = 1 , 2, 3. In both examples the number of critical points found equals the one given by the argument principle, so Θ b is complete, and the winding of R is zero on every circle, as used in Proposition 3. The certified fraction of the phase circle grows with m, consistently with item 1 of the proposition and with the decrease of the residual. The phase θ = 0 used in Figs. 4 and 5 is certified for m ≥ 2 in the Flagship example and for every m in the Warehouse example, so the plotted paths ( m = 3) are covered by Theorem 1. The rejected case is instructive. For the Flagship with m = 1, the certificate fails for θ = 0 by 2 · 10 −3 rad, and the traced branch does enter an obstacle, by 0 .04 in the units of D. For m = 2 and 

m = 3 the same branch is certified and keeps a clearance of 0 .004 and 0 .025. Here the rejection is not a false alarm: the test detects a configuration in which the truncation is too coarse.

8. Conclusions 

We have shown that the GRL of a rational function encoding starts and goals, read through its logarithmic potential after a conformal map, provides certified coordinated paths for N interchangeable robots in polygonal environments: for every admissible phase the robots reach distinct goals along disjoint paths, with an assignment produced by the construction and a time law given by the gain. For potentials 14 computed with finitely many reflections, a finite test certifies obstacle safety, and the RRL is recovered as a special case. The method is offline and geometric: tracking, dynamics and online replanning are left to the execution layer, robot size enters through obstacle inflation, and optimality of the paths is not claimed. The main open directions are faster series-based obstacle potentials [19, 21] for larger scenes, a sharper certificate exploiting the cancellations between image zeros and poles, a systematic study of the dependence on the phase and on the normalization α, and the coupling with an online layer.
