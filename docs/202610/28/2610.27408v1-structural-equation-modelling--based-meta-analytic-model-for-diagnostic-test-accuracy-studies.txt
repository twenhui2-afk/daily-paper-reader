ABSTRACT 

Bivariate random-effects models are commonly used to jointly synthesize sensitivity and specificity in diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) meta-analysis. However, existing approaches may be sensitive to distributional assumptions and may produce biased estimates of accuracy and between-study heterogeneity with sparse meta-analytic data. We proposed a novel bivariate structural equation modelling (BSEM) approach for DTA meta-analysis and evaluated its performance relative to the bivariate linear mixed model (BLMM) and bivariate generalized linear mixed model (BGLMM). We conducted extensive simulations under varying study, sample-size, accuracy, and heterogeneity conditions. We assessed model performance using bias, root mean squared error (RMSE), confidence interval width, empirical 95% coverage probability (CP), and convergence. We generated study-specific random effects from bivariate normal, t, and Laplace distributions to assess robustness to distributional misspecification. We also applied the models to two empirical DTA datasets concerning interviewer-administered questionnaires for smoking and ultrasonography for deep venous thrombosis. The BSEM generally produced smaller bias and RMSE and nominal-level CP for pooled sensitivity and specificity than the BLMM, while the BGLMM generally yielded results between the two approaches. The BLMM showed stable convergence but tended to underestimate between-study heterogeneity. We observed similar performance patterns under the three random-effects distributions. In the empirical applications, differences between model estimates were generally consistent with patterns observed in the simulations. Overall, the proposed BSEM provided a flexible alternative approach for jointly modelling sensitivity and specificity in DTA meta-analysis, with performance that was generally favourable relative to the BLMM and comparable to the BGLMM across the simulated and empirical settings considered.

Sensitivity and specificity 

1 Introduction 

Diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) studies evaluate how well a medical test discriminates between diseased and non-diseased individuals or how well a diagnostic or screening tool discriminates between a target condition and a non-target condition. 1 The fundamental characteristics of DTA are sensitivity and specificity, defined as the probability of a positive test result among diseased individuals and the probability of a negative test result among non-diseased individuals, where a gold or reference standard identifies the true target condition. 1;2 Researchers often summarize these accuracy measures jointly using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which describe the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity across different decision thresholds. 3–5 DTA studies play a central role in clinical decision-making, guideline development, and health technology assessment, as they provide evidence on the usefulness of diagnostic tools. 6

Meta-analysis has become an essential tool for synthesizing evidence from primary DTA studies, as individual DTA studies are often limited by small sample sizes, study-specific biases, and variability in design or study-participant populations. 2;7 By combining results, meta-analysis increases statistical power and allows more precise estimation of test performance. Importantly, it also enables the investigation of heterogeneity, a defining feature of DTA data due to differences in thresholds, populations, and study quality. 8

Standard approaches to DTA meta-analysis 9;10 explicitly account for the correlation between sensitivity and specificity and variability within and between studies. A widely used approach is the bivariate linear mixed model (BLMM) proposed by Reitsma et al. (2005), 9 which describes logit-transformed sensitivity and specificity while allowing heterogeneity and correlation between studies. Closely related is the hierarchical model of Chu and Cole (2006), 10 which formulates the problem using a bivariate generalized linear mixed modelling (BGLMM) approach. These models have become standard in practice 11 and are implemented in major statistical software. Despite their widespread use, standard models for DTA meta-analysis have several limitations. First, they typically assume the availability of a perfect reference standard, one that can observe true disease status without error. In real-world settings, this assumption is unrealistic, and misclassification in the reference test can bias results. 12;13 Second, standard models rely on relatively rigid distributional assumptions, 14 such as bivariate normality of random effects, and may not easily accommodate complex dependence structures, such as multiple correlated tests 15 or conditional dependence beyond that induced by random effects. 16–19 Finally, extensions to handle missing data, verification bias, 20;21 or multiple tests 18 can become cumbersome within the traditional framework for DTA meta-analysis. These limitations motivate structural equation modelling (SEM) as a flexible, unifying alternative. SEM provides a general framework for modelling latent variables, measurement error, and hierarchical structures within a single system, and its utility has been demonstrated in the context of meta-analysis of intervention studies using frequentist and Bayesian approaches. 22–29 In the context of DTA meta-analysis, SEM would also allow explicit modelling of latent disease status when no gold standard is available, incorporation of measurement error in both index and reference tests, and flexible representation of between-study heterogeneity and correlation structures, among others. Moreover, SEM naturally connects to latent class models, enabling principled extensions to settings with imperfect reference standards. The primary aim of this manuscript is to introduce, for the first time to the best of the author’s knowledge, a bivariate structural equation modelling (BSEM) approach for meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) studies and to evaluate its performance relative to two established approaches, the BLMM 9 and the BGLMM. 10 The proposed BSEM jointly models sensitivity and specificity while accounting for between-study variation and their covariance. We evaluate its performance through an extensive simulation study across different levels of diagnostic accuracy, numbers of studies, within-study sample sizes, and random-effects distributions, and through applications to two empirical DTA datasets. The manuscript also provides an implementation of the proposed approach using the R30 statistical computing language and the OpenMx 31–34 and metaSEM 35 packages to facilitate its application by researchers and practitioners. Finally, potential extensions of the BSEM framework to more complex DTA settings, including imperfect reference standards and multiple diagnostic tests, are discussed as directions for future research. The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows. Section 2 presents two empirical data examples to motivate the need for SEM-based meta-analytic models. Section 3 provides an overview of the standard methods and describes our new model in detail. In Section 4, we present the methods’ finite-sample properties through an extensive simulation study, followed by an illustration with real-world data examples in Section 5. We close the paper with a discussion and conclusion in Section 6.

2.2 DVT data 

Kearon et al. (1998) 38 investigated the accuracy of venous ultrasonography for diagnosing deep venous thrombosis (DVT). The metadat 39 package provides these data, which include diagnostic accuracy results from studies evaluating venous ultrasonography for diagnosing a first episode of DVT in symptomatic and asymptomatic patients. Contrast venography determined the presence or absence of DVT, and venous ultrasonography was evaluated as the diagnostic test. The dataset contains study-level information, including patient status (asymptomatic vs symptomatic) and the numbers of true positives, positive patients, true negatives, and negative patients, from which the corresponding 2 × 2

diagnostic tables can be obtained (see Appendix Table A.2). Figure 2 presents the forest plot of sensitivity and specificity for the 30 studies included in the analysis, with both measures presented on the logit scale. Although not as high as for the IAQ dataset, considerable variation is observed in the study-specific specificity estimates and their precision. The review contains an average of about 50 and 96 participants with and without DVT, respectively. In addition, several studies have very narrow CIs, whereas others have considerably wider CIs. These characteristics provide substantial between-study heterogeneity in the observed specificity and make the DVT data suitable for illustrating the application of our proposed approach in Section 5.

3.2 Proposed approach for DTA meta-analysis 

In this subsection, we present the proposed BSEM formulation of standard models for ADMA of DTA studies. BSEM provides an alternative but equivalent formulation of the standard BLMM. 9 However, the BSEM framework allows the model to be expressed in terms of latent variables and path structures, which facilitates both interpretability and extension to more complex situations. As defined earlier, let yi=[g(cSe i), g(cSp i)] ′ denote the observed logit-transformed sensitivity and specificity for study i. BSEM’s measurement model is equivalent to BLMM’s within-study model and can be expressed in SEM notation as: 

yi = ϕi + εi, εi ∼ N2(0, Si), (5) where ϕi = ( ϕ1i, ϕ 2i)′ are the latent variables, and Si captures the measurement error. BSEM’s structural model is also equivalent to the between-study model of the BLMM that describes the hetero-geneity across studies: 

ϕi ∼ N2(μ, Σ), (6) where the latent variables εi vary across studies and their covariance structure Σ represent random effects. Finally, the combined BSEM representation is obtained by substituting the structural model (5) into the measurement model (6) as: 

yi = μ + ϕi + εi,

where ϕi ∼ N2(0, Σ) denote the between-study random effects and εi ∼ N2(0, Si) denote the within-study error. Therefore, the BLMM and BSEM representations have the same marginal model: 

yi ∼ N2(μ, Σ + Si),

3.3 Model implementations in R

We have successfully implemented the proposed meta-analytic BSEM approach for ADMA in DTA studies by adapting functions in the metaSEM 35 and OpenMX 32 R30 packages. The metaSEM 35 package implements a two-stage meta-analytic SEM approach for meta-analysis of intervention studies in which covariance matrices are pooled in stage 1, and SEM is fit in stage 2. The package uses a simpler syntax and enables faster implementation. However, if one needs more flexibility to accommodate custom likelihoods that implement imperfect reference standards, for example, the 

OpenMx 32 package can be used. Following Cheung (2015), 29 we fitted the proposed BSEM approach using ML, tested the equality of heterogeneity variances across studies using the likelihood ratio (LR) test statistic, and constructed 95% confidence ellipses around the observed and pooled logit-transformed sensitivity and specificity. In addition, we employed the Huber-White robust “sandwich estimator" 43–46 for the BSEM to obtain robust standard errors to mitigate the effect of outlying studies on CIs. We fitted the standard BLMM 9 and the BGLMM 10 using the Laplace approximation algorithm with the mada 37 and 

lme4 47 packages in R,30 respectively. For all data analysis, we used R30 version 4.5.2 (2025-10-31) and RStudio 48 

version 2026.8.2.200.

other scenarios are available in the appendices of the online Ancillary files . We also present results based on normally distributed random effects as our primary findings and discuss the effects of other random-effects distributions. Regarding the bias of the pooled sensitivity and specificity estimators (Figure 4), the three models underestimated both parameters in the low (Se = 0 .70 , Sp = 0 .80) or moderate (Se = 0 .80 , Sp = 0 .90) diagnostic accuracy settings. In particular, the standard BLMM’s negative bias was dominant across the three diagnostic accuracy levels, whereas the proposed BSEM generally yielded the least biased estimates. The standard BGLMM approach produced bias values between those of the BLMM and the BSEM, especially in moderate (Se = 0 .80 , Sp = 0 .90) to high 

(Se = 0 .95 , Sp = 0 .99) diagnostic accuracy settings. Moreover, although the biases of the BSEM and BGLMM improved with increasing numbers of studies, those of the BLMM remained constant. However, all three models yielded unbiased and almost indistinguishable results when the number of participants in the diseased and non-diseased groups increased to n1 = 300 and n2 = 700 , respectively, irrespective of the values of the other parameters (Appendix Figures B.3, B.6 and B.9). 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

We evaluated the performance of the proposed BSEM against two widely used standard approaches for ADMA of DTA studies: the BLMM of Reitsma et al. (2005) 9 and the BGLMM of Chu and Cole (2006) 10 through an extensive simulation study. The relative performance of the three approaches depended on the diagnostic-accuracy level, the number of studies, and the sample size per study. Overall, the proposed BSEM was comparable to the BGLMM or outperformed the BLMM in estimating pooled sensitivity and specificity and their associated uncertainties. In addition, the proposed BSEM has flexible characteristics that unify many extensions of standard models, which we discuss further below. An important finding was the strong influence of the within-study number of participants on the performance of all three methods. When the numbers of diseased and non-diseased participants were increased, the differences among the models largely disappeared. Bias and RMSE approached zero, CIs became increasingly similar, and empirical CPs became more comparable, particularly when the number of studies was also large. Thus, the principal differences between the methods emerged in settings in which both the number of studies and the amount of information within the individual studies were limited. This is an important practical consideration because DTA meta-analytic data frequently involve relatively small numbers of studies and heterogeneous studies. 54;55 Therefore, in such settings, the choice of model could have a meaningful effect on both point estimation and inference about pooled sensitivity and specificity. Our proposed BSEM is particularly advantageous in DTA meta-analysis settings characterized by small within-study sample sizes, relatively few studies, and high diagnostic accuracy. Under these conditions, the BSEM generally provided less-biased pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates than the BLMM, competitive RMSEs, and comparable or better empirical coverage than the BGLMM. Moreover, BSEM demonstrated generally stable computational performance, with convergence rates exceeding 90% across all three random-effects distributions. The simulation results also showed that the apparent precision of the BLMM can be misleading since, although its CIs were often narrow, its empirical CP was substantially below the nominal level in several important scenarios. In addition, model choice became less consequential when meta-analyses included many studies with large within-study sample sizes because, as information increased, differences among the three methods decreased. Furthermore, changing the distribution used to generate the random effects from bivariate normal to bivariate t or the Laplace distribution did not significantly alter the main conclusions about the pooled sensitivity and specificity for all three models. The models’ relative performance remained similar, and we observed the same general patterns in bias, RMSE, CI width, and empirical CP. Finally, applying the models to two empirical DTA datasets demonstrated the approach’s utility for practitioners and showed differences between the models that generally aligned with patterns observed in the simulation study. The strengths of this manuscript include introducing an SEM-based approach and inferential methods for meta-analyzing DTA studies, evaluating the proposed BSEM against well-established approaches through extensive simulation studies, and demonstrating the practical utility of the proposed method using real-world datasets. However, the current study also has limitations that present opportunities to further develop the proposed BSEM framework. We considered settings that compare an index test with a perfect reference standard, although imperfect reference standards are common in many DTA applications. We also compared a single index test against a reference standard, although real-world scenarios may involve comparing multiple DTA tests against the same reference standard. Our simulation study also did not account for meta-analyses with study-level covariates to fit a meta-regression model or for DTA studies with partial verification bias due to not all participants receiving the reference standard or missing test outcomes. 56 

Because the proposed BSEM is flexible, future studies can extend it to address the limitations mentioned above. An SEM framework that unifies LCM and SEM-based meta-analytic modelling can handle imperfect reference standards by interpreting sensitivity and specificity as conditional response probabilities when constructing the conditional measurement model, modelling study-specific parameters as random effects, and constructing a full hierarchical likelihood by combining the measurement and structural components. However, it should be noted that in this extension, the SEM-based meta-analytic parameters will not be identifiable without additional assumptions, such as the need for at least three diagnostic tests, parameter constraints (e.g., fixing sensitivity, specificity, or assuming known prevalence), or posing informative priors if the model is fit in a Bayesian context. The proposed BSEM also extends naturally to the setting where multiple diagnostic tests are evaluated simultaneously. 57 In this extension of the BSEM, multiple observed indicators (index tests) will load on either latent accuracy parameters (in models with perfect reference standards) or on latent disease status in imperfect reference standard models. This multivariate extension would allow for joint estimation of multiple sensitivities and specificities, modelling of correlation between tests, and evaluation of index test combinations. As demonstrated in our real-life data examples, meta-regression within the BSEM framework is also possible, as covariates can be incorporated into SEM-based DTA models naturally. For example, study-level covariates such as study design or patient characteristics can be included as predictors of latent sensitivity and specificity or of disease prevalence; test-level covariates can influence measurement parameters; and study-level moderators can help explain between-study heterogeneity due to differences in thresholds, population characteristics, or test execution. Furthermore, the proposed BSEM approach is again suitable for modelling DTA studies with partial verification bias, because missingness can be handled via a full-information likelihood and disease status can be modelled as latent 15 SEM–based meta-analysis of DTAs A P REPRINT 

when verification is incomplete. Finally, developing an extension of the proposed BSEM approach for meta-analysis of individual-participant data from DTA studies is a worthwhile future study to evaluate the framework’s performance in this gold-standard meta-analysis setting. In conclusion, our simulation study and real-world data applications provided evidence that the proposed BSEM is a flexible alternative to existing bivariate approaches for DTA meta-analysis. Its principal advantage is that BSEM provided stable, better estimates across a broad range of practically relevant scenarios, with particularly favourable pooled sensitivity and specificity when the meta-analysis size was small and the index test had high diagnostic accuracy. The results therefore supported using BSEM as an alternative modelling approach for ADMA of DTA studies, although the relative advantages of the standard methods depended on the meta-analytic data size, number of participants within the study, and the specific parameter being estimated by the models.
