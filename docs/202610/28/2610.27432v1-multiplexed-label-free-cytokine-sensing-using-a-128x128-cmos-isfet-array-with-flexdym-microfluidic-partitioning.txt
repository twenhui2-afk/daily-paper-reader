# Multiplexed Label-Free Cytokine Sensing Using a 128 ×128 CMOS ISFET Array with FlexDym Microfluidic Partitioning 

Utku Noyan, a,b Sahil Shah *a and Pamela Abshire *a,b,c 

Cytokine-mediated inflammation disorders such as sepsis and cytokine release syndrome demand bedside multiplex panels that resolve pro-and anti-inflammatory dynamics in minutes, yet label-free FET cytokine sensors remain limited to few devices, single analytes, and PDMS packaging that sorbs small molecules and leaches oligomers. We present a CMOS platform pairing a 128 × 128 ion-sensitive field-effect transistor (ISFET) array (SkyWater 130 nm, SiN gate) with a FlexDym 4 × 1 micro-well overlay that partitions the membrane into four chemically independent regions ( ≈ 2176 pixels each, √N averaging). Two wells carry capture antibodies (anti-IL-6, anti-IL-10), one is a non-specific reference, and one tests specificity; the transduced signal is defined as the differential current relative to the reference signal (∆ID(Wn) − ∆ID(W3)). Single-analyte dose-response measurements spanning 100 fM–10 nM successfully resolve both IL-6 and IL-10 above the non-specific detection floor, including at healthy-serum baseline concentrations. The array shows a per-pixel pH sensitivity of 1.13 μA/pH (CV ∼ 14%). Across two chips, device-to-device variation is dominated by transconductance spread ( gm CV ≈ 62%); a one-time per-device gm calibration reduces analyte-measurement variance to ≈ 16%. We further validate the surface functionalization, characterize device-to-device variation, and demonstrate the FlexDym integration workflow. Simultaneous multiplexed cytokine measurements resolve IL-6 (W1) and IL-10 (W2) against the chemistry-only reference (W3). At the same time, cross-specificity characterization is assessed by applying IL-6 to an anti-IL-10 well (W4), establishing a scalable label-free ISFET architecture for multiplexed cytokine sensing. 

1 Introduction 

Cytokines are short-lived, low-abundance signaling proteins whose temporal trajectories carry decisive diagnostic infor-mation about many inflammatory disorders including sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, cytokine release syn-drome, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) toxic-ity 1–3 . Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a canonical pro-inflammatory mediator; serum levels rise from <10 pg/mL in healthy adults to >1 ng/mL during sepsis or cytokine release syndrome (CRS), and the timing of that rise governs therapeutic win-dows for tocilizumab and other IL-6-axis interventions 4.Interleukin-10 (IL-10) is the principal counter-regulatory anti-inflammatory cytokine; the IL-6/IL-10 ratio outperforms either marker alone for prognostic stratification in septic shock 5,6 . A fast, simultaneous IL-6/IL-10 readout is therefore the smallest meaningful inflammation panel. ELISA delivers pg/mL sensitivity but requires multiple wash steps and 3–5 h of bench time per analyte 7. Multi-plex bead assays (Luminex) consolidate panels but demand a flow cytometer and 3–4 h workflows 8. Lateral-flow strips are bedside-deployable but typically resolve only ≥10 pg/mL and are endpoint assays, missing the kinetics that matter most 9. Optical multiplex platforms based on localized sur-face plasmon resonance approach pg/mL sensitivity 10 but retain optical instrumentation and fluidic complexity that re-sist handheld miniaturization. Field-effect transistor biosensors offer a CMOS-compatible, label-free, optics-free alternative that converts charge-on-binding into a threshold-voltage shift 11–13 . Rani

2 Background and Related Work 

> 2.1 Cytokines as Multiplex Analytes

The cytokine network is multivariate: it encompasses the relative dynamics of pro- and anti-inflammatory mediators that distinguish resolving inflammation from progressing sep-sis 1,27 . The IL-6/IL-10 pair is the canonical minimal panel, and their ratio improves prognostic accuracy over either marker alone 6. Both are small monomeric proteins ( ∼21 kDa IL-6, ∼18.6 kDa IL-10) with well-characterized capture an-tibodies, making them tractable for immobilization-based field-effect transduction. 

> 2.2 FET-Based Cytokine Sensors

FET-based cytokine sensors have demonstrated highly sensi-tive, label-free detection across a range of transducer archi-tectures. Rani et al. 14 reported sub-picomolar detection of IL-2 and IL-4 using nanoISFET arrays, establishing the feasi-bility of real-time cytokine monitoring with field-effect trans-duction. Hu et al. 15 achieved an IL-6 limit of detection of 2.1 pg/mL using an aptamer-functionalized silicon nanowire FET, highlighting the sensitivity gains attainable with com-pact recognition elements. Extending field-effect biosensing to integrated platforms, Ryazantsev et al. 21 demonstrated aptamer-based troponin-I detection on a CMOS ISFET array, while Yang et al. 22 reported 45 pM IL-6 detection using a 0.35 μm CMOS biosensor and validated the results against ELISA measurements. Table 1 places these studies within the broader landscape of label-free cytokine biosensors. Individual FET trans-ducers have achieved fM–pg/mL sensitivity for single cy-tokines 28–30 , while integrated microelectrode platforms have demonstrated multiplex cytokine detection on a common sub-strate 31 . Collectively, these efforts illustrate the trade-offs between sensitivity, multiplexing, integration, and scalability. The present work builds on these advances by combining a dense CMOS ISFET pixel array fabricated in a standard CMOS process with spatial microfluidic partitioning, dif-ferential referencing, and array-level statistical averaging, leveraging the scalability of semiconductor manufacturing to integrate thousands of sensors for multiplex cytokine sensing on a single chip. 

> 2.3 CMOS ISFET Pixel Arrays

Rothberg et al. 18 demonstrated million-pixel CMOS ISFET arrays for semiconductor sequencing, establishing the scal-ability of dense ion-sensing architectures. Moser et al. 20 

extended this concept with automatic calibration schemes for large-scale nucleic-acid amplification assays, while Mis-courides and Georgiou 19 analyzed current- and voltage-mode readout strategies in a 12 800-pixel array. Collectively, these studies showed that pixel-to-pixel offset, drift, and 1/f noise can be mitigated through differential measurement and cali-bration, and that large sensor populations enable statistically robust signal extraction through spatial averaging. However, these platforms were developed primarily for nucleic-acid sensing and sequencing applications. The present work ap-plies the same dense-array sensing paradigm to multiplexed protein detection, integrating a statistics-rich CMOS ISFET array with a microfluidic partitioning layer designed for spa-tially resolved cytokine assays. 

> 2.4 Microfluidic Partitioning Materials

PDMS has been the dominant material for microfluidic parti-tioning because of its optical transparency, ease of fabrication, and widespread adoption in biological assays 36 . However, several material properties can complicate quantitative protein sensing: (i) PDMS absorbs hydrophobic molecules, altering reagent concentrations and assay kinetics 16 ; (ii) it can release uncrosslinked oligomers that adsorb onto nearby surfaces and affect sensor stability 17 ; and (iii) permanent bonding typi-cally requires oxygen-plasma activation, which may interfere with surface-functionalization workflows. SEBS, commercialized as FlexDym, retains the elas-tomeric handling characteristics of PDMS while address-ing several of these limitations 25 . Roy et al. 24 introduced SEBS for rapid microfluidic prototyping, and Lachaux et al. 23 

demonstrated rapid hot embossing, reversible bonding, and substantially reduced small-molecule absorption. Commer-cial FlexDym formulations are ISO 10993 and USP Class VI compliant 25 , form room-temperature seals against Si 3N4 sur-faces without plasma activation, and can be reversibly bonded with reported pressure tolerance approaching 4 bar. These characteristics make SEBS attractive for CMOS biosensor integration, where preservation of the sensing sur-face and compatibility with post-fabrication functionalization are critical. To our knowledge, no prior CMOS ISFET biosen-sor has employed SEBS as the microfluidic partitioning mate-rial. Bonding and cross-leak validation are presented in ESI †

3 Platform Architecture 

This section describes the integrated sensing platform, com-prising the CMOS ISFET array, the FlexDym microfluidic partitioning layer, and the differential sensing strategy used for multiplexed cytokine detection. The hardware architecture establishes spatially isolated sensing regions with thousands of ISFETs assigned to each assay well, while the differential measurement framework combines cluster-level averaging with reference-well subtraction to improve measurement ro-bustness and suppress common-mode disturbances.

3.1 CMOS ISFET Array 

The sensing chip is fabricated in the SkyWater 130 nm CMOS process 37 and contains a 128 × 128 array of PMOS ISFET pixels (Fig. 1a) on a 10 μm pitch, giving a 1 .28 mm ×1.28 mm active area. Each pixel exposes a floating gate to the elec-trolyte through the foundry passivation stack terminated in Si 3N4.

Pixel circuit. The 3T cell uses one PMOS sensing device 

Msense (floating gate, electrolyte-exposed) in series with Mrow 

and Mcol access transistors; Pix_ref is the reference elec-trode voltage which determines VSG . When addressed, Msense 

sources a drain current ID that depends on the surface poten-tial ψ0 at the Si 3N4–electrolyte interface, hence on bound charge in the Debye layer. 

Readout. On-chip, each addressed pixel drives a column-shared current conveyor that holds the column at a virtual reference (fixing VDS ) and copies ID off the array at unity cur-rent gain; the row/column decoders and the current conveyor are integrated on the CMOS die, producing one 128 × 128 current frame per raster. The primary metric is the drain current ID(t) at fixed bias; per-pixel gm from ID(VGS ) sweeps converts ∆ID to an equivalent ∆Vth for cross-device reporting (Section 5.4). 

3.2 FlexDym 4 × 1 Micro-Well Overlay 

The FlexDym overlay (Fig. 1b) is replica-molded from an SU-8 master and aligned so each well covers a known pixel cluster. The measured geometry is 170 μm-wide wells sepa-rated by 200 μm walls; at the 10 μm pitch this maps to 17 pixel columns per well and 20 columns per wall (4 × 17 + 3 × 20 =

3128 exactly). Each well therefore covers 17 × 128 ≈ 2176 pixels and provides √N ≈ 47 × noise averaging relative to a single pixel. Each well is a 500 μm-high channel that runs across the full array from an inlet reservoir to an outlet reser-voir; the solution dispensed into a well (50 μL) passes over the sensing pixels and collects in the outlet reservoir, so the channel volume above the array is only a small fraction of the loaded volume. After surface functionalization (Section 4.2), the FlexDym overlay is activated in O 2 plasma (150 W, 30 s), placed on the functionalized die, and bonded on a hotplate at 90 °C for 30 s followed by 120 °C for 2.5 min 25 , and the bond is mechanically reversible. An 8 × 1 layout ( ∼1000 pixels per well) is supported in the same area for higher-multiplex panels. Bonding mechanics and cross-leak isolation are dis-cussed in ESI † Section S-II.

4 Materials and Methods 

The experimental methods are organized according to the assay workflow. We first describe the universal surface-functionalization protocol that establishes a common amine-coupling chemistry across all sensing wells, followed by the allocation of capture antibodies and control chemistries within the four-well microfluidic architecture. We then detail the electrical characterization procedures used to establish the baseline sensor performance and the protocols used for cytokine dose-response, limit-of-detection, and specificity measurements. 

> 4.1 Materials

Capture antibodies were anti-human IL-6 (clone 5IL6, mouse IgG1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, M620) 38 and AF488-conjugated anti-human IL-10 (clone JES3-9D7, rat IgG1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 53-7108-42). 39 Goat anti-mouse IgG–AF488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11001) was used for fluorescence verification of W1. Recombinant human IL-6 (PeproTech, 200-06) and IL-10 (PeproTech, 200-10) were reconstituted to 100 μg/mL in 0.1% BSA in sterile wa-ter, aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. APTES (Thermo Scien-tific Pierce, 80370), 25% glutaraldehyde (Thermo Scientific, A17876.AP), sodium cyanoborohydride (NaBH 3CN; Sigma-Aldrich, 156159), BSA ( ≥98%; Sigma-Aldrich, A7906), PBS (1 ×, pH 7.4; Gibco, 10010031) and PBS-T (Pierce BupH, 28352) were used as received; anhydrous ethanol and sterile water were laboratory stock. FlexDym sheets were obtained from Eden Tech. 25 

> 4.2 Universal Amine-Coupling Recipe

A common amine-coupling surface chemistry was used across all wells, with assay specificity determined solely by the immobilized capture antibody. The protocol was adapted from a previously validated Si 3N4 functionalization workflow used for Annexin V immobilization in phosphatidylserine sensing 26 and follows established silane–aldehyde coupling procedures 40,41 .The Si 3N4 surface was first cleaned using RCA-1 (70 °C, 15 min) and activated by O 2 plasma (150 W, 30 s) to generate surface hydroxyl groups. The surface was then silanized with 2% APTES in anhydrous ethanol (90 min, room temperature) and cured (110 °C, 15 min) to form a stable aminosilane layer. Primary amines were subsequently crosslinked using 2.5% glutaraldehyde (50 min), followed by reduction with 160 mM NaBH 3CN (15 min) to stabilize the linkage. Following surface preparation, the FlexDym overlay was bonded to the chip and capture antibodies were dispensed into the designated wells at 10 μg/mL (60 min, room temperature, humidified environment). The wells were then rinsed with PBS-T, blocked with 1% BSA (W1–W3), and washed prior to electrical measurements. Because the underlying chemistry is identical for all wells, different targets can be accommodated by substituting the capture probe while preserving the same surface-functionalization workflow. The complete protocol is provided in ESI † Section S-I. 

Safety. RCA-1 (NH 4OH/H 2O2) and glutaraldehyde were handled in a fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment. NaBH 3CN is toxic and releases hydrogen cyanide on contact with acid; it was handled only in a fume hood, kept away from acids, and disposed of as cyanide-containing waste according to institutional procedures. Oxygen-plasma treatment was performed in an enclosed plasma system.

4.4 On-chip Electrical Characterization 

Chips are characterized in 1 × PBS at pH 4, 7, and 10. For each buffer, full ID–VGS transfer curves are acquired on the central sensing region (a 4 × 4 pixel block at the array center) and full-array heatmaps are collected at fixed VGS . Per-pixel pH sensitivity is extracted as the slope of ID versus pH; per-pixel gm at the operating point is the ID–VGS derivative. A pH4 →pH10 →pH4 reversibility frame brackets the titration so drift can be separated from the pH response. Device-to-device variation is assessed by repeating this characterization on a second physical chip (Section 5.4). Per-cell transfer curves and the reversibility test are in ESI † Section S-IV. 

4.5 Dose-Response and Specificity Protocol 

After surface-functionalization validation, the chip was buffer-exchanged to 0 .01 × PBS through at least three sequential washes to increase the Debye length for cytokine detection. Cytokine dose-response measurements were performed using a concentration ladder spanning 100 fM–10 nM in 10 × incre-ments. Each concentration (50 μL per well) was introduced sequentially from lowest to highest using 1 mL and 100 μL syringes, followed by a 10 min incubation to allow binding and a 1 min PBS-T rinse before the next injection. The pri-mary measurement was the drain current, ID(t), acquired at a fixed gate bias of VGS = + 2 V from the central sensing pixels of each well. For each channel, ∆ID was referenced to its pre-injection baseline, and the differential sensing signal was obtained by subtracting the response of the chemistry-only reference well (W3) according to (2). 5 Results 

The results are presented in the order of the experimental validation workflow. Baseline electrical characterization first establishes the intrinsic performance of the CMOS ISFET array, including pH sensitivity, drift, noise, and transcon-ductance, providing the reference operating conditions for biosensing measurements. The surface functionalization is then validated electrically, followed by characterization of IL-6 and IL-10 dose responses, limits of detection, and temporal kinetics. Finally, device-to-device reproducibility, multiplex operation, specificity, and FlexDym microfluidic integration are evaluated to assess the complete sensing platform.

> 5.3 Dose Response and Limit of Detection

The measured dose-responses for both cytokines are shown in Fig. 4(a), with the spatial (masked-well) rendering in Fig. S6 (ESI †). IL-10 rises steeply: the response is already a large fraction of its 10 nM level at 100 fM, indicating a high-affinity capture (apparent half-maximal EC 50 ≈ 0.8 pM), and remains dose-graded across the full 100 fM–10 nM window without saturating, reaching 5.40 μA at 10 nM. IL-6 is graded across the same window (apparent EC 50 ≈ 17 pM), reaching 2.94 μA. Reading each means-line down to the 3 σ non-specific detec-tion floor gives LODs of < 100 fM ( < 1.9 pg /mL) for IL-10 and ≈ 0.7 pM ( ≈ 14 pg /mL) for IL-6, both at or below the healthy-serum baseline (<10 pg/mL 4). 

Basis of the detection limit. Ideal whole-well averaging predicts a sub-nanoampere noise floor, which would imply an unrealistically low limit of detection. In practice the limit is not set by thermal noise but by how reproducibly the baseline returns between injections (residual drift and re-equilibration) once non-specific binding is subtracted via the W3 reference. We therefore report the LOD as the concentration at which the dose-response rises above this measured non-specific de-tection floor (the dotted line in Fig. 4(a)), rather than the value implied by the averaged thermal floor. The non-specific de-tection floor is determined from three repeated measurements in each sample Well W1 and W2 and, as it provides a larger and more realistic estimate for limit of detection, we report it as the LOD for comparison against ELISA.   

> 5.3.1 Temporal kinetics.

The cumulative dosing trace (Fig. 4(b)) resolves each concentration step within ∼30 s of in-jection and plateaus within ∼60 s, with plateau σ ≤ 0.08 μA— well below the inter-step difference at clinically relevant con-centrations. The near-saturation of the two highest steps is the temporal signature of the steep IL-10 affinity seen in Fig. 4(a). 

> 5.4 Device-to-Device Variation

Because prior single-device FET cytokine sensors leave device-to-device variability uncharacterized 15 , we quantify it directly across two physical chips. The base transfer char-acteristics differ markedly: saturation current by ≈ 16% and peak gm by ≈ 62% (14 vs 36 μA/V). Since the raw bind-ing signal scales with transconductance ( ∆ID = gm ∆Vth ), the 

uncalibrated device-to-device measurement variance is dom-inated by this gm spread, ≈ 62%. A one-time per-device gm

calibration, reporting ∆Vth = ∆ID/gm rather than raw ∆ID, re-moves the gain term and leaves the saturation-current-level residual, ≈ 16% (Fig. 4(c)). Translated to the analyte plateaus this is 2 .94 μA ± 0.47 μA (IL-6) and 5 .40 μA ± 0.86 μA (IL-10) gm-calibrated, versus ±1.82 and ±3.35 μA uncalibrated. The intra-die per-pixel transducer-gain CV ( ≈13%) is consis-tent with the calibrated device-to-device figure. We therefore report gm-calibrated ∆Vth as the platform’s primary metric, which keeps device-to-device variance near ±16% rather than 

±62%. 

> 5.5 Multiplex Demonstration and Specificity

Four-well operation is shown in Fig. 5. With IL-6 dosed on the anti-IL-6 well (W1) and IL-10 on the anti-IL-10 well (W2), the two channels respond independently at ∆ID ≈ 7μA

and ≈ 10 μA, respectively, while the chemistry-only reference (W3) stays at the non-specific floor. Capture specificity is tested in W4, an anti-IL-10 well challenged with IL-6: the response remains at ≈ 5.5μA, i.e. 7.1% of the on-target IL-6 signal in W1, below the 10% multiplex-specificity criterion. Wall regions read ∼0 throughout, confirming fluidic isolation. Two elements of the specificity characterization remain. The reciprocal pairing (IL-10 challenged onto an anti-IL-6 well) has not been run, so cross-reactivity is established in one direction only. 

> 5.6 FlexDym Integration and Cross-Leak Performance

The FlexDym microfluidic overlay formed a stable, thermally bonded seal on the CMOS chip, preserving the function-alized sensing surface. Fluidic isolation between adjacent wells was verified by alternately introducing pH 4 and pH 10 buffer into neighboring channels and measuring the result-ing threshold-voltage maps. Sharp transitions were observed at each well boundary, with a plateau-to-plateau difference of approximately 121 mV, while the interiors of individual wells remained uniform within the measured per-well noise, indicating negligible cross-leakage over the assay duration. The reversible bond remained mechanically stable through-out all measurements. A quantitative analysis of well-to-well isolation, including boundary leakage, edge-transition width, and leakage as a fraction of the plateau response, is provided in ESI † Section S-II. 

6 Discussion 

The platform addresses three persistent gaps in label-free CMOS cytokine sensing. First, dense-array cluster averag-ing converts the per-device sensitivity of individual SiNW-FETs 14,15 into a measurement qualifiable against ELISA, with both IL-6 and IL-10 single-analyte dose-responses mea-sured and 3 σ LODs at the healthy-serum baseline. Second, adopting FlexDym removes the absorption/leaching patholo-gies of PDMS 16,17 while keeping an elastomeric, reversibly-bonded overlay. Third, allocating a chemistry-only control and a reference well makes the differential subtraction in (2) defensible with built-in controls. The two-chip study further shows that the dominant reproducibility risk is transconduc-tance spread ( gm CV ≈ 62%) and that a per-device gm calibra-tion is sufficient to suppress it to ≈ 16% – a concrete, testable reporting recommendation that single-device studies cannot make. Several limitations remain. (i) Cross-specificity is charac-terized in one direction only: IL-6 was challenged against an anti-IL-10 well (W4), but the reciprocal pairing (IL-10 on anti-IL-6) and the non-cytokine interferents (BSA, IgG, lysozyme) remain to be measured.(ii) IL-10’s steep low-end response (apparent EC 50 ≈ 0.8 pM) concentrates most of the dynamic range below ∼10 pM, so IL-10 is detectable but only weakly quantifiable across the upper decades. (iii) Antibody-mediated detection places the binding event ∼8–15 nm from the gate, near the 0 .01 × PBS Debye length, so absolute sen-sitivity is Debye-bounded; aptamer recognition 15,21 would reduce the binding distance and, in principle, gain an order of magnitude. (iv) Validation here is in 1:100-diluted buffer; undiluted serum will require anti-fouling chemistry, which

7 Conclusion 

We have presented the architecture, surface chemistry, and validation of a CMOS ISFET pixel-array biosensor integrat-ing a FlexDym thermoplastic-elastomer microfluidic parti-tion for multiplexed label-free cytokine sensing. With the corrected 4 × 1 geometry (17-column wells, ≈ 2176 pix-els each, √N ≈ 47 × averaging), measured single-analyte dose-responses give IL-10 and IL-6 3 σ LODs of ≈< 100 fM (≈< 1.9 pg /mL) and ≈ 0.7 pM ( ≈ 14 pg /mL) at the healthy-serum baseline, and a two-chip study shows that per-device 

gm calibration holds device-to-device measurement variance near ±16%. To our knowledge, this is the first CMOS IS-FET biosensor to use SEBS rather than PDMS, the first to apply √N cluster averaging to cytokine detection, and the first to quantify and calibrate device-to-device cytokine-measurement variance on a dense array.
