# HypoNet Nankai PS: rapid hypocenter determination for the Nankai Trough using physics-informed neural networks with three-dimensional P- and S-wave velocity models 

Ryoichiro Agata, Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), Japan, agatar@jamstec.go.jp Ayako Nakanishi, Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), Japan, ann@jamstec.go.jp Dan Bassett, Earth Sciences New Zealand, New Zealand, dan.bassett@earthsciences.nz Satoru Baba, Faculty of Sciences, Kyushu University, Japan, baba.satoru.083@m.kyushu-u.ac.jp Yasuyuki Nakamura, Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), Japan, yasu@jamstec.go.jp Gou Fujie, Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC), Japan, fujie@jamstec.go.jp Preprint submitted to Earth, Planets and Space 

1arXiv:2610.27561v1 [physics.geo-ph] 20 Oct 2026 Abstract 

Rapid and accurate hypocenter determination based on three-dimensional (3D) seismic velocity models is essential for seismic monitoring in subduction zones, yet it remains computationally demanding. HypoNet Nankai, a hypocenter determination package that uses physics-informed neural network (PINN) travel-time inference for a realistic 3D P-wave velocity model of the Nankai Trough subduction zone, was designed to address this problem, but supported only P-wave arrival times. Here, we present HypoNet Nankai PS, which extends the framework to both P- and S-wave travel times. We first constructed an S-wave velocity model from the existing P-wave model using empirical conversion relations for marine sediments and the crust. Because the resulting S-wave velocity structure has lower velocities and therefore considerably steeper travel-time gradients, the original PINN training procedure designed for the P-wave velocity structure was insufficient. We therefore revised the training procedure by reconsidering the distribution of collocation points, the NN architecture, and the optimizer. Benchmark comparisons with the fast marching method showed that the revised approach substantially reduced the root-mean-square deviations for both P- and S-waves compared with the original algorithm. Recovery tests using the DONET and N-net station geometries for synthetic earthquakes showed that the travel-time predictions of HypoNet Nankai PS were sufficiently accurate to reliably recover hypocenters and that the system performed robustly. When applied to 223 earthquakes recorded by DONET stations off the Kii Peninsula, the system yielded hypocenters generally consistent with the JMA catalog beneath the network, suggesting that the 3D velocity model captures travel-time variations accommodated by JMA’s 1D model and station corrections. Farther seaward, the estimated hypocenters were substantially shallower than those in the JMA catalog and were located in the oceanic crust and uppermost mantle, consistent with previous relocations that used DONET data and 3D velocity models. Together, these results establish a compact and flexible framework for rapid hypocenter determination using P- and S-wave travel times in realistic 3D subduction-zone structures, enabling newly deployed or temporary stations to be incorporated without recomputing travel-time tables.

Accurate hypocenter determination from seismic arrival-time data remains challenging in seismically active regions such as the Nankai Trough, Southern California, and the Hikurangi margin, despite its importance for both seismic hazard mitigation and scientific research. Incorporating realistic three-dimensional (3D) velocity models can substantially improve hypocenter location accuracy, particularly in structurally complex regions such as subduction zones (Johnson and Vincent 2002; Katsumata 2015; Nakano et al. 2015). Although constructing an appropriate 3D velocity model is demanding, several models suitable for such analyses are available (e.g., Nakanishi et al. 2018; Arnulf et al. 2022; Bassett et al. 2022 for the Nankai Trough, Shaw et al. 2015 for Southern California, and Bassett et al. 2025 for Hikurangi). A variety of numerical approaches for 3D seismic travel-time computation have been developed, including methods based on the eikonal equation (Sethian 1996; White et al. 2020; Giroux 2021) and ray tracing (Julian et al. 1977; Um and Thurber 1987; Moser 1991). These techniques have also been incorporated into software frameworks for determining and relocating hypocenters in 3D heterogeneous velocity structures (Lomax et al. 2000; Zhang and Thurber 2003). Nevertheless, hypocenter determination based on one-dimensional (1D) velocity models remains common in practice because constructing 3D velocity models and repeatedly computing travel times through them remain costly, which can hinder the use of 3D models in operational earthquake monitoring and rapid response. In this context, approaches based on physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) (Raissi et al. 2019) have recently emerged as a promising alternative to traditional travel-time computation methods. In a PINN-based framework, a neural network (NN) takes the coordinates of a source and receiver as inputs and is trained to approximate the solution of a governing equation by minimizing residuals evaluated at sampled collocation points (Smith et al. 2021; Waheed et al. 2021). Training over all possible source and receiver positions within the target velocity model enables an NN to rapidly infer travel times for arbitrary source– receiver pairs. PINNs have increasingly been applied to realistic velocity structures and integrated into hypocenter determination systems (Smith et al. 2022; Taufik et al. 2023; Ross et al. 2026). HypoNet Nankai (Agata et al. 2026) is one such tool and is publicly available as a hypocenter determination package that incorporates PINN-based travel-time inference using a realistic 3D P-wave velocity model of the Nankai Trough region. HypoNet Nankai is the first PINN-based travel-time modeling and hypocenter determination system developed for an entire subduction zone that can generate M8-class earthquakes. 3However, HypoNet Nankai did not support S-wave velocity structures, primarily because the S-wave velocity model corresponding to the P-wave velocity model used for PINN training was not available. Although recent studies have estimated offshore S-wave velocity structures in limited parts of the Nankai Trough (Tonegawa et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2025), an observationally constrained 3D S-wave velocity model covering the entire region has yet to be constructed. Deriving an S-wave velocity model from an existing P-wave model using empirical conversion relations therefore remains a practical approach for regional-scale applications. Unconsolidated marine sediments in the shallowest parts of sediment-rich subduction margins such as the Nankai Trough are known to have high Poisson’s ratios, often exceeding 0.4, and correspondingly high VP/V S ratios that can reach several times the Poisson-solid value of √3(Hamilton 1979; Takahashi et al. 2002; Liu et al. 2025). The resulting low S-wave velocities pose a particular challenge for PINN-based travel-time modeling because, according to the eikonal equation, the magnitude of the spatial travel-time gradient equals the slowness. The low velocities in the shallow parts of the S-wave velocity model therefore yield substantially steeper travel-time gradients than those in the corresponding P-wave model, making the S-wave travel-time field more difficult for an NN to approximate accurately. Consequently, a training strategy that accurately represents the P-wave travel-time field does not necessarily provide comparable accuracy for the S-wave field. Extending a P-wave-based framework to S waves therefore requires reevaluating the training strategy rather than simply training an additional network with the same configuration. Addressing these challenges, we developed HypoNet Nankai PS, which supports both P- and S-wave travel-time inference. We first derived an S-wave velocity model from the existing P-wave velocity model using empirical conversion relations that account for the high Poisson’s ratios of marine sediments. We then developed a PINN model capable of inferring travel times for arbitrary source–receiver pairs within this S-wave velocity structure. To achieve accurate PINN-based travel-time inference for the S-wave velocity structure, which has lower velocities and therefore steeper travel-time gradients than the P-wave structure, we revised the training procedure by sampling both source and collocation points from the full 3D domain with a random-distance sampling strategy, adopting the Deeper-PINN architecture, and replacing the Adam optimizer with the second-order SOAP optimizer. We also retrained the PINN for P-wave travel times using the updated training configuration. We assessed the accuracy of the resulting P-and S-wave travel-time models through benchmark comparisons with the fast marching method (FMM) (Sethian 1996). We then conducted comprehensive synthetic hypocenter determination tests to evaluate 4whether the remaining differences between the PINN and FMM travel times affect location accuracy, assess the robustness of the determination system, and examine whether the results reflect the inherent limitations of the station geometry. Finally, we demonstrated its practical applicability by determining hypocenters from observed P- and S-wave arrivals recorded by the Dense Oceanfloor Network system for Earthquakes and Tsunamis (DONET) (Kaneda et al. 2015; Aoi et al. 2020) ocean-bottom seismometers off the Kii Peninsula. Ultimately, HypoNet Nankai PS aims to enable a broad range of researchers to readily obtain reliable hypocenter distributions based on 3D velocity structures as the first step toward more detailed analyses, including for datasets from newly deployed and temporary stations. 

2 Construction of S-wave Velocity Model of the Nankai Trough Subduction Zone 

To develop an NN model for S-wave travel-time calculations, we first constructed an S-wave velocity model of the Nankai Trough subduction zone based on the P-wave velocity model proposed by Nakanishi et al. (2018) (hereafter referred to as the N2018 model). The N2018 model was constructed by combining two-dimensional (2D) P-wave velocity profiles derived from previous wide-angle seismic reflection surveys that used OBS arrival-time data (JAMSTEC 2004) with a 3D velocity model derived from seismic tomography of natural earthquakes (Yamamoto et al. 2013, 2014). Following Agata et al. (2026) (hereafter referred to as A2026), we focused on developing PINNs for travel-time calculations in the offshore region. This region covers a horizontal area of 900 km ×300 km and extends to a depth of 60 km (Fig. 1(a)). We selected this region because the N2018 model prioritizes accurate modeling of offshore structures. We used the following empirical relationships between P- and S-wave velocities to construct the S-wave velocity model from the N2018 model (MEXT and JAMSTEC 2022): For marine sediments extending from the seafloor to 500 m below the seafloor, we used conversion relations derived from in situ measurements (Hamilton 1979). At greater depths, we used crustal relations derived from a compilation of borehole logs, vertical seismic profiles, laboratory measurements, and seismic tomography models (Brocher 2005). Although Hamilton’s relation is not applicable to P-wave velocity values lower than 1.512 km/s, the N2018 model contains values slightly below this limit (but still above the typical sound speed in seawater of 1.5 km/s), resulting in unrealistically low S-wave velocities. Based on the range of S-wave velocities previously estimated for the Nankai Trough region using seismic data (Tonegawa et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2025), we set a minimum threshold of 0.2 km/s for the S-wave velocity model. Applying 5this threshold changed the velocity in less than 0.1 % of the total model volume. As a result, the largest 

VP/V S ratio in our velocity models was around 7.7. Figures 1(b) and (c) show cross sections through the N2018 P-wave and newly developed S-wave velocity models. We transformed the velocity-model data into a topocentric Cartesian coordinate system centered at (136 ◦E, 33 ◦N, 0), without using a map projection. We used geoid data from EGM2008 (Pavlis et al. 2012) for this transformation. We further translated and rotated the coordinate system to align it with the grid used for subsequent travel-time comparisons. In the original N2018 dataset, point-cloud locations had uniform spacing only in the vertical direction. On average, the N2018 model sampled points at 0.1 km intervals vertically and approximately 0.5 km intervals horizontally. Although dense vertical sampling helps capture sharp velocity discontinuities, such as the Moho, it oversamples other regions with moderate velocity changes. In regions where the vertical velocity gradient was less than 0 .1 s −1, we downsampled the point cloud to a grid spacing of 1 km. This approach reduced computational cost by concentrating resolution in regions with significant vertical velocity changes. After this procedure, the dataset contained approximately 50 million points. We used all of these points for mini-batch training of the PINN, as described later. Because grid-based travel-time calculation methods provide reference solutions for validating our calculations, we did not split the data into validation and test sets, unlike standard machine-learning practice. We used the GEBCO Gridded Bathymetry Data (GEBCO Bathymetric Compilation Group 2023 2023) and EGM2008 geoid data (Pavlis et al. 2012) to define the Earth’s surface within the velocity-model domain. As with the velocity model, we transformed the original bathymetric data, which consisted of latitude, longitude, and elevation, into the Cartesian coordinate system.

3.2 Algorithm and settings of PINN training for travel-time inference 

Because the minimum velocity in our S-wave model is as low as 0.2 km/s, the spatial gradients of the S-wave travel-time field are substantially larger than those of the P-wave field, making accurate approximation of the field by the NN more difficult. Preliminary training experiments confirmed that the 7algorithm adopted in the original HypoNet Nankai (Agata et al. 2026) was insufficient to infer the S-wave travel-time field with acceptable accuracy, necessitating a systematic revision of the training procedure. The updated algorithm consists of three main modifications described below: the sampling strategy for source and collocation points, the optimizer, and the NN architecture. Table 1 summarizes the differences in training settings between A2026 and HypoNet Nankai PS. We also applied the updated algorithm to retrain the P-wave PINN. We sampled both x(i)c and x(i)s from the full 3-D domain to improve inference accuracy. This differs from A2026, in which the source points x(i)s were drawn exclusively from the Earth’s surface, whereas the collocation points x(i)c were sampled from the full 3-D velocity-model domain. In A2026, this strategy interchanged the source and receiver roles at inference time using reciprocity, allowing the NN to predict travel times from any subsurface source to any surface receiver. Although restricting x(i)s to the Earth’s surface, i.e., a 2D manifold, narrows the solution space and thereby appears to facilitate training, our experiments presented later demonstrate that sampling both source and receiver points throughout the full 3-D domain combined with the random-distance sampling strategy (Smith et al. 2021) yields higher travel-time inference accuracy: The random-distance sampling provides a more uniform distribution of source–receiver distances by first sampling a source location and then placing the receiver at a random distance along a random direction. A new set of collocation points was randomly sampled in each epoch and used for a single loss evaluation and parameter update. Learning a travel-time function over the six-dimensional input space formed by the coordinates of a source and a receiver, each specified in 3-D, requires a network with sufficient capacity. We adopted the Deeper-PINN architecture (Jiang et al. 2025), which was specifically designed so that inference accuracy improves reliably as network depth increases, even when gradients of the NN output appear in the loss function, as in a PINN-based loss (e.g., Equation 3). Deeper-PINN extends a standard fully connected PINN by stacking multiple shortcut connection blocks to improve the trainability and expressiveness of deep networks. In this study, each fully connected layer had a width of 256, and the network consisted of 30 shortcut connection blocks. We retained the random Fourier feature (RFF) embedding (Tancik et al. 2020; Hennigh et al. 2021) used in A2026 as the input encoding layer. Domain decomposition, which was employed in A2026 to achieve accurate travel-time inference within local subdomains, was not used in the present study. A single Deeper-PINN handling the entire domain yielded better inference accuracy, as shown in Section 4.1. Using a single domain rather than domain decomposition affects only the accuracy 8and does not change the user experience. To accelerate training of this larger network, we adopted the state-of-the-art second-order optimizer SOAP (Shampoo as Adam’s Preconditioner) (Vyas et al. 2025), which has been reported to improve the convergence of PINNs (Wang et al. 2025). SOAP combines hidden layer-wise second-order preconditioning with an efficient Adam-style update rule, providing faster convergence than Adam (Kingma and Ba 2015) for NN training. We selected the P- and S-wave models at 30,000 and 40,000 epochs, respectively, trained by using eight NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs in Earth Simulator at JAMSTEC. We terminated the training based on the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of inferred travel times with FMM results (see Section 4.1). 

3.3 Hypocenter determination method 

HypoNet Nankai PS uses a maximum a posteriori (MAP) approach for hypocenter determination (Hirata and Matsu’ura 1987), following A2026. This method defines a joint posterior probability density function (PDF) for the hypocenter parameters (longitude, latitude, and depth) and the origin-time shift. The joint PDF is marginalized over the origin time, yielding the posterior PDF for the hypocenter locations alone. The MAP solution of this location posterior is obtained using the gradient-based L-BFGS method (Liu and Nocedal 1989). To reduce sensitivity to the initial hypocenter location, the system performs three parallel searches starting beneath the station with the earliest observed arrival, at depths of 7.7, 30.5, and 53.3 km, and selects the solution with the highest posterior probability. Location uncertainties are estimated using the Laplace approximation around the MAP solution. The origin-time shift and its uncertainty are additionally estimated as a post-processing step. Whereas the hypocenter determination system presented in A2026 was designed primarily for CPU-based operation, we implemented the present system to take advantage of GPU acceleration. Although the achievable speedup depends on the number of observations and the hardware specifications, GPU execution is expected to reduce the computation time for hypocenter determination by a factor of several. The prior distribution, optimization, parameterization, and other settings generally follow A2026. 94 Numerical validation experiments 4.1 Forward travel-time comparison with FMM 

We designed verification problems to compare travel times inferred by the trained PINN with those computed using FMM (Sethian 1996; Ganster 2023), one of the most widely used grid-based finite-difference methods. We generated 20 random seismic sources within the target 3D domain and computed travel times at virtual receiver points spaced at 1 km intervals on the Earth’s surface. The FMM grid intervals were 0.5 km horizontally and 0.1 km vertically, matching those of the data points in the N2018 velocity model. Preliminary runs confirmed that these grid intervals were sufficiently fine to provide reliable reference solutions. We used 15 sources as a validation set to determine the training termination epochs reported in Section 3.2. Every 10,000 epochs, we calculated the root-mean-square difference (RMSD) between the PINN and FMM travel times over all surface grid points for each validation source and used the mean of these per-source RMSDs as the validation metric (Figure S1). The remaining five sources were reserved as a test set. For the S waves, the travel-time functions on the Earth’s surface for five test sources, calculated using both PINN and FMM, show good agreement (Fig. 3(a)). We quantified the difference using RMSD at the surface grid points. The RMSDs for S-wave travel-time inference range from 0.1 to 0.3 s. We also computed travel times using an NN trained with the same algorithm as in A2026, which resulted in considerably larger RMSDs, ranging from 0.6 to 1.0 s (Fig. 3(b)). For simplicity, we omitted the domain decomposition used in A2026, which had little impact on the overall evaluation. We attribute the improved travel-time accuracy to the newly introduced PINN training strategy presented in Section 3.2. P-wave travel times from PINN and FMM agree with RMSDs smaller than 0.12 s, compared with those ranging from 0.1 to 0.3 s in A2026, suggesting improved accuracy with the present training strategy (Fig. 4). We further visualized the distribution of the differences between travel times inferred by the PINN trained in this study and those computed with FMM at each grid point on the Earth’s surface using histograms (Fig. 5). In addition to the results for all grid points, we compared the results for points within 200 km of the source in the horizontal direction. We made this comparison because the hypocenter determinations routinely performed by the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) place greater weight on stations closer to the epicenter. The histograms show that the RMSD is generally smaller when only surface points 10 within 200 km of the source are evaluated although their shapes vary among sources. 

4.2 Synthetic hypocenter determination 

The results in the previous subsection validate the ability of our PINN to provide travel times of comparable accuracy to FMM for forward modeling. However, we further investigated how differences between the two methods affected the target inverse problem of hypocenter determination. We also assessed the robustness of our determination algorithm. In addition, we examined whether the results were consistent with physical expectations regarding the inherent limitations imposed by limited observational coverage. We conducted synthetic hypocenter-determination tests using P- and S-wave arrival times, assuming the use of seafloor seismic observation systems in the Nankai Trough subduction zone. We used 51 stations of DONET (Kaneda et al. 2015; Aoi et al. 2020) and 36 stations of Nankai Trough Seafloor Observation Network for Earthquakes and Tsunamis (N-net) (Aoi et al. 2023), which together span the major portion of the offshore model domain (Fig. 1(a)). To perform the test comprehensively, we placed synthetic hypocenters on a geographic grid covering this domain, with a horizontal spacing of approximately 20 km and at five ellipsoidal heights of -10, -20, -30, -40, and -50 km. In total, we considered 3313 sources whose Cartesian coordinates fell inside the computational domain. We calculated synthetic travel-time data between these hypocenters and the DONET and N-net stations using FMM and used these values as synthetic observations. We added Gaussian noise with a variance of (0 .1 s) 2 to the synthetic travel-time data. We then performed hypocenter determination using HypoNet Nankai PS to estimate the source locations. Each hypocenter estimate was completed in less than 1 s using an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU. This computation time is sufficiently short for real-time applications, given the specifications of a widely used automatic hypocenter determination system (Matsumura et al. 2006). The MAP-estimated horizontal locations of the hypocenters within and around the DONET and N-net observation networks are generally accurate and precise (Fig. 6). Hypocenters on the eastern side of the modeling domain show moderate horizontal location errors, defined as the distance between the estimated and true hypocenters, reaching approximately 10 km at most. For these hypocenters, depth errors are larger, reaching nearly 30 km at some points. Cross sections showing the estimation uncertainty illustrate these tendencies more clearly. The sections at x=100 km, 280 km, and 460 km (Fig. 7 (a)(b)(c)) suggest 11 that the estimated locations of events at 150 km ≤ y ≤300 km, which are located below the observation network, agree well with the true locations and have small uncertainties, with standard deviations generally below 1.5 km. Outside these regions, both location errors and estimation uncertainties tend to be larger. At the sections at x=640 km and 820 km (Fig. 7(d)(e)), above which no observation points are located, both location errors and estimation uncertainties are larger throughout. The extent appears to depend on the distance from the observation points and on their azimuthal coverage. To clarify these relationships, we further visualized the location errors and estimation uncertainties against the distance from the nearest station and the azimuthal gap (Fig. 8). The azimuthal gap is defined as the largest angle between the azimuths from the epicenter to two adjacent stations, with larger values indicating poorer station coverage. Although the location errors exhibit substantial scatter and the effect of the finite extent of the model domain is apparent, the upper bounds of the errors tend to increase with both the station distance and the azimuthal gap. In contrast, the estimated horizontal and vertical uncertainties show a clear systematic increase with both quantities. The vertical uncertainty starts to increase at an azimuthal gap of about 200 ◦, whereas the horizontal uncertainty remains relatively stable up to about 300 ◦ and then increases sharply. In summary, when information from data is sufficient, the inverted locations are consistent with the true locations despite the difference in the forward solvers used in data generation and inversion, that is, FMM and PINN, respectively. The dependence of the location errors and estimation uncertainties on the station distance and azimuthal coverage is consistent with the expected effects of station geometry, providing further support that the hypocenter determination system functions appropriately. 

5 Application to real data: Hypocenter relocation using DONET observations off the Kii Peninsula 

The offshore Nankai Trough off the Kii Peninsula, including the Kumano-nada and Tonankai segments, is characterized by the subduction of the Philippine Sea Plate beneath the Eurasian Plate and a highly heterogeneous velocity structure associated with thick accretionary-prism sediments and the subducting oceanic crust. In such a setting, accounting for three-dimensional velocity heterogeneity is particularly important for accurate hypocenter determination. To assess the applicability of HypoNet Nankai PS to observed arrival-time data, we applied the system to earthquakes recorded by DONET off the Kii 12 Peninsula. Rather than comparing the resulting hypocenters with those reported in previous studies that used different 3D velocity models, we compared them with JMA catalog hypocenters. This comparison is intended for illustrative purposes, with the JMA hypocenters serving solely as an operational reference for interpreting the results. Because the two analyses used different station sets, velocity models, and station-correction schemes, the comparison is not intended to directly assess relative location accuracy or isolate the effect of the 3D velocity model. For hypocenter determination, the JMA uses one-dimensional velocity models assigned according to station location: JMA2001A for land stations and JMA2020C for ocean-bottom stations in the Nankai Trough, with station corrections applied to the latter to account for low-velocity sediments beneath the seafloor (Japan Meteorological Agency 2026). The JMA determines hypocenters using P- and S-wave arrivals from land and ocean-bottom stations selected from the nationwide observation network. Between September 2020 and December 2023, the latest period with available records, stations in the entire DONET systems, namely DONET1 and DONET2, were included in the JMA unified processing. The JMA unified processing included eleven of the 51 stations connected to the twelve DONET nodes (see blue triangles in Fig. 9(a)). This selection reflects the close proximity of stations deployed at the same node (Naoi et al. 2024). We selected 223 natural earthquakes whose JMA hypocenters are located within the modeling domain and for which P- or S-wave arrivals were reported at six or more distinct DONET stations in the catalog, allowing stable hypocenter determination. We did not use land-station arrivals because the target domain of HypoNet Nankai PS is an offshore region. Accordingly, differences from the JMA catalog reflect differences between the complete analysis configurations rather than the effect of the velocity model alone. We obtained travel times by subtracting the JMA origin time from the reported arrival times. We applied HypoNet Nankai PS to these travel-time data. We did not apply the station corrections employed in the JMA analyses because those corrections were determined under the assumption of the JMA 1D velocity model. The horizontal locations obtained by HypoNet Nankai PS show minor differences from the JMA catalog locations and moderate uncertainty, except for a few events located east of the observation network (Fig. 9 (a)(b)). This indicates that the locations of most events are well constrained horizontally in both our results and the JMA catalog. By contrast, hypocenter distributions along three cross sections show a marked contrast in depth patterns (Fig. 9 (c)(d)). 13 We examine the depth differences in more detail, focusing on their spatial variation. In the region landward of a distance of -20 km in the cross sections, where the events lie beneath the DONET network and near the land, the estimated hypocenter depths are generally consistent with the JMA catalog depths. JMA hypocenters near the coast are generally expected to be more accurate than those farther offshore (Japan Meteorological Agency 2026). This agreement suggests that, for events with favorable station geometry, the 3D velocity model used here captures travel-time variations that the JMA analysis effectively accommodates through a combination of JMA2020C, a 1D velocity model tuned for offshore stations in the Nankai Trough, and the station corrections. In the region seaward of a distance of -20 km, where the receivers are distant, the hypocenters estimated by HypoNet Nankai PS are substantially shallower than those in the JMA catalog. These events lie within the oceanic crust and upper mantle, as indicated by the plate boundary model proposed by Nakanishi et al. (2018). To clarify how the depth difference depends on the distance along the cross sections, we plotted our estimated depths against the JMA depths separately for two groups divided at a distance of -20 km, namely the seaward group and the landward group (Fig. 10). The dashed lines in Fig. 9 (a)(b) indicate this division. Both the bias and the RMS of the depth differences are considerably larger for the seaward group than for the landward group. For the seaward group, the bias, defined as the mean of our depths minus the JMA depths, is -18.6 km. Although the analysis periods do not overlap, this bias is comparable to that reported by Yamamoto et al. (2022), who relocated offshore earthquakes using DONET data and 3D velocity models and obtained hypocenters 10–15 km shallower than the JMA catalog depths. At large offsets, travel times are expected to be dominated by long paths through complex three-dimensional structures, whose effects cannot be absorbed by station statics. We therefore interpret the offshore depth difference between our hypocenters and JMA’s as an effect of velocity-model representation combined with station geometry and increased uncertainty. Our results show many hypocenters in the uppermost oceanic mantle. Although interpreting the seismicity distribution is beyond the scope of this study, Obana et al. (2005) similarly located earthquakes in the uppermost oceanic mantle off the Kii Peninsula using temporary OBS observations and a 3D velocity structure, and associated this activity with compressive stress based on focal mechanisms. Finally, we assessed the impact of incorporating S-wave travel times by repeating the analysis using only P-wave data (see Text S1 and Figure S2 for details). Omitting S-wave data left some events insufficiently constrained. Even for successfully located events, posterior uncertainties increased substantially: the 14 95th percentile of the horizontal posterior standard deviation increased from approximately 5 km to 26 km. These results demonstrate the practical benefit of incorporating S-wave travel-time modeling into HypoNet Nankai PS in stabilizing hypocenter estimates and reducing their uncertainties. 

6 Discussion and Conclusion 

In this study, we developed HypoNet Nankai PS, an extended hypocenter determination framework that enables rapid hypocenter determination using S-wave arrival times in addition to P-wave arrivals in a realistic 3D velocity structure of the Nankai Trough subduction zone. The principal methodological implication of this study is that extending PINN-based travel-time inference from P to S waves in a realistic subduction-zone structure is not simply a matter of training an additional network using the same configuration. The S-wave velocity model constructed here from empirical conversion relations contains very low velocities in unconsolidated near-seafloor sediments, producing substantially steeper travel-time gradients than those in the P-wave model. A training configuration developed for the P-wave field did not provide comparable accuracy for this more demanding S-wave field. By combining full-domain 3D sampling of source and collocation points with the Deeper-PINN architecture and the SOAP optimizer, the revised approach enabled a single global PINN for each wave type to reproduce FMM travel times with sufficient accuracy for hypocenter determination. Synthetic tests using FMM-generated arrival times showed that, where station geometry provided sufficient observational constraints, residual PINN travel-time approximation errors did not degrade the recovered hypocenter locations. The larger errors and estimated uncertainties outside the station networks instead reflected the expected loss of constraint with increasing station distance and azimuthal gap, consistent with physical intuition. The application to observed DONET data further demonstrated the practical viability of the framework. The resulting locations generally agreed with the JMA catalog beneath and near the DONET network, whereas the relocated depths farther offshore were substantially shallower and generally consistent with previous studies using DONET data and 3D velocity models. Because these analyses differed in the data sets, velocity models, and station-correction schemes, this comparison provides qualitative context rather than a direct assessment of relative accuracy or an isolated evaluation of the 3D velocity model. Finally, although the general importance of S-wave arrivals for hypocenter determination is well established, the P-only comparison quantified their practical contribution in the present DONET configuration, particularly 15 in reducing their estimated uncertainties. The compact storage requirement of the proposed framework is an important advantage of HypoNet Nankai PS. This point was already one of the strengths of the original HypoNet Nankai developed by A2026. However, that framework divided the model domain into one global region and 35 local subregions and trained separate neural networks for each region. In contrast, the improved training strategy introduced in this study enabled sufficiently accurate travel-time inference without such domain decomposition and the associated large number of neural networks. As a result, the data size of the P-and S-wave travel-time models of HypoNet Nankai PS is only 31 MB each and 63 MB in total. This is substantially smaller than the original HypoNet Nankai, for which the P-wave travel-time models alone required approximately 500 MB. This data size contrast is even more pronounced when compared with conventional travel-time tables. If FMM-based travel-time tables were constructed for the DONET and N-net observation networks, the estimated data volume would be approximately 80 GB for each of the P- and S-wave models. Thus, HypoNet Nankai PS provides a storage-efficient alternative to conventional precomputed travel-time tables. HypoNet Nankai PS aims to enable a broad range of researchers to rapidly and conveniently determine hypocenters with results approaching the quality achieved through expert analyses accounting for three-dimensional velocity structures and/or appropriate station corrections. Conventional travel-time tables and station corrections must be recalculated and re-estimated, respectively, when new stations are added. In contrast, the PINN-based framework offers the flexibility to evaluate travel times for arbitrary source and receiver locations. This feature is particularly advantageous for seismic monitoring involving temporary campaign observations and also makes HypoNet Nankai PS well suited for preliminary numerical experiments designed to plan new observation campaigns. It should again be noted that the S-wave velocity model introduced in this study was derived through an empirical parameter conversion from the P-wave velocity structure and is not directly constrained by observed seismic data. Moreover, even when HypoNet Nankai PS is used, combining the framework with station corrections may be preferable in practical applications (Toh et al. 2026).
