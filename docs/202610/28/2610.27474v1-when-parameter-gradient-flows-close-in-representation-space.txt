# When Parameter Gradient Flows Close in Representation Space

Abstract 

The represented gradient flow of a parameterized model depends on a mobility that can retain hidden parameter information. We study when one entire represented mobility map works for every autonomous loss from one fixed initialization. For two-factor matrix representations wider than both output dimensions, with individually full-row-rank factors, we classify all admissible joint ranks: closure occurs precisely at effective scalar-balanced minimal joint rank or a two-value signed spectral family at full joint rank. All intermediate ranks are excluded; positive represented flows belong to known square-root families. For general representations with constant ambient representation and gradient-Lie ranks, ordinary closure is equivalent to a local invariant-level metric in the analytic case and specified smooth regimes. An endpoint section forces the initially unrestricted mobility map to admit a locally smooth representative on the represented image. We also separate testing families: linear losses locally admit closure at every submersion, whereas one fixed translated strongly convex quadratic family fails it on a delayed-bracket example. A fixed output readout gives an ordinary squared-loss witness. These supporting results do not characterize quadratic-only closure in general. We retain the unresolved smooth output-rank-two case with Lie rank at least four. A frozen deterministic experiment checks a separate engineered-loss construction; it is distinguished from the squared-loss proof, universal theorems and empirical learning performance. 

# 1 Introduction 

For an open parameter domain Ω ⊂ RD and a smooth representation ϕ : Ω → Rd, parameter gradient flow of a represented loss f gives ˙θ = −Dϕ (θ)T grad f (ϕ(θ)) , ˙z = −M (θ) grad f (z), M = DϕDϕ T . (1) States with the same z may have different M . We ask when the entire mobility, rather than only its action on the gradient of a particular loss, is determined by z along all loss trajectories from one fixed initialization. This is the ordinary intrinsic-dynamic contract of [1], distinct from its stronger piecewise-loss testing convention. The two results proved below are an initialization classification for wide two-factor matrix representations and a converse from ordinary trajectory tests to invariant-level metric factorization for regular representations in stated analytic and smooth regimes. All positive matrix mobilities reduce to known scalar-balanced flows; no new optimizer, optimization rate or task gain is claimed. The distinction is a necessity theory for eliminating hidden mobility, not a new formula for the already known positive flow. The loss-family quantifier is substantive: Proposition 6.1 shows that 1arXiv:2610.27474v1 [cs.LG] 20 Oct 2026 all linear tests can pass locally at a submersion, while Theorem 6.3 and Corollary 6.4 give exact nonclosure with familiar output least-squares losses in a fixed model. The latter are supporting exclusion controls, not new learning algorithms. Generic continuous initializations in the nonscalar wide model fail this exact closure, as a direct consequence of the classification. This does not exclude approximate closure or a simpler description under one particular loss. The work uses the conservation-law and Lie-distribution framework of [2, 1]. In particular the gradient Lie algebra and maximal-stack factor-orbit dimensions in the matrix proof are prior ingre-dients, explicitly attributed below. The additional inference under study is how single-initialization, autonomous-loss tests constrain Gram fibers and a local invariant-level mobility. Familiar Frobe-nius, constant-rank, inverse-function, orbit and ODE results are used as standard local theorems, with their hypotheses identified at each application. 

Results and reading guide. Theorem 3.1 gives an initialization classification, including the distinction between minimal, intermediate, and full joint rank. Theorem 4.2 supplies the fixed-start converse in its stated regular regimes. Theorem 6.3 separates linear testing from a fixed-Hessian quadratic family; Corollary 6.4 transfers the witness to squared error after one fixed readout. The scalar-output case and positive square-root flows are known. Section 7 compares these statements with specific predecessor results. Complete proofs are in the appendices.

# 5 Exact autonomous-loss exclusion controls 

The following constructions illustrate the logical strength of the ordinary premise. They are alge-braic witnesses, not experiments or separate novelty claims. They do not refute the bridge theorem, because they fail its ordinary closure assumption. 

Proposition 5.1 (Arbitrarily delayed brackets and a two-loss witness) . For each integer k ≥ 2,let ϕk(x, y, z ) = ( x, y + xkz). Its representation rank is two and its gradient Lie rank is three everywhere. At the origin all iterated brackets of lengths two through k vanish, while a bracket of length k + 1 supplies the third direction. The first differential mobility test passes at the origin, yet ordinary closure fails there, as witnessed by two global smooth autonomous losses. Proof. See Appendix D.1. 

Proposition 5.2 (A rank-four affine-output exclusion) . For ϕ(x, y, z, w ) = ( x, y + xz + zw ), q = 2 

and p = 4 everywhere, and ordinary closure fails at every initialization. Proof. See Appendix D.2. 

# 6 What restricted loss families test 

The arbitrary-loss quantifier in property (O) is substantive. For a family F of global output losses, write (O) F for the same property with only f ∈ F tested: one parameter neighborhood U and one entire matrix map K : Rd → Rd×d, independent of f , must satisfy 

M (θf (t)) = K(ϕ(θf (t))) 9on every forward local solution interval before exit from U , from the same fixed initialization. The matrix K is initially arbitrary. In particular the equality concerns the entire mobility, rather than only its product with the tested loss gradient. Property (O) restricts to every smaller family, but its necessity conclusions cannot be transferred in the opposite direction without proof. The following supporting results make this distinction explicit.

Intrinsic dynamics and initialized tests. Marcotte, Peyré, and Gribonval [1] introduce the ordinary intrinsic-dynamic and invariant-metric notions used here, together with a stronger def-inition for piecewise C2 autonomous losses. Their Theorem 3.8 gives a positive relaxed-balance branch. Their Theorem 3.9 assumes full-rank factors and hidden width at most max( n, m ), and excludes their strong intrinsic dynamic property outside relaxed balance. Proposition 3.10 already resolves n = m = 1 at arbitrary width. Our wide classification addresses ordinary closure with individually full-row factors; its scalar subcase is inherited. Their Theorem 2.14 starts from a dif-ferentiable invariant-level metric and Theorem 2.17 from recoverability. The converse here starts from an unrestricted K and fixed-start autonomous tests, including specified cases with Lie rank larger than representation rank. The conservation-law framework of Marcotte, Gribonval, and Peyré [2], Theorem 3.3 and Propo-sitions H.3/H.5, supplies the Lie distribution, the count of independent common laws, and matrix factor-orbit ingredients. Their Proposition 3.7 concerns recovery from invariants and represen-tation. The additional necessity step here passes from actual autonomous endpoint patches to mobility constraints on Gram fibers. A factor-orbit dimension count alone does not supply that step. 

Known positive matrix flows and larger closed states. Dominé et al. [3], Theorem 5.2 and Appendix C.2.1, recover the factors up to hidden orthogonal transformations, and thereby determine the two mobility Gram blocks, under their λ-balanced and full-rank-product assumptions, giving the square-root structure. Bah et al. [4], Lemma 2 and Propositions 10–11, give the balanced product flow and its Riemannian interpretation on fixed-rank matrix manifolds. These are positive reduction results. The initialization exclusion and original ambient invariant-level arguments in Theorem 3.1 answer a different necessity question. Proposition 3.10 explicitly matches our positive flows to a known narrow family. Zhang and Peyré [5], Proposition 2.1, close the flow on the full weight covariance, retaining the hidden Gram blocks. The state here is only Z = U V ⊤. Importantly, their Proposition E.2 also gives predictor-only square-root closure on the hyperbolically isotropic leaf CJC = α2J after the appropriate width normalization; this is the a = b part of our full-rank positive branch. For the unequal pair, the fixed shift D = C − (a − b)J/ 2 satisfies DJD = (( a + b)/2) 2J and has the same covariance flow because HGJ + JH G = 0. Thus this positive reduction remains a known-family consequence. Their Remark E.3 shows that conserved spectral values generally do not determine predictor velocity. That two-state example does not itself establish ordinary failure from one fixed initialization. We make no novelty claim for covariance closure or the positive square-root formula, and do not infer a global priority claim from this comparison. 

Geometric criteria and mirror flows. Li et al. [6], Theorem 4.9, obtain mirror flow from a regular commuting parametrization under their flow-domain assumptions. Theorem 4.10 gives a necessary Lie-bracket condition when a mirror representation works for all initializations and time-dependent losses. Mirror flow requires an integrable inverse-Hessian metric, whereas properties (O) and (I) allow general mobility. Our initialized autonomous-loss premise and invariant-level conclu-sion therefore have different quantifiers and targets. Horstmeyer and Atay [7], Proposition 1 and Theorem 4, characterize exact lumpability by all-state projectability and invariance of the vertical distribution under Lie differentiation. Their criterion applies to a fixed vector field on smooth man-ifolds with the stated submersion/fiber hypotheses. Applying it backwards here would first require 12 proving nearby-fiber consistency from initialized tests; this is precisely the extra implication we study. Achour, Kohn, and Rauhut [8], Theorem 4 and Corollary 6 in the April 2026 revision, show that for one-dimensional linear convolutional networks with the specified strides larger than one, fixed norm-difference invariants determine the NTK on the smooth neuromanifold, with finite fibers. Their broader work also treats higher-dimensional convolutions. The finite-fiber recovery geometry differs from the matrix-factorization fibers and p > q regimes considered here. We do not extend the matrix classification to convolutional architectures. 

Optimization and implicit bias. Saxe et al. [9] provide exact deep-linear learning solutions for structured settings. Arora et al. [10] interpret depth-induced preconditioning; Gunasekar et al. [11] and Arora et al. [12] study implicit regularization in matrix factorization. These motivate examining represented dynamics, but a convergence or bias result under one loss is not an all-loss mobility classification. Min et al. [13] bound convergence using imbalance and product singular values. Nordio [14] studies training-set dynamics with fixed readout and quadratic loss, retaining a hierarchy of Gram operators at depth. Castin et al. [15] use balancing to change LoRA conditioning. Our results concern continuous Euclidean parameter gradient flow and claim neither their discrete-time guarantees nor an improvement in learning performance. 

# 8 Discussion and Limitations 

Theorem 3.1 determines when the prediction matrix can serve as a complete mobility state for every autonomous loss from one fixed initialization. In the nonscalar wide model, its generic nonclosure corollary rules out that exact description under absolutely continuous initialization. It leaves open approximate closure, loss-specific reductions, and dynamics on larger states such as a full covariance. The factors must be individually full row rank and r > max( n, m ). Individually deficient ini-tializations are not classified. The general bridge requires representation and gradient-Lie ranks constant on an ambient open neighborhood; singular width reduction does not transfer that hy-pothesis. The smooth case q = 2 , p ≥ 4 remains unresolved. All-order flatness alone cannot replace equality of a smooth function on a neighborhood. The fixed-Hessian quadratic witness is theoret-ical: no numerical a∗, quadratic grid, or empirical convergence order is asserted. The historical engineered-loss check in Appendix G is a different test. Continuous-time mobility equality does not imply equality of finite-step updates.
