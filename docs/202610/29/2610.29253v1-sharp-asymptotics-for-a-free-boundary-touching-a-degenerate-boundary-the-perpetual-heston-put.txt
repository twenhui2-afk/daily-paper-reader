# Sharp asymptotics for a free boundary touching a degenerate boundary: the perpetual Heston put 

Takwon Kim ∗ Junkee Jeon † Jehan Oh ‡

Abstract 

We study the exercise boundary of the perpetual American put in the Heston model near zero variance. There the pricing operator degenerates, and the exercise boundary, a free boundary in the stock–variance half-plane, touches the boundary of the half-plane. No Feller condition is imposed. The boundary is analytic at positive variance and extends as a regular C1 curve tangent to the zero-variance axis. Its approach to the axis follows one of three laws, according to whether the Feller ratio 2 κθ/σ 2 is larger than, equal to, or smaller than one: a logarithmic correction to linear order, an exponential correction in the square root of the logarithm, or a fractional power. In the first two cases the leading coefficients are explicit and the tangent is not Hölder continuous at the axis. In the third case the exponent is selected by a possibly nonsymmetric stable operator on the axis, and the amplitude is finite and positive. 

Keywords. Free boundary, obstacle problem, degenerate elliptic equation, perpetual Ameri-can put, Heston model, stable operator.

# 1 Introduction 

This paper concerns a free boundary that touches a boundary on which the elliptic operator degenerates. The problem comes from the perpetual American put in the Heston model. In the stock–variance half-plane the exercise boundary is the free boundary of an obstacle problem, the diffusion coefficient vanishes on the zero-variance axis, and the variance has a positive inward drift there. We address three questions: whether the exercise boundary has a nontrivial limit at zero variance, what regularity the boundary curve has at that endpoint, and what asymptotic law governs its approach. The entry argument is used under a contradiction hypothesis that the ratio is bounded, so it does not require stock smooth fit at zero variance as an input. Theorem 3.12 obtains continuity of the tangent by rescaling at moving contact points. This completes the geometric part of the argument before the exact rates are determined. Second, vertical integration of the obstacle equation controls the error after averaging in log-stock. Theorem 4.4 uses convexity to obtain compactness of the rescaled inverse boundaries and concavity of their normalized limits. Lemma 4.7 gives an O(v) approach estimate for the value on the whole axis. After a shear, Proposition 4.8 represents the axis trace by an explicit adjoint kernel. The lower-order terms have a tangential C1,λ remainder for every λ < 1. Third, this equation selects the rate. For ν > 1, the contribution near the observation point balances the derivative tail and gives a logarithmic power in Proposition 5.1. At ν = 1, the local integral has a logarithmic divergence, and the finite-part calculation in Proposition 5.2 determines the coefficient. For 0 < ν < 1, the thin-layer limit is a nonsymmetric Riesz potential. In Proposition 5.3, convexity of the entire axis trace supplies the growth and integrability conditions for a stable half-line classification. Lemma 5.4 then proves convergence to a positive finite amplitude by a Mellin transform, and Proposition 5.5 applies this argument to the actual boundary.
