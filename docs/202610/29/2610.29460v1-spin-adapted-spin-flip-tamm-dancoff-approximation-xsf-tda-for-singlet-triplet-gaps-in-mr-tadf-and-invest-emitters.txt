# Abstract 

Reliable singlet–triplet gaps are central to the design of multiple-resonance thermally acti-vated delayed-fluorescence (MR-TADF) and inverted singlet–triplet (INVEST) emitters, yet conventional time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) and its Tamm–Dancoff ap-proximation (TDA) often fail to capture the small correlation-driven energy shifts that de-termine the magnitude and sign of ∆ EST . In this work, we assess the recently introduced spin-adapted TDA for spin-flip-down exci-tations (XSF-TDA) [Mol. Phys. e2631735 (2026)] as a practical approach to this prob-lem. Starting from a high-spin restricted open-shell Kohn–Sham reference, XSF-TDA generates spin-adapted low-spin roots arising from open–open (OO), core–open (CO), open– virtual (OV), and core–virtual (CV) types of spin-flip-down excitations. Across four appli-cation sets comprising 243 benchmark cases, XSF-TDA substantially reduces the system-atic positive bias observed for TDA and un-restricted spin-flip TDA (USF-TDA). With the B3LYP functional, XSF-TDA reduces the equal-set-weighted macro-MAE relative to USF-TDA from 0.252 eV (0.158 eV) to 0.102 eV (0.056 eV) with the ALDA0 (multicollinear) kernel. Its accuracy is competitive with state-specific ∆DFT and quasiparticle-energy DFT (QE-DFT), while being able to provide deeper mechanistic insight through the response vec-tor. In particular, spin-adapted CV configu-rations provide selected higher-excitation char-acter that strengthens the OO–CV dynamical-spin-polarization channel, which preferentially stabilizes the singlet states. We also identify an INVEST emitter with appreciable OO–OV mixing, suggesting a possible additional con-tribution to singlet stabilization when the CV contribution is small. The XSF-TDA method therefore provides practical singlet–triplet gap predictions together with interpretable state analysis for the screening and mechanistic un-derstanding of MR-TADF and INVEST emit-ters. 

# 1 Introduction 

Thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) enables organic light-emitting diodes to harvest both singlet and triplet excitons by reverse intersystem crossing (rISC), pro-vided that the lowest singlet–triplet gap, ∆EST = E(S 1) − E(T 1), is sufficiently small. 1

1arXiv:2610.29460v1 [physics.chem-ph] 21 Oct 2026 Multiple-resonance TADF 2 (MR-TADF) intro-duced a complementary molecular-design prin-ciple: alternating electron-rich and electron-deficient atoms in a rigid π framework local-izes the frontier orbitals on adjacent atomic sites, simultaneously reducing their exchange interaction and suppressing the structural re-laxation that broadens conventional donor– acceptor emission. The seminal report by Hatakeyama and co-workers 2 demonstrated this multiple-resonance effect in ultrapure-blue TADF molecules, establishing the foundation of the modern MR-TADF field. The result-ing combination of narrow emission, high color purity, and efficient triplet harvesting has stim-ulated extensive experimental and computa-tional development. 3,4 

An even more demanding regime is en-countered in inverted singlet–triplet (INVEST) molecules, for which S 1 lies below T 1 (∆ EST <

0), thereby eliminating, in principle, the en-dothermic energy-gap penalty for reverse in-tersystem crossing (rISC). The predicted inver-sions of heptazine, cyclazine, and related com-pounds 5–7 and their subsequent experimental realization 8 have made reliable prediction of both small positive and negative gaps an im-portant problem in emitter screening. 9–13 Al-though MR-TADF and INVEST are not syn-onymous material classes, they share a difficult electronic-structure regime: reduced frontier-orbital exchange first creates a near-degeneracy, after which singlet-selective dynamical spin po-larization (DSP), orbital relaxation, and dy-namic correlation determine the magnitude and sometimes the sign of ∆ EST .3,14–16 

This correlation balance explains why the problem is not solved merely by choosing an ap-propriate exchange-correlation (XC) functional in conventional time-dependent density func-tional theory (TD-DFT) 17,18 and its Tamm-Dancoff approximation (TDA). 19 Important DSP pathways contain double- and higher-excitation character relative to a closed-shell determinant, whereas conventional adiabatic linear-response TD-DFT and TDA lack an ex-plicit description of double excitations. 20 This limitation contributes to the positive singlet– triplet gap bias observed for many INVEST sys-tems. 7,14 Wave-function methods such as cou-pled cluster, algebraic-diagrammatic construc-tion, and multireference perturbation theory can recover more of this physics, but their cost limits systematic screening of realistic emitters. 21–26 Double-hybrid response methods introduce perturbative correlation, 27–30 while state-specific ∆DFT and quasiparticle-energy DFT (QE-DFT) can benefit from separately optimized states. 4,31–37 The latter approaches, however, do not by themselves provide one response-state manifold for transition moments and related excited-state properties. A use-ful complementary target is therefore a re-sponse method that remains near TD-DFT cost but admits the configurations controlling correlation-driven gaps. Spin-flip TD-DFT approaches this target by generating low-spin states from a high-spin reference. 38–41 This compact construction al-ready captures important multiconfigurational character, but ordinary spin-unrestricted SF-TDA (USF-TDA) can suffer from severe, state-dependent spin contamination. 42,43 Several par-tially or completely spin-adapted variants have therefore been proposed. 44–48 A thorough anal-ysis of different spin-adapted spin-flip-down methods is presented in Ref. 48 Here, we only summarize the major distinctions. Specifically, spin-flip-down single excitations from a high-spin reference can be classified into four cat-egories: OO(1), CO(1), OV(1), and CV(1), where C, O, and V refer to closed-shell, open-shell, and vacant-shell orbitals, respectively, and (1) indicates the excitation is an triplet excitation. The mixed-reference SF-TDA 46 

(MRSF-TDA) spin-adapts the OO(1), CO(1), and OV(1) sectors only for a triplet reference. Both the spin-adapted spin-flip DFT (SA-SF-DFT) 44 and the recently proposed XSF-TDA 48 

spin-adapt all four sectors, including the CV(1) configurations most directly connected with DSP. However, XSF-TDA constructs a Her-mitian response equation following our previ-ous construction for spin-conserving excitations in X-TD-DFT, 43,49,50 whereas the eigenvalue equation in SA-SF-DFT is non-Hermitian. The original XSF-TDA study established the method on spin-flip benchmarks, atomic 2degeneracies, and bond-breaking curves, 48 

and included a pilot application to the ten-molecule triangulene benchmark of Loos and co-workers. 7 For that benchmark, XSF-TDA/B3LYP and XSF-TDA/PBE0 with aug-cc-pVTZ achieved mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 0.07 and 0.10 eV, respectively, and markedly improved upon conventional TDA, USF-TDA, and the tested MRSF-TDA pro-tocols. 48 Those results were encouraging but deliberately limited in chemical scope. The present study moves from that ten-molecule proof of concept to systematic validation on 243 application cases: the NAH159 set, 36,51 

35 MR-TADF benchmark, 3,4 four experimen-tally characterized heptazine derivatives, 8 and the 45 INVEST molecules derived by Pang et al. 15 This broader design makes it possible to ask not only whether XSF-TDA works for prototypical inverted gaps, but also when it transfers across calculated and experimental references and chemically realistic MR-TADF structures. Besides, the previous XSF-TDA uses the ALDA0 kernel 42,52 to overcome nu-merical instabilities encountered in the spin-flip noncollinear kernel for XC functionals involving generalized gradient approximation (GGA). In this work, we extend it to support the multi-collinear kernel, 53 which contains GGA contri-bution to the spin-flip kernel, but is free from numerical instabilities. The enlarged dataset also enables a more use-ful mechanistic analysis for molecular screen-ing. In XSF-TDA calculations for ∆ EST , the 

MS = +1 component of the T 1 state is used as the reference, and ∆ EST between S 1 and T1 is directly obtained as the second lowest eigenvalue. Hence, the amplitudes for config-urations arising from different classes of exci-tations can be used to analyze different mech-anisms that leads to small or negative ∆ EST .This provides a common response-vector frame-work for analyzing the configuration mixing as-sociated with small or negative ∆ EST , which is advantageous over other state-specific DFT or TD-DFT based methods that only correct ∆EST . For instance, in conventional CIS(D)-corrected double-hybrid TD-DFT/TDA, the perturbative contribution is added to the ex-citation energy without rediagonalizing the un-derlying response problem, so its response am-plitudes are unchanged by this energy correc-tion. 54 State-specific ∆DFT and QE-DFT can also support electronic-structure analysis, but do not provide the same decomposition into spin-adapted OO(1), CO(1), OV(1), and CV(1) response amplitudes. The results below suggest a qualitative association between larger CV(1) weights and smaller ∆ EST , particularly within NAH159, consistent with DSP-like singlet sta-bilization. Besides, XSF-TDA also identifies an interesting INVEST molecule for which the CV(1) weight is small but the OV(1) weight is appreciable. This case motivates further ex-amination of OO(1)–OV(1) mixing as a pos-sible additional contribution to singlet stabi-lization that has, to the best of our knowl-edge, received limited attention in previous MR-TADF/INVEST studies. Accordingly, this work aims to answer three application-oriented questions: (i) how accu-rately does XSF-TDA predict positive, near-zero, and inverted gaps across realistic MR-TADF and INVEST sets; (ii) which func-tional/kernel combinations provide a sensible starting protocol; (iii) how can CV(1), OV(1), and CO(1) amplitudes be used to recognize the electronic mechanism of small or inverted gaps. We provide an open-source implementation of XSF-TDA in the XTDDFT package, 55 and the results of this work provides a guide to apply XSF-TDA to MR-TADF and INVEST systems. The remaining part of this paper is orga-nized as follows: Section 2 presents the XSF-TDA method and its extension to the multi-collinear kernel, as well as an analysis of the possible singlet stabilization channels. Sec-tion 3 summarizes the computational details. Section 4 first calibrates the multicollinear ex-tension, then tests transferability across MR-TADF and INVEST sets and shows how the weights of different classes of excitations can be used to interpret the negative ST gaps. Con-clusions are given in Section 5. 32 Theory

Unlike the CV(1) channel, this OO(1)–OV(1)/CO(1) contribution is more nat-urally associated with relaxation and mixing of the open frontier orbitals with the virtual/core space. 

# 3 Computational details 

XSF-TDA is implemented in the open-source 

XTDDFT package 55 and interoperates with PySCF 62,63 reference calculations. A practi-cal XSF-TDA workflow consists of (i) generat-ing a high-spin ROKS reference, (ii) performing XSF-TDA with ALDA0 or MCOL kernel, and (iii) requesting the desired XSF-TDA roots and configuration analysis. The XSF-TDA calcu-lation returns excitation energies and OO(1), CV(1), CO(1), and OV(1) amplitudes, allow-7ing users to diagnose spin recoupling and the DSP-active channels discussed in Sec. 2.3. La-bels such as XSF-TDA(ALDA0) and XSF-TDA(MCOL) distinguish the kernel used. The attenuation factor for the MCOL kernel is calibrated using the ten-molecule triangulene benchmark 7 (denoted by Loos-10) with the 6-31G* basis. 64,65 Its transferability is further ex-amined using a 19-molecule open-shell subset of the QUEST database 66 (denoted by QUEST-19) with aug-cc-pVTZ. 67 The four application benchmarks include the NAH159 benchmark set, 36,51 the 35-molecule MR-TADF bench-mark 3,4 (denoted by MR-TADF-35), the four-molecule experimental heptazine benchmark 8

(denoted by Heptazine-4), and the 45-molecule INVEST subset derived by Pang et al. 15 (de-noted by INVEST-45). To compare with previ-ous DFT/TD-DFT results, def2-SVP 68 is used for the first three benchmarks, and aug-cc-pVDZ 69 is used for the last benchmark. The considered XC functionals include B3LYP, 70–72 

PBE0, 73,74 and BHHLYP 71,75,76 throughout the main benchmarks, while BLYP 71,76 is addition-ally included in the calibration. The four application sets probe distinct as-pects of method transferability. Some repre-sentative molecules are illustrated in Fig. 2. ## 4.2 NAH159: broad small- and inverted-gap benchmark 

NAH159 combines aza-aromatic cores with electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The ref-erence vertical singlet–triplet gaps were calcu-lated by Kunze et al. 36 at the CC2/aug-cc-pVTZ level using the ωB97X-D/def2-TZVP ground-state geometries taken from Garner et al. 51 

As shown in Fig. 4(a), conventional TDA and TD-DFT overestimate most gaps by more than 0.2 eV, regardless of the XC functionals in use. USF-TDA reduces ∆ EST , but still re-tains a strong positive bias. Compared with USF-TDA(MCOL), USF-TD-DFT further low-ers ∆ EST and reduces the magnitude of the MSE. The sign-classification counts in Table 2 show improved recovery of negative gaps, at the expense of fewer correctly classified positive gaps. However, the larger RMSE and |MAXSE |

indicate larger errors in the predicted gaps. Fol-lowing Ref. 49, we monitor spin contamina-tion in USF-TDA/USF-TD-DFT through the spin-square change ∆ ⟨ ˆS2⟩ = ⟨ ˆS2⟩ex − ⟨ ˆS2⟩ref ;for a spin-flip-down target with Sf = Si − 1, the ideal change is −2Si (equal to −2 for a triplet reference). As shown in Fig. 4(b), ∆⟨ ˆS2⟩ for the S 1 state in USF-TDA deviates from the ideal values significantly for most of molecules in NAH159. USF-TD-DFT improves the spin diagnostic significantly for B3LYP and PBE0, but only slightly for BHHLYP. Relative to USF-TDA(MCOL), XSF-TDA(MCOL) re-duces the positive bias in ∆ EST and improves agreement with the CC2 reference for all three XC functionals. XSF-TDA(ALDA0) also im-proves the accuracy for B3LYP and PBE0, but overcorrects the gaps for BHHLYP, increasing the MAE from 0.255 to 0.330 eV relative to USF-TDA(ALDA0). In particular, the MAE of XSF-TDA(ALDA0) and XSF-TDA(MCOL) with the B3LYP functional is only 0.067 eV and 0.057 eV, respectively. ## 4.3 MR-TADF-35: experimen-tally characterized MR-TADF emitters 

For MR-TADF-35, the experimental reference gaps were taken from the fluorescence and phos-phorescence peak energies recompiled by Kunze et al. 4 These emission-derived gaps are com-pared with calculated vertical gaps at ground-state geometries following their benchmark pro-tocol, although the two quantities are not strictly equivalent. Unlike NAH159, all ∆ EST in MR-TADF-35 are small, but positive values. Figure 5(a) shows that TDA and TD-DFT overestimate the experimental gaps with their MAEs ex-ceed 0.27 eV, see Table 3. In this bench-mark set, most USF-TDA and USF-TD-DFT results have ∆ ⟨S2⟩ close to −2, see Fig. 5(b), indicating that spin contamination is less se-vere here than in NAH159. Nevertheless, USF-TDA remains positively biased. USF-TD-DFT improves the agreement with the refer-ence values significantly for B3LYP and PBE0, with MAEs being 0.046 and 0.059 eV, respec-tively. However, USF-TD-DFT/BHHLYP exci-tation energies are complex for some molecules, see the points highlighted by open circles in Fig. 5. In this case, we take the real part of the excitation energy as ∆ EST . # 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we demonstrate that XSF-TDA offers a practical computational tool for the small correlation-controlled singlet–triplet gaps encountered in MR-TADF and INVEST emit-ters. Across four chemically distinct application sets, it substantially reduces the strong posi-tive bias of TDA and USF-TDA. The method also returns a chemically readable explana-tion. The CV(1) configurations in XSF-TDA are spin-adapted combinations of the nominal core-to-virtual spin-flip-down determinant and the companion determinants required for spin-adaptation. Mixing these configurations with the OO(1) singlet provides the main DSP-like route to a smaller ∆ EST . XSF-TDA also iden-tifies an INVEST molecule with appreciable OV(1) character, suggesting a possible addi-tional contribution from OO(1)–OV(1) mixing to singlet stabilization. An open-source implementation 55 of XSF-TDA is provided and supports both the ALDA0 and multicollinear XC kernels. Extensions to energy gradients, nonadiabatic couplings, and spin-orbit couplings are underway and will be reported in due course.
