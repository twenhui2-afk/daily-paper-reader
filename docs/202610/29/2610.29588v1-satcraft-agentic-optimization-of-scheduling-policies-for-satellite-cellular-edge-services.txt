# SatCraft: Agentic Optimization of Scheduling Policies for Satellite–Cellular Edge Services 

Pengcheng Luo, Dengke Deng, Zenghui Zhang, Genke Yang, and Jian Chu 

Abstract —Mobile edge services can combine local computation with satellite and cellular access to deliver time-sensitive results in areas with intermittent terrestrial coverage. Meeting their deadlines and quality requirements calls for joint computation and transmission scheduling that accounts for task dependencies, changing link capacity, and shared resource budgets. However, optimizing resource allocations or tuning parameters within a fixed policy structure offers limited scope to correct failures rooted in the scheduling logic. We propose SatCraft , a framework in which a large language model (LLM) agent uses execution feedback to revise the code of joint scheduling policies. Each revision undergoes policy checks and complete-run evaluation to assess its validity, runtime, and effects on service comple-tion. Because policy performance varies with access conditions, SatCraft retains valid alternatives in a policy library and selects one for each episode using initial access observations and the known resource configuration. Experiments using Starlink and cellular measurement data show that SatCraft improves cumula-tive completed-service value by 3.61%–16.89% over baselines, including recent state-of-the-art scheduling methods ADPRL, EdgeOPT, and TF-DDRL. It achieves the highest completed-service value in all four resource profiles and averages 25.02 ms per scheduling call. These results demonstrate how LLM-guided policy optimization can improve complete service delivery un-der the computing, communication, and quality constraints of satellite–cellular networks. 

Index Terms —Satellite–cellular networks, edge service schedul-ing, large language model agents, policy optimization.

By interpreting service requirements, policy code, and execution feedback together, an agent can propose changes to the condi-tions and priorities that govern computation and transmission. An LLM agent constructs and repairs joint scheduling policies using feedback from complete service executions. Policy checks and complete-run evaluation assess validity, runtime, and completed-service value; a policy library retains valid alternatives. SatCraft selects one policy from initial access ob-servations and known resource conditions. During execution, that policy jointly determines processing modes, computing and transmission priorities, and cellular use as tasks progress and quality becomes known. Fig. 2 connects this policy optimization process to changes in task schedules and their service outcomes. SatCraft thereby turns execution feedback into reusable scheduling logic that coordinates scarce com-puting and access resources around timely, acceptable service completion. 

B. Contributions 

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:  

> •

We formulate a dual-access scheduling model around complete service delivery. It jointly represents output timing after local processing, delivered-result quality, task dependencies, and satellite and cellular resource limits. The objective is the cumulative value of services whose acceptance conditions are met before their deadlines, allowing processing and transmission decisions to be assessed by their downstream effects.  

> •

We develop an execution-feedback-driven method for optimizing scheduling policies. SatCraft uses an LLM agent to revise policy code according to specified repair directions and execution feedback. Interface checks and complete-run evaluation admit policies that satisfy va-lidity and runtime constraints to a policy library, where changes to load anticipation, link activation, and quality-dependent paths can be evaluated and reused.  

> •

We design a mechanism for context-dependent policy selection and execution. A shallow policy selector relates development-set utility to initial dual-access observa-tions and known resource configurations. After the initial access observation period, SatCraft selects one policy, which then adapts its actions to arriving tasks, revealed quality, and remaining resources.  

> •

We evaluate SatCraft using measured Starlink and cellular rates and modeled service workloads. SatCraft achieves the highest cumulative completed-service value among the compared methods, including ADPRL, EdgeOPT, and TF-DDRL. It also achieves the highest completed-service value across all four resource profiles, with gains of 3.80%–30.16% over these three methods under different resource pressures.

We review how prior work connects resource constraints to useful edge-service results, and how LLMs turn task feedback into executable policies. These two lines inform the scheduling objective and the construction of policies that pursue it. 

A. Edge-Service Scheduling with Satellite and Terrestrial Ac-cess 

Computing and access conditions determine which edge-service results can be delivered. Laniewski et al. character-ize throughput, latency, loss, and power consumption during vehicle-mounted Starlink operation [11], while Wang et al. 

2measure simultaneous Starlink and cellular access to examine performance diversity and multipath opportunities [12]. Pro-cessing is itself constrained: for onboard satellite workloads, Orbital Edge Computing examines pipelines under orbital, energy, and downlink limits [13], and Xing et al. measure the thermal and power constraints of in-orbit COTS devices [14]. Across these distinct deployment settings, communication opportunities and computing limits motivate decisions about where and how much processing to perform before transmis-sion. Collaborative inference addresses this allocation problem by coordinating processing placement and communication. Fan et al. combine deep reinforcement learning and numerical opti-mization to select offloading locations and allocate resources under latency, energy, and accuracy considerations [6]. SLICE uses layer-level energy measurements to schedule deep neural network (DNN) partitioning and satellite–ground execution under a deadline [4]. TargetFuse+ combines satellite–ground object counting with model protection and bandwidth- and energy-aware data throttling [15], while Shisher et al. co-schedule feature generation and transmission for timely multi-task inference at the wireless edge [7]. These approaches link processing effort to transmission demand and delivery time. Assessing the benefit of these choices also requires relating their resource costs to the quality of the resulting output. Application-level adaptation makes this quality–resource relationship explicit. AWStream profiles accuracy–bandwidth tradeoffs to adapt data quality and sending rate [16]; OneAdapt estimates how input configuration affects inference accuracy to guide rapid adaptation [17]. For computing resources, Ekya uses performance profiles to schedule inference and retraining on shared edge GPUs while balancing resource demand and accuracy [18]. When an output feeds subsequent stages, how-ever, the value of such adaptation also depends on its effect on the remaining workflow: a useful intermediate result must still enable the application to finish its required work. Workflow optimization provides a framework for following these downstream effects. Vulcan constructs, configures, and places live machine learning (ML) analytics pipelines across edge tiers, with online adaptation after deployment [19]. Decima represents job dependencies as graphs and learns scheduling policies to optimize completion time under compet-ing workloads [20]. These studies connect resource decisions to progress through an entire workflow. For satellite–cellular services, this connection also involves delivered quality: a result may determine which downstream products remain necessary, while the resources spent delivering it affect other services. Joint scheduling therefore calls for relating quality-dependent task progress and shared resource use to the timely completion of acceptable services. 

B. LLM-Driven Policy Construction and Optimization 

LLM-based methods offer ways to improve the decision logic linking task information to service outcomes. NetLLM adapts LLMs to networking decisions, including bitrate se-lection and cluster job scheduling [21], while Reflexion uses task feedback to guide subsequent trials through ver-bal reflection [22]. Task information and feedback can also guide revisions to the executable code implementing a policy. FunSearch couples generated functions with execution-based evaluation [8]; Evolution of Heuristics (EoH) evolves heuristic descriptions and code together, and ReEvo uses reflection to guide heuristic search [9], [23]. Treating code as the optimiza-tion object makes proposed changes directly testable through execution, with the evaluator determining which changes count as improvements. The evaluator consequently shapes both the search direction and the practical value of the resulting code. Eureka uses reinforcement-learning outcomes to guide reward-program re-visions [24], while AlphaEvolve applies evaluator-guided code evolution to scientific and systems algorithms, including data-center scheduling [25]. For systems scheduling, evaluation must also account for workload behavior and execution con-straints. SchedCP integrates workload analysis, a scheduler repository, and validation interfaces for eBPF scheduling policies [10]. AI-Driven Research for Systems (ADRS) con-nects iterative program improvement to evaluators for cloud scheduling, load balancing, and transaction scheduling [26]. Such evaluation supports measurable code improvement, but its results are tied to the workloads and constraints under which candidates are assessed. Reusing the resulting policies therefore also requires considering how their effectiveness varies across operating conditions. This variation motivates retaining policies with com-plementary strengths and deciding when to use them. Multi-objective Evolution of Heuristic (MEoH) exposes performance–efficiency tradeoffs by evolving heuristics under multiple objectives [27]. Algorithm selection offers a way to exploit differences among candidates: SATzilla selects solvers using instance features and empirical difficulty models [28]. Within LLM-based program search, EoH-S constructs com-plementary heuristics for different instance sizes and distri-butions [29], while LACE uses problem interfaces, a tool library, and time-constrained search to discover complemen-tary heuristics [30]. These studies connect candidate diversity to differences in problem instances and resource demands, making the conditions under which a policy is useful part of the policy-optimization problem. For satellite–cellular services, those conditions interact with dependencies that unfold over a complete execution. A policy revision can change when outputs become ready, whether delivered results enable downstream tasks, and how much capacity remains for later arrivals. Evaluation must follow these effects through the completion of acceptable services, while selection must associate policy performance with access observations and resource conditions available at deployment. Relating complete-service feedback to the context in which a policy will be used is therefore an important consideration when adapting LLM-driven policy optimization to joint com-putation and transmission scheduling. Asynchronous deep progressive reinforcement learning (ADPRL) [31] uses deep deterministic policy gradient learning with asynchronous experience collection, a sin-gle critic, target networks, and uniform replay. > •

EdgeOPT [32] combines dynamic resource pricing, ser-vice admission, and minimum-rate water-filling. Six price/history configurations are evalu-ated on 32 development cases; the selected price scale is 0.01 and the rate-history window is four slots.  

> •

Transformer-enhanced distributed deep reinforcement learning (TF-DDRL) [33] uses a distributed actor–learner architecture with Gated Transformer-XL, V-trace correc-tion, and prioritized trajectory replay. Its policy maps task and access history to the same joint scheduling actions. The network has two attention layers, four heads per layer, and a 16-step memory.

D. Ablation of the Policy Library and Context-Dependent Selection 

We evaluate SatCraft and five ablation variants on the validation set to examine the contributions of generated poli-cies, current repairs, and context-dependent policy selection to completed-service value. When candidate policies are re-moved, the selector is refitted on the development set under the same features and tree constraints. The new-repair ablation removes all current future-load and link-repair policies. The future-load and new-link ablations re-move only the corresponding policies; the future-load ablation retains the matched disabled version. The generated-structure ablation retains only parameter policies with the same selector, while the selection ablation fixes one policy per resource profile from the full library. Table V reports completed values and SatCraft’s gain over each variant. SatCraft’s completed value of 14,792 exceeds that of all five ablations. Restricting the library to parameter policies yields 14,556; the full library adds 236, a 1.62% increase. Fixing one policy per profile from the full library yields 14,516; context-dependent policy selection adds 276, or 1.90%. These compar-isons support the contribution of generated policy structures and context-dependent policy selection when processed-result qualities change on the same access traces. Removing all current repairs reduces value by 104, cor-responding to a 0.71% SatCraft gain. Removing future-load or new link-repair policies separately reduces value by 32 or 44, corresponding to 0.22% and 0.30%. Thus, the current repair round adds a smaller incremental benefit. Because the selector is refitted after policy removal, these differences measure changes in the library and its fitted selection jointly. Overall, the validation results support the role of a varied policy library and context-dependent policy selection, with a further contribution from the current repairs.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

We studied joint computation and transmission scheduling for mobile edge services with satellite and cellular access. Our formulation combines processing modes, quality-dependent task paths, and cellular budgets in an objective based on timely completion of acceptable services. SatCraft combines LLM-guided policy optimization with context-dependent policy se-lection to turn execution feedback into reusable scheduling logic that coordinates processing and transmission for timely, acceptable service delivery. Experiments using Starlink and cellular measurement data show that SatCraft outperforms all baselines in cumulative completed-service value on the test set. Ablations on the validation set support the contribution of generated policy structures and context-dependent policy se-lection. Paired execution traces connect future resource reser-vation and cellular-activation changes to additional completed services. These results demonstrate the value of LLM agents for optimizing executable joint scheduling policies to improve service completion under the computing, communication, and quality constraints of satellite–cellular networks. Future work will calibrate processing costs and result quality for concrete inspection tasks, account for operational uplink access and measurement and device-energy costs, and test the policy library and selector in corresponding workflows. This appendix presents two parameterized prompt templates for the policy optimization process in Section IV-B. The initial template specifies the task, policy interface, repair direction, and budgets; the continuation template incorporates execution feedback to guide subsequent policy revisions. 

A. Task Interface and Repair Direction 

The initial template combines the task, available context, policy interface, and repair direction. Italicized brackets indi-cate fields filled from the working policy code, development records, and branch configuration.                   

> Initial policy optimization Task. Improve the executable scheduling policy for satellite–cellular edge services. Maximize the cumulative value of services delivered by their deadlines with acceptable quality, subject to the service model and resource budgets.
> Context. [Service and product-task interface; quality dependencies; computing and wireless constraints; current policy code; development results and execution traces; repair direction; submission, diagnostic, and runtime budgets.]
> Policy interface. Implement policy(observation, memory) .Return mode choices, a computing task, shared transmission-queue order, cellular activation, and a cellular sending cap; update memory if needed. Use only available observations and memory. Respect mode and timing constraints, and the static checks on imports, file access, and dynamic evaluation.
> Revision. Inspect the current logic and complete-run outcomes. Revise the policy code to address [repair direction] , then submit it through the supplied tools. Use completed-service value, validity, resource states, runtime, and errors to guide bounded revisions.
> Return. Policy code, the reason for the change, and locations of complete evaluation and validity records.

The two repair-direction fields in the current round are (i) future load and resource contention : infer demand from arrived tasks and observed batch cadence to coordinate cur-rent work with later services’ computing, transmission, and cellular needs; and (ii) cellular activation and output timing :account for startup wait, activation budget, per-slot supply, and computed-output availability when assessing when supplemen-tary access can transmit useful data. Interface modes raw and 

compress correspond to the raw and processed modes in the model. Each branch allows up to six submissions and 12 diagnostics; policy callback and episode limits are 2 and 90 s. 

B. Continuation After Execution Feedback 

A continuation keeps the task and interface fixed while adding execution feedback for the next revision.                

> Continuation after execution feedback Task. Continue improving [current policy code] under the same inter-face, service objective, and repair direction.
> Feedback. [Value, validity, resource, and runtime summary; relevant cases and complete execution traces; errors and timeouts; remaining submission and diagnostic budget.]
> Diagnosis. Identify which policy decisions affected output availability, resource use, and later service completion. Distinguish changes in individual actions from changes in service-completion outcomes.
> Revision. Modify the working policy code and submit the next candidate policy within the remaining budget. After errors or timeouts, reduce ex-ecution complexity. Keep the record of each submission and evaluation.
> Return. The revised policy code, reason for the revision, and corre-sponding complete-evaluation results.
