## ABSTRACT 

We study federated learning from continuously arriving data: each client samples from its own distribution, these distributions drift over time, and the server must track the minimizer of the clients’ weighted average loss. Clients are not alike as they collect data at different rates, with different noise, and change at different speeds. The main question is how much history should each one keep? A single shared window must serve every client at once, and so makes a fast-changing client carry the sampling noise of a slow, nearly stationary one. We give each client a memory of its own, sized to that client’s noise, arrival rate, and drift, and refresh it by restarted accelerated method that starts from a common point. The tracking error is then bounded by

A lower bound that puts different groups of clients on different drift clocks shows this quantity is the right one. The two bounds agree up to a constant whenever the clients occupy boundedly many refresh scales, and in general differ only by the sixth root of the number of scales. Experiments show the gain comes from the memory rather than from the solver: it persists across accelerated, stochastic-gradient, and one-step methods, and against a separately tuned common-memory baseline. 

## 1 Introduction 

Many learning systems receive data continuously from several clients whose statistics change over time: sensors in a monitoring network, institutions or devices contributing to a federated model, users of a recommendation service. Because the target is the current population objective, an observation loses value as the distribution that produced it moves away, and the learner must decide how much history to keep. For a single stream the classical answer is a sliding window whose length balances the bias caused by drift against the variance of estimating from few samples (Gama et al., 2014; Mazzetto & Upfal, 2023a). But clients rarely change at the same speed: one sensor may supply a few noisy observations of a population that hardly moves, while another supplies many observations of a population that changes quickly. One common memory for all clients then faces a dilemma. A short memory throws away the stable client’s history, which is still informative, whereas a long memory keeps the changing client’s history, which is already stale. The statistical question behind this dilemma is how long each client’s past observations remain useful, and what a common memory costs. arXiv:2610.15543v1 [math.OC] 11 Oct 2026 Beyond Aggregate Drift

## 2 Related work 

This paper differs from prior guarantees in its prescribed clientwise drift vector, simultaneously running unequal clocks, and the resulting pooled statistical quantity, all introduced in Sections 3–6; Table 5 in Appendix L compares assumptions and criteria. 

Streaming and federated learning. Marfoq et al. (2023) study fixed-weight population targets with local caches and sample reuse; their laws are stationary, and their optimization analysis relies on uniform gradient-discrepancy control. Huynh et al. (2025) treat dependent Markovian streams and nonconvex objectives relative to fixed stationary laws, and Zhao & Liang (2026) optimize admission and retention under sampling and buffer budgets for stationary 2Beyond Aggregate Drift independent streams; we track persistently changing populations from independent samples with uncharged storage and computation, and none of these models contains the others. LASG’s selective communication (Chen et al., 2020) and FedDrift’s multi-model drift clustering (Jothimurugesan et al., 2023) address different mechanisms; we contact every client and track one fixed-weight objective. 

Drift and adaptation. Master-FL (Ganguly & Aggarwal, 2024) uses detection and restarts to control dynamic regret under unknown aggregate variation of empirical losses, whereas our target fixes population weights and separates clientwise gradient drift from information rates. Mazzetto & Upfal (2023a) select a recent window under uniform convergence and discrepancy assumptions. Our adaptive result instead competes with fixed joint memory experts through an independent validation stream. The aggregation and the delayed-feedback reduction are standard (Freund & Schapire, 1997; Joulani et al., 2013); the contribution is that the pooled statistical comparison survives in the causal reuse model, not a new drift-detection principle. 

Optimization and lower bounds. Acceleration with reciprocal-count variance control is established (Tyurin, 2025), and single-stream tracking has guarantees under strong convexity (Cutler et al., 2023); historical reweighting admits discrepancy and complexity bounds (Awasthi et al., 2023), and Mazzetto & Upfal (2023b) prove average-risk block lower bounds for drifting distributions, whereas our construction retains unequal client-group clocks under a prescribed drift vector.

## 4 Client-specific memory under known drift bounds 

A common memory ties the bias of a rapidly changing client to the noise of every other client. When each client’s drift bound is prescribed, a different balance is possible: each client keeps its own window, and the server solves a fresh stochastic problem on the frozen windows whenever it refreshes its report.

## 6 Choosing among joint memories without drift inputs 

Corollary 4.2 identifies good memories when the drift bounds are available; when they are not, a finite collection of joint memory vectors can be combined on the basis of an independent validation stream, under one additional assumption: the sample loss has bounded oscillation at each client, |ℓ(x; ξ) − ℓ(y; ξ)| ≤ Bm for all x, y ∈ X , and

Procedure. Alternate arrivals at each client are assigned to a training and a validation stream. For each of E fixed memory vectors h1, . . . , h E , a separate reset SGD state runs on the shared training array with disjoint batches within each solve; these experts never see validation samples or mixture weights. The server reports a convex mixture of the expert outputs. Fresh validation samples from each reporting interval score all experts, and two interleaved exponential-weight forecasters (for even and for odd intervals) ensure that a full interval of feedback has arrived before the forecaster that used it reports again. The aggregation principle is standard (Freund & Schapire, 1997); the independent split and a source-time correction make it valid here. Appendix H specifies the communication pipeline and proves the complete form of the following statement (Theorem H.2).

Temperature stations. Three stations of the U.S. Climate Reference Network (Diamond et al., 2013), chosen by insolation before any simulation, report every five minutes: a desert, a maritime site, and a site in polar night. A station’s population path is its diurnal cycle fitted to ten Decembers, and a draw adds a resampled day-to-day residual, so the drift and noise bounds hold by construction. The loss is nearly linear, and one round per refresh suffices ( K = 1 , where Algorithm 1 and SGD coincide). The desert cycle is fast, the maritime one slow, the polar path constant: g = 0 .32 , and the prescribed windows are 0.56 and 1.28 hours and the full history, which no single length can offer. Used without a multiplier, they reduce the gap by 51% against the bound-optimal common window and by 52% against one tuned on separate seeds (Figure 1, left). In July the cycles align and g falls to 0.066 . Against the bound-optimal common window the reduction is 6% , and the paired interval [−0.00158 , 0.00005] contains zero. Against the window tuned on separate seeds the reduction is 11% , with interval [−0.0025 , −0.0008] (right). Across 200 random station triplets the realized gain grows with g (Spearman 0.73 ), and for g near zero a tuned common window can win by a few percent: the comparison of upper bounds predicts both where client-specific memory pays and where it does not (Appendix J.1). 

Recorded order. Replayed in recorded order, held-out Decembers leave the model: persistent weather acts as drift, and the climatological inputs, which keep the whole history at the polar site, lose in every year to a common window tuned on the other nine. A drift input on the time scale of weather, computed on the training folds, matches that window without any search (Appendix J.2): what fails is the drift input, not the window rule. 

Clinics. Twelve clinics in six specialties share a logistic model with one intercept per clinic that predicts an emergency or inpatient encounter within 90 days from synthetic records (Walonoski et al., 2018). A cohort the model does not observe, patients with recent acute care, follows public influenza and COVID-19 surveillance curves, so the optimum moves at speeds that differ by two orders of magnitude across clinics, while the specialized clinics, which see the fewest patients, are stationary. The drift and noise inputs are evaluated along the exact optimal trajectory rather than certified, and the single scale of every memory is tuned on separate streams, so this experiment compares the shape of the memory, not its constant. Under exact minimization of the buffered risk, client-specific windows reduce the gap by 33% 

against the best common window, which is the full history, and by 26% against a common window with a per-clinic data floor, reaching 89% of the improvement of tuning all twelve windows. With Algorithm 1 ( K = 8 ) the optimization error dilutes every difference: the reduction is 16% against the best common window, and the data-floored common window comes within 1% (Appendix I). Where drift is seasonal and returns to its mean, the worst-case prescription is conservative: per-clinic tuning keeps the full history for pediatrics and primary care, where Corollary 4.2 forgets. 

## 8 Conclusion and limitations 

Client-specific memory separates the statistical uncertainty of stable clients from the forgetting that drifting clients require: Hγ is attainable with explicit finite-horizon costs, a lower construction with unequal drift clocks retains each group’s contribution, an exact periodic calculation separates common from client-specific windows within a specified estimator class, and independent validation competes with a finite family of fixed memories without any drift bound as input. Several limitations remain. Whether the J1/6 factor between the upper and lower bounds is necessary 8Beyond Aggregate Drift for unrestricted memory scales is open; adaptive aggregation needs a bounded-loss assumption, spends samples on validation, and may require many experts and full-history storage; the experiments are the station laws, the clinic network, and the controlled synthetic studies. Full history can be preferable under smooth, seasonal, or shared drift. Temporal dependence, efficient selection over large joint grids, and communication budgets that exclude raw-data pooling call for further analysis; the source-age extension assumes an unbiased oracle for the source time rather than inferring censored labels from silence.

## B Supporting statements 

This appendix collects the statements that the main text refers to but does not display: the raw-buffer implementation, the projected-SGD counterpart of Theorem 4.1, the general unequal-clock lower bound behind Corollary 5.1, the explicit regime in which the upper and lower bounds are comparable, and the source-time extension of the upper bounds. B.1 The raw-buffer implementation 

Messages are unrestricted, so clients with finitely encodable samples may upload their frozen buffers, timestamps, and counts in one synchronization at the start of each solve. The server then runs the same ordered batch updates of Algorithm 1 locally and either waits to reproduce the report times of the gradient-message implementation or reports after τ . The latter schedule uses c = τ and ∆ ≥ τ in the proofs, with unchanged sample-count floors and optimization error, so Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.2 hold for it with Kτ replaced by τ . The elapsed time Kτ therefore describes the gradient-message implementation and is not intrinsic to the model.

## I Clinics with different specialties: when forgetting pays 

The logistic study of Section 7 marks a boundary: along smooth population paths on which both the covariates and the label mechanism change, the best common memory is the full history, and client-specific windows bring no measurable benefit. That result says what the theory does not promise. This appendix asks the complementary question. What does a network of data sources look like in which forgetting does pay, and does the memory rule of Corollary 4.2 capture that benefit when the data are realistic and the solver is Algorithm 1? The answer we construct is a regional network of twelve clinics. Its acute services see many patients and follow epidemic waves; its specialized clinics see few patients from stable populations; and in between, cardiology drifts slowly as its population ages. The shared risk model is the same for all of them. With the solver held fixed, client-specific windows lower the time-averaged tracking error by 33% relative to the best single memory, and they attain almost nine tenths of what an oracle obtains by tuning every clinic’s window separately. 

I.1 The network 

Table 3 lists the clinics. There are six specialties, each with an urban and a rural site. All clinics estimate one quantity: the probability that a patient seen at a visit has an emergency or inpatient encounter within the following 90 days. They differ in three respects that matter for memory. The acute services , pediatrics, the emergency department and primary care, see between 25 and 120 patients a week. Their case mix changes with the season and, for the emergency department, with the waves of COVID-19. The drift is fast, but so is the flow of new data, so a short memory is affordable. The specialized clinics , endocrinology and oncology, see between one and a half and eight patients a week, and the populations they serve do not change over the three years we follow. Every discarded record costs them precision and buys nothing. Cardiology is the intermediate case: 15 to 40 patients a week and a population whose risk profile shifts slowly as it ages, with winter peaks on top. I.5 Memories, solvers, and evaluation 

Five memories are compared. One common window gives every clinic the same memory length, the comparator of the theory. Common window with a data floor uses one length but never lets a clinic’s window hold fewer than 100 of its records, which protects the logistic fit of small clinics from buffers with no events; it is a stronger practical baseline. 

Full history keeps everything. Client-specific windows use the shape hm ∝ pbm/a m of Corollary 4.2, with the full history for stationary clinics and the same data floor. The per-clinic oracle starts from the client-specific windows and adjusts each clinic’s window separately by a coordinate search; it is an upper benchmark, not a method. Each memory is combined with two solvers. Exact minimization of the buffered empirical risk isolates the quality of the memory. Algorithm 1 is the method of the paper: K = 8 accelerated rounds on disjoint batches, restarted at every solve from a public point, with solves every week and a round duration of 1/16 week. Its public point is the model fitted once on the held-out pilot patients. Every memory has one free scale, selected on six tuning streams; the evaluation uses twenty further independent streams, on which all memories receive the same patients at the same times. Because the objective is exact, the scale is selected by the true tracking error; this compares memories at their best scale and is not a data-driven procedure for choosing windows. The score is the time-averaged gap F t(xt) − min F t after the first eleven weeks, during which every memory reports the same public model.

I.7 Reading the results through the theory 

Theorem 4.1 charges a memory hm through two terms per clinic, a drift cost growing like amhm and a statistical cost falling like pbm/h m. Their balance is struck at hm ∝ pbm/a m, and it moves in opposite directions across this network: bm is largest in the small specialized clinics, am largest in the emergency departments. One common length has to serve both ends. Here it is pushed to the full history, because the stable clinics and the slowly drifting ones form most of the network, and the emergency departments then carry the drift cost of every wave. This is precisely the situation in which the statistical-drift scale Hγ of the client-specific rule falls well below the common-memory scale, since the drift–noise masses √ambm are spread over very different ratios bm/a m.The comparison with the data-floor baseline isolates what the rule adds. The floor 100 /λ m already makes the memory depend on the visit rate, which is one half of the prescription. It does not depend on drift. Under exact minimization this is decisive: the floored common window shortens the memory of pediatrics, cardiology and primary care to the same few weeks as the emergency departments and pays for it in noise, whereas Corollary 4.2 lengthens their windows in proportion to γ−1/2 

> m

. The remaining 26% is the value of accounting for drift, clinic by clinic. Where the prescription and the oracle part, the reason is the shape of the drift. The theorem is built for a worst-case drift that can keep moving in one direction, so the bias of an old record grows without bound. Seasonal drift returns to its average. For pediatrics and primary care, whose shares oscillate around a stable level, the bias of a long memory saturates at the amplitude of the season, and with a moderate flow of patients the full history is the better compromise. Cardiology, whose ageing trend does not revert, is the opposite case, and there the oracle keeps a finite window as the theory prescribes. The prescription is thus conservative exactly where the drift is mean-reverting, and it still captures most of the attainable gain. The solver changes the size of the effect but not its sign. Proposition B.1 makes precise that the memory scale does not depend on acceleration; what the solver contributes is the optimization remainder, which with K = 8 is comparable to 37 Beyond Aggregate Drift the statistical term and dilutes every difference between memories. Under Algorithm 1 the two memories that shorten the emergency departments’ windows, the data-floor baseline and the client-specific rule, therefore end up close, while both remain clearly better than any memory that treats the network as one. Section 7 and this experiment differ in how the optimum moves. There the population changes smoothly in both covariates and labels, and the best common memory is the full history. Here the optimum is moved by a cohort the model cannot see, at speeds that differ by two orders of magnitude from clinic to clinic. Heterogeneous drift bounds justify different memories in both settings; only in the second does the difference between memories carry a large price, and the client-specific rule then pays off by a third of the error. Client-specific memory also holds far fewer records: the emergency departments keep a week or two of patients instead of three years, so every solve touches a small fraction of the data that the full history requires. 

I.8 Scope 

The records are synthetic, and each clinic’s population is a mixture of two fixed cohorts whose shares follow real surveillance curves; the experiment tests the memory mechanism of the paper, not a clinical prediction rule. The drift and noise bounds are evaluated along the exact optimal trajectory, which is possible only because the objective is known; in deployment they would have to be certified or replaced by the validation-based selection of Section 6. The single free scale of each memory is chosen with the true tracking error, for every memory alike. Algorithm 1 is used unchanged, with a public starting point fitted once on patients who never enter the streams. 

## J Station temperature records 

This appendix runs Algorithm 1 on laws fitted to measured temperature records; Figure 1 in Section 7 summarizes it. Two experiments are reported. The first is inside the model of Section 3: day-to-day weather is independent noise around a fitted diurnal clock, the drift and noise bounds are attained by construction, and Corollary 4.2 is scored against a common window. The second replays held-out months in their recorded order, where weather persists, and asks whether the same corollary improves on a common length once its drift input matches that path.

The curvature is kept small so that the optimization remainder is negligible: the number of rounds is K = ⌈max {1, 2pLD/ Hγ }⌉ = 1 , the stride is ∆ = Kτ , and s0 = 2 K/λ min , the choices of Corollary B.3. The score inside the model is the population gap AvgErr T . On time-ordered months it is the prequential excess over the zero report: the average, over the recorded readings, of the loss of the current report on each reading as it arrives, minus the loss of the report x = 0 on the same reading; lower is better. 

J.1 Experiment I: inside the model 

The U.S. Climate Reference Network (Diamond et al., 2013) reports air temperature every five minutes. We take the ten Decembers 2010–2019 at three stations chosen by insolation before any simulation: Stovepipe Wells (desert), Sitka (maritime), and Utqiagvik, where the sun does not rise in December (polar night). The population path of a station is the truncated Fourier series of its average day; leave-one-December-out cross-validation selects 2, 3, and 0 harmonics, so the polar-night path is constant. Paths are centred, and paths and residuals are divided by one common scale, 4.56 ◦C;the bounds below are quoted in degrees, before this scaling, and the windows do not depend on it. The drift bounds are the largest slopes of the fitted paths, (1 .58 , 0.354 , 0) ◦C per hour. A draw at a slot is a reading of a uniformly chosen December day at that slot, minus the slot mean, plus the fitted path. J.2 Experiment II: time-ordered records 

The resampled law treats day-to-day weather as independent noise. To see what that choice hides, each December is held out in turn; the law is fitted on the other nine years; and the held-out month is replayed in its recorded order for T = 744 

hours. A common length is tuned on the nine training months replayed the same way. The climatological windows, which treat weather as noise, lose to that tuned length in all ten years: mean −0.167 against −0.238 , paired difference 

0.071 with interval [0 .037 , 0.104] . They keep the full history at the polar site, whereas exponentially weighted memories tuned per station on the training years keep less than an hour there: in recorded order, persistent weather acts as drift. Corollary 4.2 takes (γm, σ m) as inputs. Two other certificates are scored on the same folds, both fixed on the training folds before their held-out scores were computed. The stream-scale pair uses five-minute differences for noise and the largest hourly-mean slope for drift. Polar night is then the fastest client. Every window binds at the count floor 2K/λ = 1 /6 hour, personalization vanishes, and the score is −0.231 against −0.238 for the tuned length. The two-scale pair keeps the climatological noise and sets γm to the sum of the clock Lipschitz constant and the largest change of successive 12 -hour block means of the climate residual, per hour, a scale above the hour-scale windows and below a day.
