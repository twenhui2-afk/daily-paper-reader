Among the most striking predictions of General Relativity are black holes: regions of spacetime bounded by an event horizon, from which nothing, not even light, can escape, and which, in classical theory, contain spacetime singularities. Understanding the motion of freely falling test bodies and light rays in black-hole spacetimes is both a fundamental problem of general relativity and an indispensable tool of relativistic astrophysics. Historically, geodesics provided the first contact between the theory and obser-vation. The anomalous advance of Mercury’s perihelion is the leading relativistic correction to bound timelike orbits around the Sun, and the deflection of starlight measured in 1919 is the weak-field limit of null geodesics in the same geometry. These classic tests, however, probe the weak-field regime, where the departure from New-tonian motion is small. Close to a black hole, the situation is qualitatively different. Circular orbits of light exist; there is an innermost stable circular orbit, inside which no bound motion is possible; particles can whirl around the hole many times before escaping or plunging; and in the Kerr geometry, orbits are no longer confined to a plane, while the ergoregion admits states of negative energy. These strong-field features are no longer of purely theoretical interest. The images of M87 ∗ and Sgr A ∗ obtained by the Event Horizon Telescope show a bright ring whose size is closely tied to the critical curve traced by unstable photon orbits. The same orbits govern the relativistic images of strong gravitational lensing and, in the eikonal limit, the quasinormal-mode spectrum of the black hole. The innermost stable circular orbit sets the inner edge of thin accretion disks, and with it the radiative efficiency of accretion and the X-ray methods used to measure black-hole spins. Extreme-mass-ratio inspirals, among the main targets of the LISA mission, are modeled to leading order as a slow drift through a sequence of Kerr geodesics, so that the emitted waveform encodes the fundamental frequencies of the orbits the small body traverses. In all these applications, what matters first is the qualitative structure of the motion: which kinds of orbits exist for given constants of motion, where the transitions between them occur, and what happens at the boundaries. As we shall see, this structure is entirely encoded in the roots of the polynomial governing the radial motion and, for the Kerr geometry, in the roots of the one describing the polar motion. Systematic treatments of geodesic motion can be found in the standard textbooks by Weinberg [1972], Misner et al. [1973], Chandrasekhar [1983], and Frolov and Zel-nikov [2011], to which we refer the reader for a comprehensive account. This monograph investigates the geodesics of three black-hole geometries, namely the Schwarzschild, Kerr, and Schwarzschild–de Sitter ones, by employing a different, algebraic approach. The aim is not to solve for the geodesic trajectories explicitly or numerically, but to classify the possible motions of test particles through their turning 1points. This amounts to studying the pattern of the roots of the polynomials that appear in the geodesic equations. To achieve this, the key algebraic tool adopted in this monograph is provided by Vieta’s formulae, which relate the aforementioned roots to the constants of motion. Together with these formulae, the employment of Descartes’ rule of signs also proves to be extremely useful. This project stemmed from an idea that came to my mind when I was a PhD student studying Chandrasekhar’s book. There, the root pattern of a polynomial was treated in relation to Kerr geodesics, but I felt that it was not being fully exploited. Then came Vieta’s formulae, which I found baffling at first. But, thinking about it, if one just needs the qualitative features of a motion and, in particular, of its trajectory, then it makes sense that some information can be drawn from the coefficients of a polynomial alone. I thought that the idea was not completely trivial and, at least, fun to pursue. I could not always find the time to do that, though, so the project was developed intermittently over many years. Only recently, with the help of Claude, was I able to go the last mile, especially in producing the plots that I hope the reader will enjoy. The content and the idea, however, are the same as they were many years ago, when the only AIs I knew were R. Daneel Olivaw and HAL 9000.

We use geometrized units G = c = 1 , the metric signature is (−, +, +, +) , Greek indices run over the four spacetime coordinates, and repeated indices are summed over. Along a geodesic, λ denotes the proper time for a massive particle and an affine parameter for a photon; a dot denotes differentiation with respect to λ.We only consider the region outside the event horizon, the domain of outer com-munication, unless stated otherwise; in Schwarzschild–de Sitter spacetime, this is the static region between the event horizon and the cosmological horizon; turning points inside a horizon do not belong to the exterior classification. 5Chapter 1 Introduction 

Thanks to the constants of motion, the geodesic equations in the Schwarzschild and Kerr spacetimes can be reduced to first-order differential equations in which the square of the radial or polar velocity equals a polynomial. In Schwarzschild spacetime, the radial polynomial is cubic; in Kerr spacetime, it is quartic, accompanied by a quadratic polynomial that governs the polar motion; in Schwarzschild–de Sitter spacetime, the cosmological constant raises the degree of the timelike radial polynomial to five. The coefficients of these polynomials depend only on the constants of motion, and their real roots are the candidate turning points , where the radial or polar velocity vanishes and the motion may reverse. The qualitative behavior of a geodesic, whether it stays bound, escapes to infinity, or falls into the black hole, is therefore encoded in where these roots lie. The roots can be computed in closed form, but the resulting expressions are rarely the clearest route to a classification. This book follows a different route and reads the qualitative motion directly off the coefficients. The main tool is provided by Vieta’s formulae, which express the sums and products of the roots in terms of the constants of motion. These relations alone can tell whether the number of negative roots is even or odd, exclude entire configurations of roots, or show that the roots cannot all be real. Two further classical tools complete the picture: the discriminant, which decides whether the roots are real, and Descartes’ rule of signs, which counts how many of them lie beyond a given point, such as the event horizon. Together with the values of the polynomials at the horizon and at infinity, these tools classify the generic motions without computing a single root. Not every real root is a turning point, however. A root is physically relevant only if it lies in the region under consideration, outside the event horizon for the radial motion and between the poles for the polar one, and if the other separated equation admits real motion for the same constants of motion. Roots inside the horizon, negative roots, and complex roots still enter Vieta’s formulae, and they carry useful information, but they are not places where the motion turns around. In Kerr spacetime, there are constants of motion for which the radial polynomial has roots outside the horizon, but no polar motion is possible, and no geodesic exists at all. The Schwarzschild problem provides a transparent introduction because spherical symmetry confines every geodesic to a plane. The Kerr problem is richer: axial sym-metry leaves only one manifest component of angular momentum, while the hidden symmetry discovered by Carter supplies a further constant and separates the radial and polar motions [Carter, 1968b,a]. Classic classifications and analytic treatments 6include those of Wilkins [1972], Schmidt [2002], Drasco and Hughes [2004], and Fujita and Hikida [2009]. Finally, the Schwarzschild–de Sitter problem brings back spherical symmetry but adds a cosmological horizon and a quintic radial polynomial, for which the roots cannot be written in general by radicals: there, reading the motion off the coefficients is not only convenient but practically necessary. The plan of the book is as follows. Chapter 2 introduces Vieta’s formulae, together with the discriminant and Descartes’ rule of signs; it then defines root patterns and turning points. Chapter 3 classifies Schwarzschild geodesics, first through the effec-tive potential and then through the roots of the radial cubic. Chapter 4 treats Kerr geodesics: the polar motion, the radial quartic in each energy regime, spherical and equatorial circular orbits, and the photon shell. Chapter 5 treats Schwarzschild–de Sit-ter geodesics: the horizons and the static radius, the inner and outer stable circular orbits, and the root patterns of the radial quintic. Each of these chapters ends with worked examples in which the predicted root patterns are compared with numerically computed roots. Chapter 6 summarizes the results. 7Chapter 2 Vieta’s formulae and turning points 

In all the spacetimes studied in this book, the geodesic equations can be reduced to first-order, non-linear differential equations of the form ˙x2 = P (x), one for each separated coordinate, where P is a polynomial whose coefficients depend on the con-stants of motion. Everything that is qualitatively important about the motion, namely where it is allowed, where it turns back, and whether it can stay at a constant radius, is encoded in the roots of P . The strategy of this book is to extract this information without computing the roots. This chapter provides the two tools that make this possible. The first is algebraic: Vieta’s formulae (Section 2.1) relate the symmetric combinations of the roots to the coefficients, and hence to the constants of motion. In this way, they constrain the possible configurations of the roots, which we call root patterns (Section 2.3). The second is dynamical: the definition of a turning point (Section 2.4) states which roots correspond to an actual reversal of the motion, and how multiple roots describe orbits at constant radius and their stability. Two further algebraic tools, the discriminant and Descartes’ rule of signs (Section 2.2), complement Vieta’s formulae by determining whether the roots are real and how many of them lie beyond a given point, such as the event horizon. 

# 2.1 Vieta’s formulae 

François Viète (1540–1603), Latinized as Franciscus Vieta, was a French lawyer and mathematician. Born in Fontenay-le-Comte, in Poitou, he spent most of his life in public service as a member of the Parlement of Brittany and later as a royal councilor to Henri III and Henri IV. Mathematics occupied his free time, and his contributions were lasting. In his In artem analyticem isagoge (1591), he introduced the systematic use of letters for both unknown and known quantities, vowels for the former and consonants for the latter. For the first time, general equations could be written and manipulated, rather than particular numerical examples. This logistica speciosa is the ancestor of modern algebraic notation. Viète also gave the first infinite product representation of π, and during the wars of Religion, he broke the ciphers of intercepted Spanish dispatches for Henri IV so successfully that Philip II of Spain is said to have accused him of sorcery. The relations between the roots and the coefficients of a polynomial that now bear his name appear in De aequationum recognitione et emendatione , published posthu-8mously in 1615 by his pupil Alexander Anderson. Viète considered positive roots only. The general statement, valid for all roots, including negative and what were then called “impossible” (complex) roots, was given by Albert Girard in his Invention nouvelle en l’algèbre (1629), which is why the formulae are sometimes attributed to both. Vieta’s formulae constrain the symmetric functions of the roots but do not, by them-selves, tell whether the roots are real or where they lie. Two classical tools complete the picture: the discriminant, which decides whether the roots are real, and Descartes’ rule of signs, which counts how many of them lie beyond a given point. Both are poly-nomial functions of the coefficients, and hence of the constants of motion, so they share the main virtue of Vieta’s formulae: no root needs to be computed. Standard references are Uspensky [1948] and, for the quartic criteria, Rees [1922].

## 2.2.2 Descartes’ rule of signs 

The rule appears in the third book of René Descartes’s La Géométrie (Leiden, 1637), published as an appendix to the Discours de la méthode . Descartes gave it without proof, as a statement about how many “true” (positive) and “false” (negative) roots an equation can have: at most as many true roots as there are changes of sign in the sequence of coefficients, and at most as many false roots as there are repetitions of sign. When no coefficient vanishes, replacing x by −x turns every change of sign into a repetition and vice versa, so the second half is equivalent to applying the first to P (−x).When coefficients are missing, it can fail: x2 −1 has no repetition of sign, yet x = −1 is a root. The modern formulation, which counts the sign changes of P (−x), avoids the problem. Newton restated the rule, again without proof, in his Arithmetica universalis 

(1707). A first demonstration was given by Johann Andreas Segner in a dissertation (Jena, 1728) whose title attributes the rule to Thomas Harriot, and the first proof generally regarded as rigorous is due to Jean-Paul de Gua de Malves, in a memoir for the Paris Academy of Sciences (early 1740s). De Gua’s argument compares the positive roots of a polynomial with those of its derivative, and the proof given below follows the same idea. Gauss later published a short algebraic proof ( Beweis eines algebraischen Lehrsatzes , Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik 3, 1828). For historical remarks and a sharpened form of the rule, which shows that nothing more can be inferred about the positive roots from the signs of the coefficients alone, see Albouy and Fu [2014].

# 2.3 Root patterns 

By the root pattern of a polynomial, we mean the qualitative configuration of its roots: how many are real and how many form complex-conjugate pairs, the signs and ordering of the real roots, their multiplicities, and their position relative to the boundary of the physical domain. Examples are 0 < r 1 < r 2 < r 3, or one negative root and a complex-conjugate pair. The classification of geodesics in this book is, at its core, a classification of the possible root patterns of the radial and polar polynomials. The Schwarzschild solution [Schwarzschild, 1916] is, by Birkhoff’s theorem, locally the unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein’s equations [see, e.g., Wein-berg, 1972; Frolov and Zelnikov, 2011]. In its black-hole interpretation, the exterior region is r > r H = 2 M , where rH denotes the event horizon. The geodesic problem has been studied since the earliest years of general relativity. Classic analyses include those of Hagihara [1931], who classified the orbits according to the distribution of the roots of the radial cubic, and of Darwin [1959, 1961], who located the turning points using the rule of signs and the sum of the roots. Several of the algebraic results of this chapter appear, in a different notation, in their work. The chapter is organized as follows. Section 3.1 derives the geodesic equations and reduces the radial motion to a one-dimensional problem. Section 3.2 explains how this problem is solved qualitatively with an effective potential, and Section 3.3 applies the method to massive particles. Section 3.4 obtains the same classification from Vieta’s formulae, and Section 3.5 treats photons using both methods.

# 3.4 Timelike geodesics from Vieta’s formulae 

We now derive the same classification by a different route, working with the roots of the radial polynomial instead of the shape of the potential. In Schwarzschild spacetime, the two methods are equivalent, and comparing them is the best way to see what Vi-eta’s formulae achieve. ## 4.2.1 Time orientation and frame dragging 

For a future-directed geodesic outside the horizon, Eq. (4.13) must be positive along the allowed orbit. Near the horizon, this implies the invariant condition 

ε − ΩHLz ≥ 0 . (4.23) Away from the horizon, the same argument applied to the ZAMOs, whose four-velocity is proportional to ∂t + ω ∂ φ, gives the stronger condition

Negative-energy geodesics can therefore occur only inside the ergoregion and, since ω > 0 for a > 0, Eq. (4.24) requires Lz < ε/ω < 0.This is the kinematic basis of the Penrose process [Penrose, 1969; Penrose and Floyd, 1971]. A particle that enters the ergoregion and splits into two fragments can place one of them on a negative-energy geodesic, which must eventually cross the horizon; by conservation of ε, the other fragment escapes to infinity with more energy than the incoming particle. The surplus is drawn from the rotation of the black hole. When the negative-energy fragment is captured, the mass and angular momentum of the hole change by δM = ε and δJ = Lz of that fragment, and Eq. (4.23) gives 

δM ≥ ΩH δJ , (4.25) so energy can be extracted only together with angular momentum. This inequality was derived by Penrose and Floyd [1971], who noted that it expresses the increase of the 36 area of the horizon. For a single decay, the gain is appreciable only if the fragments separate at relativistic relative speeds [Wald, 1974]; for matter coming from stable orbits, Bardeen et al. [1972] estimated that more than about half the speed of light is needed. Collisions offer a second route to high energies. For two particles with four-momenta p1 and p2 meeting at the same event, the center-of-mass energy is 

E2cm := −(p1 + p2)μ(p1 + p2)μ . (4.26) Bañados et al. [2009] found that for an extremal black hole Ecm grows without bound as the collision point approaches the horizon, provided one of the particles has the critical angular momentum Lz = 2 M ε . In the present notation, the critical condition is ε−ΩHLz = 0 , the equality case of Eq. (4.23), which for a = M , where ΩH = 1 /(2 M ),reduces to the value above. For generic particles, Ecm remains finite at the horizon; it diverges only when one of the colliding particles approaches criticality. This BSW effect is subject to strong kinematic restrictions: in the extremal case, the critical particle needs an infinite proper time to reach the horizon, while for a < M , critical particles arriving from infinity have a radial turning point outside the horizon, so that 

Ecm stays bounded [Berti et al., 2009; Jacobson and Sotiriou, 2010]. Grib and Pavlov [2011] showed that arbitrarily large center-of-mass energies are nevertheless possible for non-extremal black holes when the near-critical angular momentum is acquired in a previous collision close to the horizon [see also Grib et al., 2011, 2012]. They also found that Ecm can grow without bound anywhere in the ergoregion, if one of the particles has a large negative angular momentum [Grib and Pavlov, 2013], and studied collisions involving particles of zero or negative energy [Grib and Pavlov, 2017]. Whether a large 

Ecm translates into energetic particles at infinity is a separate question: the escaping products of a single collision carry only a modest multiple of the incoming energy [Bejger et al., 2012; Leiderschneider and Piran, 2016]. A review is given by Harada and Kimura [2014]. The sign of Lz alone does not determine the sign of dφ/ dλ everywhere. dφ/ dt > 0 for a > 0. Zeros of Eq. (4.14) are therefore turning points of the Boyer– Lindquist azimuth only: they carry no information beyond the constants of motion and play no role in the classification.

# 4.5 Multiple roots, spherical orbits, and stability 

The root patterns of the previous sections can change in only two ways: two roots meet, or a root crosses the horizon. The first event is the vanishing of the discriminant, 

∆4 = 0 (Section 2.2); the second is R(rH) = 0 , that is, ε = Ω HLz by Eq. (4.42). Multiple roots are therefore the boundaries of the classification in the space of constants of motion, and they also carry geodesics of their own. Differentiating (d r/ dγ)2 = R(r)

gives d2r/ dγ2 = R′(r)/2, so r = rs is a solution if and only if 

R(rs) = 0 , R′(rs) = 0 . (4.63) Such orbits are called spherical ; they need not remain at constant θ. Equatorial circular orbits are the special case Q = 0 , θ = π/ 2. The Schwarzschild–de Sitter, or Kottler, solution [Kottler, 1918] describes a spherically symmetric black hole in a universe with a positive cosmological constant Λ. Far from the hole, it approaches the de Sitter spacetime, and it has two horizons: the event horizon of the black hole and a cosmological horizon, beyond which the repulsion due to Λ dominates. Its horizons, its static radius, and its circular orbits with their stability were analyzed by Stuchlík and Hledík [1999], building on earlier work by Stuchlík; most of the results of Sections 5.1–5.3 can be found there. The complete analytic solution of the geodesic equation, in terms of hyperelliptic functions, was given by Hackmann and Lämmerzahl [2008], who also classified the orbits by the number of positive zeros of the radial polynomial. We return to spherical symmetry with the tools of the previous chapters. The treatment follows Chapter 3, and most results reduce to the Schwarzschild ones as 

Λ → 0. What changes is the algebra: the radial polynomial of timelike geodesics becomes a quintic, for which no general formula in radicals exists, and the region of physical interest is bounded by two horizons instead of one horizon and infinity. Section 5.1 locates the horizons and the static radius, Section 5.2 adapts the geodesic equations and the effective potential, and Section 5.3 classifies the timelike motion with the potential. Section 5.4 obtains the same classification from Vieta’s formulae and Descartes’ rule of signs, Section 5.5 treats photons, and Section 5.6 closes with worked examples.

The aim of this monograph was to show how far one can go in classifying black-hole geodesics without solving for the turning points. The answer is: very far. Vieta’s formulae, supplemented by the parity of the number of exterior roots, the rule of signs, the discriminant, and the values of the radial polynomial at r = 0 , at the horizon, and at infinity, determine the qualitative behavior of every generic geodesic in the domain of outer communication of the Schwarzschild, Kerr, and Schwarzschild– de Sitter black holes. Several of these results were known: some go back to Hagihara [1931], Darwin [1959, 1961] and Wilkins [1972], the complete Kerr radial taxonomy is due to Compère et al. [2022], and the Schwarzschild–de Sitter horizons and circular orbits were analyzed by Stuchlík and Hledík [1999]. What the method adds is a single, transparent route to all of them. The main results can be summarized as follows. 1. Schwarzschild spacetime. The radial cubic admits only two generic root patterns in each energy regime. Two negative roots and one positive root are excluded for ε2 < 1, and a positive root with a complex pair is excluded for null geodesics. Every positive root lies outside the horizon, so the discriminant alone decides the motion. The reciprocals of the three roots always add up to 1/(2 M ),Eq. (3.38). This gives the third root of a bound orbit in closed form and, with it, the separatrix p = 6 + 2 e, as in Darwin [1961]. The ISCO is the triple root, and the critical photon impact parameter bc = 3 √3 M is the zero of the discriminant. 2. Kerr spacetime: polar motion. The endpoint values F (0) = Q and F (1) = 

−L2

, together with the Vieta relations of the polar quadratic, give the three regimes Q > 0, Q = 0 and Q < 0. Bound timelike geodesics have Q ≥ 0

[Wilkins, 1972]. Vortical motion requires A > 0, 0 < B < 2A and Dθ ≥ 0,Eq. (4.35); for massive particles, A > 0 means ε2 > 1. Because the polar function is linear in its “energy” Q, the same classification has a transparent effective-potential reading. 3. Kerr spacetime: radial motion. For ε2 ≥ 1 there are at most two exterior turning points: for ε2 > 1 because four positive roots would have a negative sum, and for ε2 = 1 because the radial function is a cubic with an even number of exterior roots. Hence no radially bound orbit exists, as Wilkins [1972] first showed with the rule of signs. For ε2 < 1 the number of exterior roots is odd, and at least one root lies inside the Cauchy horizon, because R > 0 between 74 the two horizons. A positive discriminant is necessary for bound motion but not sufficient: three of the four real roots may lie inside the Cauchy horizon. The exact criterion is Descartes’ rule applied at the horizon, which counts the exterior roots once the discriminant has shown them to be real. The separatrix of bound motion is the locus where the periapsis meets the turning point of the horizon-connected branch. 4. Multiple roots and stability. Spherical orbits are the double roots of R, and their stability is decided by the position of the other two roots. Every spherical orbit with ε2 ≥ κ, including every spherical photon orbit, is unstable. In the equatorial plane, Vieta’s formulae alone give the circular-orbit constants, the stability criterion ε2 < 1 − 2M/ (3 rc), the ISCO equation as a triple root, and the marginally bound radii. 5. Vortical geodesics. The coefficient B that makes vortical polar motion possible is also the coefficient of r2 in R. Every coefficient of the radial polynomial is then non-negative, with a positive constant term, so vortical geodesics, whether timelike or null, have no radial turning point outside the horizon, in agreement with Compère et al. [2022]. Since they reach infinity, vortical timelike geodesics have ε > 1.6. Photons. The equatorial light rings follow from Vieta’s formulae, and spherical photon orbits fill the shell between them. Their impact parameters form the part of the discriminant locus of R on which the double root lies outside the horizon, the boundary between capture and scattering; for every spin, η ≤ 27 M 2, with equality only for the orbit at rs = 3 M [Teo, 2003]. 7. Schwarzschild–de Sitter spacetime. The horizon cubic has roots that add up to zero, and its discriminant shows that a black hole requires 9Λ M 2 < 1. The timelike radial polynomial is a quintic, which always has exactly one negative root and whose positive roots all lie between the two horizons, in even number. Only three generic patterns remain: transit between the horizons, a horizon-connected branch with a cosmological one, and the same with a bound branch, which requires ε2 + Λ L2/3 < 1. The reciprocals of the five roots still add up to 1/(2 M ), and the triple roots are the innermost and outermost stable circular orbits, both beyond 6M . Null geodesics reduce to the Schwarzschild case with an effective impact parameter. The method has clear limits. Vieta’s formulae are necessary conditions on a root pattern. Turning them into a classification requires locating the roots with respect to the horizon; in Kerr spacetime this is the role of Descartes’ rule at the horizon, without which the discriminant can mislead. The analysis covers the generic cases: horizon roots, R(rH) = 0 , and multiple roots require separate treatment, and the complete radial taxonomy, including all non-generic cases, is given by Compère et al. [2022]. The radial potential, which is quadratic in ε with a frame-dragging cross term, is useful as a picture in the equatorial plane but not as a classification tool. Finally, the detailed trajectories, the fundamental frequencies and the precession rates need the explicit elliptic-function solutions [Fujita and Hikida, 2009; Gralla and Lupsasca, 2020]. 75 The same approach applies to any spacetime whose geodesic equations separate into polynomial radial and polar parts. The most immediate extensions are the extremal Kerr black hole, a = M , where the two horizons merge and the Cauchy-horizon bounds used above degenerate, and the Kerr naked singularity, a > M , where there is no horizon and the sign of R(0) = −a2Q becomes the key boundary value. Beyond Kerr, the Kerr–Newman family keeps a quartic radial polynomial but changes its coefficients, while in the Kerr–de Sitter family the timelike radial polynomial becomes a sextic. Schwarzschild–de Sitter spacetime with a negative cosmological constant, which has no cosmological horizon and confines all timelike motion, is a natural companion to Chapter 5. In all these cases the logic of the classification stays the same: fix the constants of motion, read off the symmetric functions of the roots, and let the signs decide. 76 Bibliography
