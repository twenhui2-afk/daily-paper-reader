# Parametric Resonance in Primal-Dual Learning for Slowly Non-Stationary Constrained MDPs 

Maxime Baelde maxime@antvoice.com Antvoice, Paris, France 

# Abstract 

Primal-dual methods for constrained Markov decision processes are analyzed under a two-timescale assumption, with non-stationarity entering through two channels. In the additive 

channel, where the constraint level oscillates, the dual oscillation saturates at a floor set by the moving optimum and is amplified linearly by the timescale ratio. In the paramet-ric channel, where the constraint sensitivity oscillates, a coherent modulation carries no resonant forcing at any depth: the dual update’s stiffness modulation cancels the primal update’s damping modulation. Instability instead comes from a mismatch between the con-straint estimate used by the primal update and the measurement used by the dual update, producing a parametric resonance at twice the pair’s natural frequency. The tolerated mis-match grows with the square root of the forcing bandwidth and collapses with the square root of the dual-to-primal step-size ratio. These results hold on the tangent linear-quadratic reduction around an active saddle, for one constraint and for K constraints under a com-mon modulation and an isotropy condition. Driving both updates from the same estimate removes the resonant forcing but introduces a stationary offset equal to the estimator’s bias. In a Lagrangian soft actor-critic agent, the multiplier locks onto the sub-harmonic at the timescale ratios its identified linear loop predicts and a coherent cost critic suppresses the lock-in, while a proportional (PID) term, predicted to leave the lock-in in place, suppresses most of it through the projection of the multiplier. 

# 1 Introduction 

Constrained Markov decision processes (Altman, 1999) are the standard model for sequential decision prob-lems in which a return has to be maximized subject to a long-run budget, risk or safety requirement. The dominant solution method, alongside the trust-region approach of Achiam et al. (2017), is Lagrangian re-laxation: a multiplier weights the constraint, the policy is improved against the resulting unconstrained objective, and the multiplier is driven by the observed constraint violation. The resulting primal-dual loop is what almost every deployed constrained learner actually runs, from safe reinforcement learning benchmarks (Ray et al., 2019) and libraries (Ji et al., 2024) to budget pacing in industrial recommendation and real-time bidding (Balseiro & Gur, 2019; Balseiro et al., 2020). Its appeal is that each half is a familiar object, a policy gradient step and a projected subgradient step, with convergence argued by placing the two on separate timescales (Borkar, 2008; 2005; Bhatnagar & Lakshmanan, 2012). The application that motivates this paper is budget pacing in real-time bidding. An agent bids on impressions and must spend a target budget over a day, so the constraint is a spend rate and the multiplier is the shadow price of budget. The environment is non-stationary on a daily cycle: traffic volume, competitive pressure and conversion propensity all move by large factors between night and evening peak. Convergence in a stationary environment settles nothing here; the practitioner’s question is whether the multiplier tracks the moving constrained optimum or oscillates around it, and what the learning rates have to do with the answer. Two expectations recur in practice. The first is that a slowly varying environment can be handled by making the dual update slow enough to average over it. The second is that the timescale separation invoked to justify the analysis also makes the loop robust. Both fail on the tangent linear-quadratic reduction that the rest of 1arXiv:2610.23788v1 [stat.ML] 16 Oct 2026 the paper analyzes, defined in Section 3.1. The non-stationarity of the constraint level produces a bounded oscillation with a floor that no step size removes, and the non-stationarity of the constraint sensitivity carries no resonant forcing at all as long as the primal and the dual see the same sensitivity. What destabilizes the loop is the mismatch between two estimates of the same quantity, an implementation property internal to the algorithm, and the timescale separation is what amplifies it. Constrained reinforcement learning writes a single symbol for both channels: the primal update consumes a constraint estimate , differentiable in the policy parameter, and the dual update consumes a constraint 

measurement . Section 2 positions this split against the feedback-optimization and CMDP literatures; what neither considers is a periodic mismatch between the two objects, which is what this paper adds. We separate the two channels formally in Definition 1. In the additive channel the constraint level oscil-lates and the linearized dynamics stay time-invariant. In the parametric channel the constraint sensitivity oscillates, the coefficients become time-periodic, and the loop admits exponential growth once the mismatch crosses a threshold set by the damping; this is the realistic channel in real-time bidding (Section 3.4). Align-ing the two objects is the design prescription that follows: if a single constraint estimate drives both updates, the resonant forcing vanishes by construction at every modulation depth. Alignment is impossible when the dual has to be driven by an audited measurement while the primal needs a differentiable surrogate. In that case the tolerated mismatch scales as the square root of the ratio of the primal to the dual step size. Lowering the dual step size therefore widens the tolerance, but it also slows the multiplier’s tracking of a moving constraint. Notation is collected in Table 4 (Appendix A.1). The central fact is structural and holds to first order on the continuous surrogate of the coupled dynamics: on the tangent linear-quadratic reduction of a K = 1 CMDP around an active constrained saddle point, the primal update modulates damping and stiffness together while the dual update modulates stiffness alone (Lemma 1). A common modulation of both channels therefore cancels exactly at the principal tongue, and only the mismatch ελ − εθ survives as an effective forcing depth. Three results follow on this reduction, and the resonance follows from classical tools applied to that effective depth; Section 7 compares each with the affine recursion. (R1) Exact tracking of a moving optimum (Proposition 1(i)). The DC gain from constraint distur-bance to multiplier is exactly a/ (αc 2), precisely what is needed to track the instantaneous optimal multiplier λ⋆(t) = ab t/c 2. The peak-to-peak excursion 2 Aa/c 2 is therefore the size of that moving target, independently of both step sizes. This refutes, in the coupled system, the natural extrap-olation of the frozen-policy bound of Baelde (2026), which grows as O(α/ω ) as the forcing slows down. (R2) Worst-case amplification by the timescale separation (Proposition 1(ii)). Maximizing over the forcing frequency, the peak-to-peak dual oscillation is Aα/σ , reducing at leading order to (2 Aa/c 2) max {1, (2 ζ)−2} with ζ the reduced damping and (2 ζ)−2 = rc 2/a 2. Past r = a2/c 2, growing timescale separation amplifies the worst case linearly in r.(R3) Coherent sensitivity modulation cannot destabilize the loop (Proposition 2). If the primal and the dual see the same modulated sensitivity the resonant forcing vanishes identically at every depth, waveform and frequency. The weighted energy αθ 2 + ηλ 2 is then a Lyapunov function for the continuous flow, and the top Floquet exponent is strictly negative without any smallness assumption. What remains is a damping-borne pump bounded by σJ 1(ε), giving exponent −σ(1 − J1(ε)) at the principal tongue. The recursion stays stable at every admissible depth under a condition on the step sizes alone. Section 5 sharpens (R2) into a quantitative threshold: Theorem 1 gives the narrowband mismatch threshold

Section 7.3 carries the predictions to a Lagrangian soft actor-critic agent: the multiplier locks onto the sub-harmonic at the timescale ratios the agent’s identified linear loop predicts, a coherent cost critic suppresses the lock-in, and, contrary to its linear prediction, the proportional term of a PID Lagrangian 2agent suppresses most lock-ins through the saturation of the multiplier’s projection while leaving the forced response and most of the return loss in place. These statements hold on the tangent reduction, not for general CMDPs: Section 3.1 states what the scalar reduction assumes, the projection in (3) is inactive throughout the analysis and enters only the experiments, and Section 8 delimits the scope further. 

# 2 Related work 

The continuous-time primal-dual method itself is due to Arrow et al. (1958); Platt & Barr (1988) write it as a damped second-order oscillator, a reduction that Stooke et al. (2020) build explicitly on to motivate PID Lagrangian control: eliminating the multiplier from ˙ x = −∇ f −λ∇g and ˙λ = αg (x) yields ¨ x+D ˙x+αg (x)∇g =0 with D = ∇2f +λ∇2g, an oscillator whose damping is a weighted sum of Hessians. Feijer & Paganini (2010) analyze the stability of exactly this class of primal-dual gradient dynamics via Lyapunov and invariance arguments, characterizing when the undamped oscillatory regime persists. What that lineage writes is a 

single constraint function consumed by both updates. A second lineage, feedback and time-varying optimization, already keeps the two objects separate. Bernstein et al. (2019) and Colombino et al. (2020) drive a primal-dual recursion from a model-based gradient while a measurement of the controlled plant enters through feedback, surveyed as a general design principle by Hauswirth et al. (2024). Simonetto et al. (2020) and Tang et al. (2022) bound the tracking error of running primal-dual and gradient methods against a time-varying optimum under the same estimate/measurement asymmetry. Reward-constrained policy optimization (Tessler et al., 2019) shares that same split from the constrained-RL side, shaping the reward against a differentiable surrogate cost while updating the multiplier slowly from a Monte Carlo return, an estimate/measurement pair rather than a continuous feedback loop. None of these papers considers a periodic mismatch between the two channels, or exhibits the resonance it opens in a primal-dual recursion with inertia. Where a tracking guarantee is proved, as in Simonetto et al. (2020) and Tang et al. (2022), it bounds the error against a drifting optimum and leaves the sign of a Floquet exponent unaddressed. Hauswirth et al. (2024) surveys the design principle without proving such a bound. What we add is dynamical: periodicity turns the mismatch structure just described into a resonant forcing on the oscillator introduced above. The proportional term of PID Lagrangian control acts on that same oscillator by adding damping, mitigating a resonant forcing once it exists without removing its source. The convergence mechanism of the canonical two-timescale theorem (Borkar, 2008) is a quasi-static argument, comparing the fast variable’s interpolated trajectory to that of the frozen-coefficient ODE (the same ODE-comparison mechanism underlies the two-timescale stochastic approximation of Benveniste et al. (1990)). Stability of a frozen-coefficient family does not imply stability of the time-varying system: the textbook counterexample is Markus & Yamabe (1960)’s matrix A(t), with spectrum in the open left half-plane at every fixed t yet an unstable ˙ x = A(t)x (Khalil, 2002). Rohrs et al. (1985) document the same failure mode in adaptive control: a loop that is stable under every frozen-parameter analysis can burst into instability once unmodeled high-frequency dynamics are excited by persistent, structured input. Parametric resonance in the primal-dual loop is an instance of that known phenomenon. What remains to supply is which physical quantity opens the tongue here, why the coherent case is protected by an exact cancellation, and what the criterion becomes under broadband forcing. Extremum-seeking control deliberately engineers a related resonance, superposing a periodic dither on a slow adaptation loop and analyzing its stability (Krstić & Wang, 2000); there the periodicity is the control input, here an exogenous property of the environment. The line of work descending from Borkar (2005), continued by Bhatnagar & Lakshmanan (2012) and Panda & Bhatnagar (2024), places the multiplier on a timescale slower than the primal, the side on which the pair has no oscillatory mode to resonate through. Section 3.3 shows that deployed implementations sit on the other side. Paternain et al. (2019) establish zero duality gap for the constrained policy optimization problem itself, a guarantee on the value attained at the optimum. It says nothing about the stability of the primal-dual recursion that seeks that optimum, the question addressed here. Budget-pacing algorithms for online allocation drive the shadow price of budget by a mirror-descent update fed by observed spend (Balseiro & Gur, 2019; Balseiro et al., 2020), without a primal-side estimate distinct from that measurement, so the mismatch analyzed here does not arise. Baelde (2026) bound the oscillation 3of a Lagrangian reward signal at frozen policy, an O(α/ω ) characterization, and defer the coupled regime this paper closes; dynamic-regret analyses of non-stationary CMDPs (Ding & Lavaei, 2023) bound performance against a drifting comparator with ˆ g = g throughout, whereas the mechanism here opens in a fixed periodic environment read through two different estimates. 

# 3 Setup 

Two objects from the theory of linear systems carry the argument. The Floquet exponent of Section 5 plays for time-periodic coefficients the role the spectral radius plays for constant ones: the per-step logarithmic growth rate of the dominant solution, read off the spectral radius of the monodromy matrix over one period, whose sign decides stability. The top Lyapunov exponent is the same growth rate for stochastic coefficients, the almost sure limit of t−1 log ∥xt∥, and it is the object that survives once the mismatch broadens from a single line into a spectral density.

3.3 Ordering of the timescales as a choice 

Let r := α/η denote the timescale ratio. The two-timescale scheme of Borkar (2008, ch. 6) is neutral about which variable is fast: what the theorem requires is a separation between its two step-size sequences (Appendix A.2). The assignment of the variables is a modeling decision. The CMDP instantiations are not neutral in practice, and they consistently make the multiplier the slowest variable, although Borkar (2005, §5) notes that the latter two timescales could be interchanged. Borkar (2005) builds a constrained actor-critic on three timescales with the critic fastest, the actor intermediate and the dual ascent of the Lagrange multiplier slowest. Bhatnagar & Lakshmanan (2012) keeps that ordering, and Panda & Bhatnagar (2024) carries the same three-timescale construction to a finite-time analysis. The stated reason is exactly the quasi-static argument: a slow multiplier lets the actor-critic pair equilibrate against an essentially frozen price. So the analyzed regime is α ≪ η, that is r ≪ 1. The regime in which the phenomenon of this paper lives is the opposite one, r ≫ 1: a dual step size that exceeds the primal one. Table 1 reads the default step sizes out of three reference Lagrangian codebases; all three put the multiplier 2 orders of magnitude ahead of the policy, r ≈ 10 2, a choice with an operational motive, since a slow multiplier leaves a constraint violated for a long time. As r grows, the primal-dual pair leaves the overdamped side and acquires an oscillatory mode, a prerequisite for a parametric forcing to resonate with. Below a threshold on r the pair has no such mode, and the failure mode we analyze has no mechanism to act through. Sections 4 and 5 make this precise: the damping of the pair, its worst-case amplification under additive forcing, and its tolerance to a sensitivity mismatch are all governed by r, and all move against the practitioner as r grows. The gap we describe is therefore between an asymptotic theory and the practice that cites it. The convergence guarantee that the CMDP literature invokes covers exactly the side on which the resonance cannot exist, so our result closes a coverage gap the convergence guarantee leaves open, without contradicting it. The corresponding prescription is stated in Section 6. Constant step sizes and a fixed r make the sign of an exponent the available question, rather than a conver-gence statement (Appendix A.2).

Section 4 therefore bounds a response, and Section 5 tracks an exponent that can change sign. The parametric channel is also the realistic one in the motivating application: over a day the pacing target moves, the additive channel, but what moves more is the marginal return of a unit of bid, since the same bid buys a different volume of impressions at 3am and at 9pm and converts at a different rate, a modulation of

# 6 Algorithmic consequences 

The primary prescription is to drive the primal and the dual from the same constraint estimate, ˆ g = g. By Proposition 2 this removes the resonant forcing by construction, at every modulation depth and bandwidth, with no new gain to tune and no step size altered. It leaves the unforced spectrum ( σ, ˜ω0) of (5) exactly as it was, unlike every remedy below. What it forbids is the natural pattern of Section 3.4, the critic-versus-counter split that is exactly the mismatch of (11). Coherence means either differentiating through the same estimator that feeds the dual, or feeding the dual with the critic’s own value in place of the raw measurement, which is the lighter change in most implementations. The dual then inherits the critic’s bias, which has a consequence of its own that the analysis of this paper does not see. A dual update driven by ˆ g drives ˆ g to zero. A stationary bias δ in the critic then leaves the true constraint satisfied at the level −δ, an offset of size δ from the nominal 0 level. The offset is measurable against the raw counter and correctable by shifting the target, whereas the resonance is neither. The secondary prescription acts on the step sizes and it is available in two strengths. The quantitative version is to bring α towards η, subject to the stability condition (6): this raises ζ, hence improves Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 simultaneously, the two results coming from different channels that might have pulled in opposite directions. The ordered version is to push r below ( a/c )2, so that 4 ζ exceeds sup |h| = 2: by Corollary 1 it acts on the existence of the failure mode, with no estimate of the modulation depth, and a design margin belongs below the structural bound ( a/c )2/4. A slower dual tracks a moving constraint more slowly, which is why practitioners do the opposite and the tradeoff Stooke et al. (2020) document empirically; the analysis quantifies what is gained on the other side. PID Lagrangian control (Stooke et al., 2020) and the classical augmented Lagrangian (Bertsekas, 1982) are competing remedies, and both cure the resonance the same way, by adding damping. The proportional gain adds kp∇g∇⊤g to the damping matrix of (13), positive semidefinite by construction, raising ζ and with it the threshold (16). The augmented multiplier update is dual ascent with the penalty coefficient ρ in the role of α, whose penalty term replaces the primal curvature a by a + ρc 2, a damping term of the same kind and already the mechanism of Platt & Barr (1988). The two contributions of the penalty cancel in the determinant of (12), so (6) holds for every ρ and the tolerance (16) exceeds that of the plain loop by the factor (1 − ρc 2/a )−1. A modulated sensitivity makes that cancellation inexact and opens a second, weak parametric channel of order αaε θ /4, by the same first-order argument as the curvature-modulation channel quantified in Appendix E. Since ρ is at once the penalty coefficient and the dual step size, the damping it supplies cannot be raised without a faster dual response, unlike a proportional gain. Both raise a threshold and leave the forcing in place, since neither says which constraint signal the primal update consumes. In the agent of Section 7.3 the proportional gain doubles the linear threshold, from 0 .15 to 0 .30 at r = 100, but that threshold lies where the additive forced response already drives the multiplier into its projection; there the PID agent locks in 2 seeds out of 50 at ε = 0 .4 and 4 out of 40 at ε ∈ { 0.3, 0.35 }, against 29 out of 50 for the plain Lagrangian at 0 .4, while keeping the forced response and most of the return loss; the coherent agent halves that loss. Both remedies compose with ˆ g = g, and the prescription is to do both. Three connections to classical control practice follow. 7.3 A deep reinforcement learning agent 

The reduction predicts the failure of an implementation pattern, so we test it on an agent that has the pattern without being built for it. The environment is a one-step constrained bandit with action θ ∈ [0 , 1], reward θ − θ2/2 and cost ctθ − b, ct = 1 + ε sin ωt , b = 0 .3, whose phase the agent does not observe; at the saddle its curvature and sensitivity are those of (11) with a = c = 1; the linear term of the reward places the saddle at ( θ⋆, λ ⋆) = ( b, 1 − b) = (0 .3, 0.7). The learner is Lagrangian SAC with a reward critic Qr and a cost critic Qc fit on a replay buffer of 5 000 transitions, an actor trained by SGD against Qr − λQ c, and the projected dual ascent (3) on the cost averaged over the last W = 10 steps. The actor uses plain SGD so that its gains are constant and can be identified; adaptive optimizers such as Adam rescale the step by running gradient statistics, which this test does not cover. The buffer spans many modulation periods, so the actor’s constraint estimate is unmodulated while the dual’s measurement is modulated: εθ ≈ 0 and ελ = ε, the critic-versus-counter split of Section 3.4. # 8 Conclusion 

Separating the constraint estimate consumed by the primal update from the constraint measurement con-sumed by the dual update opens a second channel of non-stationarity, one that modulates the coefficients of 14 the linearized recursion and whose only driver is the sensitivity mismatch ελ − εθ (Lemma 1). The resonance this opens sits where the practice sits, outside the region the theory was built to secure: at the r ≈ 10 2 of Table 1, the tolerance is an order of magnitude below its value at r = 1, opposite the r ≪ 1 regime of Borkar (2008; 2005); Bhatnagar & Lakshmanan (2012). The prescription needs no new machinery: drive both updates from the same constraint signal (Proposi-tion 2), and where that is unavailable, move α towards η (Theorem 1, Corollary 1), verified numerically on the affine recursion (Section 7). On a Lagrangian soft actor-critic agent the coherent critic removes the lock-in in every cell, the √r law holds at the two ratios compared, and the projection of the multiplier, outside the linear theory, widens the tongue and decides the outcome of a PID variant (Section 7.3). The statements are proved on the tangent linear-quadratic reduction of Section 3.1, whose scope the next paragraph delimits. By Remark 2 the statements extend exactly to K constraints under a common waveform. Two extensions remain open. The first is a multidimensional policy parameter: the scalar pair ( a, c ) is exact only when d = 1 or when the constraint gradient is an eigenvector of the curvature, and otherwise the tongue comes from a coupled system of dimension d + 1. We expect the mechanism to persist on the mode most strongly coupled to the constraint, but reducing a d-dimensional curvature to the single number a carries an uncontrolled error; closing that gap is a spectral question about the pencil, not a new dynamical mechanism. The second is constraints driven by distinct waveforms. Remark 2 settles the common-waveform case exactly under its isotropy condition on the curvature; distinct frequencies instead open several tongues that can cross as the operating point moves, and nothing here says what happens at an intersection. This is left for future work; it is not addressed by Baelde (2026), whose reward-shaping construction removes the dual variable and does not analyze its resonance structure. A separate, unresolved puzzle: Stooke et al. (2020, Figure 4) sweep the integral gain KI , our α, and observe the cost oscillations shrink and vanish as the gain grows, which read naively runs against Proposition 1 and Theorem 1. We have not run their sweep ourselves. A conjectured reconciliation: their perturbation has a spectrum fixed by the rate of policy improvement rather than swept, so as α grows ˜ ω0 ∝ √α leaves that excitation band, predicting an amplitude non-monotone in KI , maximal where ˜ ω0 crosses it. That prediction is testable on the same sweep, which we leave to future work.

A.2 Slow variation and the canonical two-timescale setting 

Assumption 1 is what makes (1) a legitimate description of the environment at time t with ξ = ξt: the occupancy measure has time to equilibrate between two appreciable changes of the exogenous process, so the learner faces a slowly moving constrained optimization problem. It is also the regime of the motivating application, since a daily traffic cycle is orders of magnitude slower than the horizon of an individual bidding episode. The separation required by the two-timescale theorem of Borkar (2008, ch. 6) is written b(n)/a (n) → 0 in Borkar’s own notation, with a(n) the faster schedule and b(n) the slower one. That notation is unrelated to our curvature a and level constant b; a(n) and b(n) correspond here to η and α respectively. Three deviations from the canonical two-timescale setting determine what kind of statement is available. We work at constant step sizes, without η, α → 0, so convergence is weak, to a stationary law of dispersion O(η), and the available question is the sign of an exponent. Our ratio r is held fixed, without asymptotic separation, so a quasi-static approximation stands in for the theorem’s timescale separation, its error measured by ζ

itself. Third, and decisively, non-stationary ξt leaves the fast ODE at frozen slow variable with no fixed equilibrium, so global asymptotic stability must be replaced by stability uniform in t, requiring a uniform Lyapunov function (Khalil, 2002, ch. 4 and 9). For the reduced recursion all arithmetic is double precision and no additive gradient noise is injected, in keeping with the model of Section 3, additive gradient noise being treated separately in Remark 2. Broadband parametric channel. The modulation is synthesized by placing independent complex Gaus-sian coefficients on the lines of the real Fourier grid that fall inside [ ωc ± B/ 2], zeroing every other line, and inverse transforming; each realization is then normalized to unit variance, so ε is a root-mean-square depth. A band carrying fewer than 3 lines is rejected as under-resolved, in place of being silently degenerated. The single-line case, which reproduces the periodic setting with a random phase, is retained as a control. Top Lyapunov exponents are estimated by iterating a renormalized vector, with the reported spread being the standard deviation across realizations; the estimator is controlled at zero modulation, where it must return 

−σ to within 10 −5. Thresholds are obtained by linear interpolation of the first sign change on the mismatch grid. Each realization is a finite Fourier synthesis, a multisinusoid with independent random phases on the retained lines: it is wide-sense stationary, in place of the strictly stationary, ergodic process invoked in the proof of Proposition 3 (Appendix B.5), and the exponent is estimated on that finite-length surrogate; the estimator control at zero modulation and the agreement with the Floquet threshold at B = 0 show no dependence on this distinction at the horizons used here. 

Full recursion. Table 12 simulates the unlinearized recursion of Section 3.2, affine term included, at the reference operating point for 200 forcing periods, that is 62 800 steps. The initial condition is the unmodulated saddle ( θ0, λ 0) = ( −b/c, ab/c 2), which is ( −1, 1) under the parameterization used here, so the unmodulated row reports max t |λt| = 1 and the departure from that value measures the excursion. The final peak-to-peak is measured on the last period alone, and the clipped fraction is the proportion of steps at which the unprojected update would have sent λ negative, which is counted in both runs and acted upon only in the projected one. A divergence guard freezes a run at |λt| > 10 13 ; the largest excursion reported is 6 orders of magnitude below it, so the guard never fires in that table.

Deep RL agent. The agent of Section 7.3 is outside the protocol above. The actor is a tanh-squashed Gaussian and each critic a regression of the immediate reward or cost, all with two hidden layers of 64 ReLU units; the episodes have one step, so the critics need no target network. The critics are trained jointly by Adam at step size 10 −3, the actor by SGD, one gradient step each per environment step on minibatches of 256 from the replay buffer, after 1 000 uniformly random warm-up actions, with entropy temperature fixed at 0 .01 and λ started at 0 .7.
