> University of Gothenburg and Chalmers University of Technology

We present asymptotically optimal translations from linear temporal logic with past initially to limit-deterministic B¨ uchi automata and then onwards to deterministic parity automata. In contrast to previously known translations involving past, the ones presented here are incremental: states and transitions can be constructed as needed on-the-fly, and they do not involve backtracking in order to correct for temporal inconsistencies. The past is naturally encoded in the formula by alternating between weak and strong versions of past operators. To handle the difficulties that arise from the al-ternation of least and greatest fix-point operators (such as between the “until” operator and its weak counterpart), we rewrite the given formula based on the infinitary behavior of a given word with respect to subformulae rooted with such operators. 

# 1 Introduction 

Linear temporal logic ( LTL ), introduced in the 1970s by Pnueli [15], is a logic of particular interest to the computer science community. This is both because it is suitable for expressing how the state of a system evolves over time and because of the perceived balance between its expressivity and complexity. Translations from LTL to ω-automata have been extensively studied, not only because they provide a deeper understanding of the semantics of LTL , but also for practical reasons. Common use cases of ω-automata include model checking and reactive synthesis, with the desired type of automaton depending on the situation. In the context of model checking, non-deterministic ω-automata combined with program models in the form of transition systems allow for decidable and efficient algorithms for verifying properties with respect to the model. For models of systems that exhibit probabilistic behavior, more determinism is required from the automaton, where limit-deterministic automata become useful. The latter make only finitely many non-deterministic choices and along every accepting run become deterministic. In both cases an “on-the-fly” construction is desirable, where states of the automaton are incrementally built as needed for verification, potentially eliminating the need to construct the full state space. One of the most successful approaches for both the non-deterministic and deterministic construction mentioned above has been semantic [4, 14]. In this approach, each state of the respective automaton is a formula giving semantic information about the requirements for the future in a given state. Recently, a semi-symbolic construction of automata for LTLf [5] used this approach to win the LTLf track in the last synthesis competition. It is also used in the synthesis from LTL to combine machine learning into the strategy search [10], which won the LTL track of the same competition. Most existing translations are only capable of handling future temporal operators. This is perhaps based on the fact that linear temporal logic with past operators ( pLTL ) is no more expressive than LTL ,and that Gabbay [8] provided an algorithm for translating a given pLTL formula into an initially equiv-alent formula without past operators. Moreover, many properties are more naturally and elegantly ex-pressed using past operators, and Markey [13] showed that pLTL is exponentially more succinct than

The translation is an extension of a similar result for LTL by Esparza et al. [7], who also present a variation thereof for the construction of limit-deterministic B¨ uchi automata. In this paper we show how to adapt this variation to handle past operators, combining the frameworks developed in both papers. As with its LTL counterpart, the translation is incremental in the sense that states can be created as needed, and its number of states is asymptotically optimal relative to the size of the input formula. The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces the basic concepts behind ω-automata and the relevant types of automata, as well as the syntax and semantics of pLTL . Section 3 includes definitions and properties from previous work that are required to understand the translations. We omit repeating proofs and refer to the original papers. The translation is based on the after-function, which reads an input symbol and rewrites the input formula based on the information contained therein; the result is a formula to be verified by further input. As we allow for the arbitrary alternation of past and future operators, the current input may con-tain information relevant for the proper evaluation of past subformulae in future steps. In Section 3.1 we describe how to encode this information in the formula, while Section 3.2 presents a simplified ver-sion of the after-function for pLTL . We present the translation from pLTL to limit-deterministic B¨ uchi automata in Section 4, and show that the resulting automata are amenable to further asymptotically op-timal translations to deterministic parity automata in Section 5. We finish with concluding remarks in Section 6.

2.3 Propositional Equivalence of Formulae 

To obtain reasonable bounds on our automata we need to define an equivalence relation on pLTL formu-lae. Because semantic equivalence is difficult to determine, we use the stronger notion of propositional equivalence . This is equivalent to viewing a formula as a propositional logic formula where maximal temporal subformulae are taken as propositional variables. 

Definition 1 (Propositional Semantics of pLTL [7]) . Let I be a set of formulae and ϕ a formula. The propositional satisfaction relation I |=p ϕ is inductively defined as

I̸ |=p ⊥ I |=p ψ ∨ ξ iff I |=p ψ or I |=p ξ ,

with I |=p ϕ iff ϕ ∈ I for all other cases. Two formulae ϕ and ψ are propositionally equivalent, denoted ϕ ∼ ψ, if I |=p ϕ ⇔ I |=p ψ for all sets of formulae I . The (propositional) equivalence class of a formula ϕ is denoted [ϕ]∼. The (propositional) quotient set of a set of formulae Ψ is denoted Ψ/∼.

We state formally that propositional equivalence implies equivalence. 

Lemma 1 ([7]) . Let ϕ, ψ be formulae such that ϕ ∼ ψ , w a word, t ∈ N. Then, (w,t) |= ϕ iff (w,t) |= ψ.

For example, (a U b ∧ G a) ∨ a U b is propositionally equivalent to a U b. On the other hand, the for-mulas a U b ∨ (a W b ∧ F b) is equivalent to a U b but not propositionally equivalent to it. The following lemma allows us to lift certain functions from formulae to equivalence classes of formulae. 

Lemma 2 ([7]) . Let f be a function on formulae such that f (⊤) = ⊤, f (⊥) = ⊥, and for all formulae 

ϕ and ψ, f (ϕ ∧ ψ) = f (ϕ) ∧ f (ψ) and f (ϕ ∨ ψ) = f (ϕ) ∨ f (ψ). Then, for all pairs of formulae ϕ and 

ψ, if ϕ ∼ ψ then f (ϕ) ∼ f (ψ).

# 3 Encoding the Past and the After-Function

3.1 Encoding the Past 

Our translations involve taking a formula ϕ – essentially representing a state in the automaton – and a letter σ , and from these construct a successor formula by rewriting the formula using the local semantics of pLTL . Esparza et al. [6] present a translation from linear temporal logic (without past) to deterministic parity automata, via limit-deterministic B¨ uchi automata. # 6 Conclusion 

We have presented an asymptotically optimal translation from linear temporal logic with past to limit-deterministic B¨ uchi automata, extending previous work by Esparza et al. [7]. The translation makes use of a framework for handling past operators by rewrites developed by Azzopardi et al. [2]. In addition, we have shown that the resulting automata are amenable to a further asymptotically optimal translation to deterministic parity automata, due to Esparza et al. [6]. The automata can be built incrementally on a by-need basis and do not require altering the states and transitions already created. As future work, we would like to implement the above translations and explore whether the complex-ity involved in handling past operators is balanced by the added succinctness in practice. The translations by Esparza et al. [7] have been implemented in the tool Owl [11]. It is of interest to explore how amenable those implementations are to our extensions. Owl is used by, among other things, the reactive synthesis tool Strix [14] and the probabilistic model checker MoChiBA [16]. We would like to also study the usage of the natural encoding of the past in the conversion of pLTL to non-deterministic B¨ uchi automata with transition-based acceptance, as used in state-of-the-art model-checking tools and in the extension of pLTL to include the ‘now’ operator [12]. 146 New Translations From LTL with Past to LDBA and DPA
