# Deterministic Bounds on Accuracy and Stability of Metric-Space Regression and Classification 

## Nina M. Gottschling ∗ Jakob Gawlikowski † Michele Caprio ‡

## October 19, 2026 

Abstract 

Data-driven approaches often face ambiguity in datasets due to sensor or target noise, in-sufficient information, and noise in the input data. While best-fit machine learning methods can accommodate such noise through uncertainty estimates, they are prone to overfitting and it is challenging to quantify ambiguity of the relation between inputs and targets. We introduce a geometric framework that quantifies this ambiguity through the average kernel size, computed with data-dependent, i.e. model-independent, feasible sets derived from worst-case input and target noise. The framework provides computable, determinis-tic, non-asymptotic bounds on the best achievable empirical error and accuracy for a finite, constructed dataset, independent of the predictive model class, and applies to regression and classification with pseudo-metric input spaces and metric target spaces. The ambiguity of the input-target relation implies three consequences: an empirical error floor, an accu-racy–stability trade-off, and, under standard probabilistic assumptions, a strictly positive distribution–training error gap when training below the accuracy bounds. Under standard Borel assumptions for Euclidean norms, the distributional analogue of the average kernel size is related to Bayes risk as it reduces to the optimal distribution root-mean-squared error times √2. Experiments spanning tabular regression, image regression and classifica-tion datasets yield nontrivial bounds on the best achievable empirical error and accuracy on a constructed dataset, alongside descriptive comparisons with model performance on the original datasets. The bounds provide a computable, model-independent reference scale on the constructed dataset and comparisons with model performance on the original dataset are descriptive. 

Keywords accuracy bounds, data centric, aleatoric uncertainty, regression, classification, deep learning

(i) Non-uniqueness of the target given the observed input. In many regression and classification problems, the available input does not determine a unique target, satisfying As-sumption 1.2. This may occur because of measurement noise, omitted variables, or annotation uncertainty and is commonly referred to as aleatoric uncertainty in the field of machine learn-ing, see Murphy [2022], Der Kiureghian and Ditlevsen [2009], Gawlikowski et al. [2023]. In such settings, even an ideal regressor or classifier cannot recover a single deterministic response from the observed input alone. Consequently, part of the prediction error is intrinsic to the data-generating relation and cannot be removed by choosing a more expressive model class or a better optimization method. 

(ii) The need for computable, model-independent bounds on finite datasets. In com-putationally demanding settings, it is useful to estimate achievable empirical error and accuracy before extensive model selection and training. This motivates finite-sample bounds that are computable from the dataset itself and independent of a specific model class. To the best of our knowledge, related work does not provide such bounds in this form; see Table 1 and Section 1.3. 

(iii) Expressive regressors and classifiers do not evade intrinsic accuracy limits. Athird challenge is that expressive regressors and classifiers can fit the training dataset without resolving ambiguity inherent in the input-target relation. For example, for a finite dataset DM

with no ambiguous targets that have the same input, interpolation by a large enough model is possible, but may come at the cost of poor generalization or overfitting, [Murphy, 2022, Chapter 1] and Zhang et al. [2016, 2021], and potentially a large Lipschitz constant. Hence, approximation power alone does not determine the best achievable test error and accuracy. 

## 1.2 Contributions 

We develop a framework for computing deterministic bounds on achievable regression and classi-fication accuracy from finite datasets. Here, we introduce a metric-space framework that encodes input-target ambiguity through data-dependent feasible sets, with pseudo-metrics on inputs and metrics on targets. The main contributions are as follows. We establish an accuracy-stability trade-off , Proposition 2.5. We show that a too small point-wise error on separated, ambiguous targets at nearby, but distinct, inputs forces a large (local) Lipschitz constant and that ambiguous targets produce a pointwise error floor. We derive computable, deterministic, non-asymptotic lower and upper bounds on the best achiev-able empirical error and accuracy on datasets defined in Assumption 1.2. The lower bounds in Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11 hold for all regressors and classifiers, while for distinct inputs the upper bound holds and the optimum is attained by an upper hemicontinuous compact-valued regressor or classifier. 4Using a graph-based argument, we show that for Lipschitz continuous regressors and classifiers, the difference between empirical error, respectively accuracy, on the constructed and original datasets is bounded in terms of the Lipschitz constant and input-target ambiguity for finite feasible sets of equal cardinality (Proposition 2.13). Under standard Borel assumptions, we show that regressors and classifiers achieving errors below the conditional ambiguity scale, necessarily incur a strictly positive generalization gap 

(Proposition 2.18). Under Euclidean squared loss, the Bayes squared risk equals one half the squared distributional analogue average kernel size. Under an ideal two-stage design with inputs sampled from the input law and conditionally inde-pendent repeated targets, we prove a finite-sample concentration bound for the empirical average kernel size estimator (Proposition 2.19). This differs from the average kernel size computed by Algorithm 1, which is a data-dependent geometric quantity unless additional sampling assump-tions are imposed. We provide open-source code for reproducibility and experiments illustrating the computation of the deterministic bounds across regression and classification tasks , their dependence on the chosen pseudo-metric, thresholds, and feasible set sizes, and their comparison with benchmark models and locally trained models. The experiments examine whether the bounds are nontrivial, how selected model performances compare numerically with the resulting intervals, and how sensitive the intervals are to the finite feasible sets (Section 3). ## 1.3 Relation to previous work 

The proposed framework to tackle the three challenges in Section 1.1 is related to several existing lines of work, including optimal recovery, approximation theory, statistical learning theory, and modern deep learning. 

Optimal recovery and worst-case approximation. Classical approximation-theoretic frame-works quantify best achievable worst-case error in settings related to (1.1). For noiseless linear problems, examples include Gelfand widths Pinkus [2012] and best k-term approximation Cohen et al. [2009]. Worst-case bounds for set-valued recovery maps were established on Banach spaces in Arestov [1986] and extended to metric spaces in Magaril-Il’yaev and Osipenko [1991]. For noisy problems, Binev et al. [2024] introduced optimal learning and upper bounds based on the Chebyshev radius, while Cohen et al. [2022] studied the optimal accuracy of nonlinear approxi-mation methods; see also the radius of information in Traub et al. [1983] and the survey Micchelli and Rivlin [1977]. These works show that non-uniqueness can impose unavoidable error. Our approach differs in using metric and Hausdorff distances on feasible sets and optimal (set-valued) regressors and classifiers, without requiring a nested family of approximate prediction sets, and in providing a computable finite-dataset perspective relevant to modern machine learning. 

Statistical learning theory and model-dependent bounds. Another line of work stud-ies regressor and classifier error through sampling assumptions, hypothesis-class complexity, or specific model classes, including neural-network architectural restrictions. For example, Zhang [2009] derives sharp bounds for parameter estimation in linear regression under specific assump-tions, and Qi et al. [2020] gives upper bounds for the mean absolute error of neural-network re-gressors under architectural assumptions. Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) theory, see Vapnik [2006, 1999], and related measures such as Rademacher complexity, see Bartlett and Mendelson [2002], provide generalization bounds for restricted model classes, while Cherkassky et al. [1999] show the usefulness of VC-type bounds for model selection in small i.i.d. datasets and linear regres-sion. However, such bounds are typically not directly computable for arbitrary finite datasets and broad model classes, and they often rely on assumptions, such as i.i.d. sampling, that may fail in practice or be difficult to verify Arnold [1979]. 

Modern deep learning and the limits of classical explanations. Neural networks are universal approximators, see Hornik et al. [1989], Maiorov and Pinkus [1999], Bach [2017], Beck et al. [2022], Petersen and Voigtlaender [2018], Yarotsky [2017], but, as emphasized in Adcock and Dexter [2021], approximation-theoretic expressivity does not by itself provide guarantees on trainability or practical error and accuracy. Zhang et al. [2016, 2021] show that deep networks can fit datasets with random labels, and argue that classical notions such as VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, and uniform stability do not explain the generalization behavior of state-of-the-art neural networks. Separately, Northcutt et al. [2021] identified substantial label errors in widely used datasets, such as CIFAR-10, highlighting that both label uncertainty and annotation errors can limit measurable test accuracy. In some applications, deep learning is not even the best empirical choice: for instance, tree-based methods can outperform deep neu-ral networks on tabular data Grinsztajn et al. [2022]. These observations motivate the search for accuracy limits that depend on the task and dataset rather than on the expressive power of a specific model family. The perspective of this paper is closest to geometric and recovery-based approaches that quantify ambiguity in the input-target relation. It is complementary to information-theoretic minimax lower bounds, such as Fano and Le Cam methods Tsybakov [2003], Le Cam [2012], Fano and Hawkins [1961], and to bias-variance decompositions of predic-6tion risk Geman et al. [1992]. 

Bayes risk, irreducible error, and Bayes-error estimation. In statistical decision the-ory, the model-independent benchmark under distribution μ for a loss ℓ is the Bayes risk inf ϕ Eμ[ℓ(Y, ϕ (X))], where the infimum is over measurable decision rules ϕ, see Berger [1985] and Murphy [2022], which could be either regressors or classifiers. Under Euclidean squared loss, the Bayes rule is the conditional mean and the irreducible term is the expected conditional variance. Cover and Hart [1967] showed that the asymptotic error of a nearest-neighbor classi-fier is bounded above by twice the Bayes error; in their conditional sampling formulation, this error is characterized by pairwise disagreement between conditionally independent labels, which is the distributional analogue average kernel size in our settin. The problem of estimating this irreducible squared-loss term, often called the residual variance, has also been studied in non-parametric regression under i.i.d. sampling assumptions; see, for example, Devroye et al. [2018], who analyze a nearest-neighbor estimator together with asymptotic normality and concentration results. Our distributional analogue average kernel size gives an equivalent pairwise represen-tation, as its square is exactly twice the Bayes squared risk. Recent work develops statistical estimators of Bayes error and optimal classification error and accuracy, including multiclass and computationally efficient approaches, see Chen et al. [2023], Jeong et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2025], Ushio et al. [2026]. These methods estimate distribution-level limits under probabilistic sampling assumptions, such as i.i.d. sampling. Related empirical studies refine the classical bias-variance picture in overparameterized regimes; see, for example, Neal et al. [2018], Yang et al. [2020]. In contrast, our bounds are deterministic and finite-sample, apply to the realized empirical loss on the constructed dataset from Assumption 1.2, extend to metric or pseudo-metric target spaces, and do not require i.i.d. sampling. 

Nonparametric estimation of conditional structure. Estimating conditional means, condi-tional variances, and Bayes risk from finite data is a central problem in nonparametric statistics, see Stone [1977], Gy¨ orfi et al. [2002]. Such estimators typically rely on i.i.d. sampling together with smoothness assumptions. Here the bounds are computed from deterministic and geometric finite feasible sets with a chosen pseudo-metric and threshold rule. It should be distinguished from an estimator based on genuine conditionally i.i.d. replicates. The convergence theorem connects the deterministic bounds to such an ideal sampling design. 

Stability and generalization. Classical algorithmic-stability results control expected general-ization error by studying how a learning rule changes when observations in the training sample are replaced Bousquet and Elisseeff [2002]. The accuracy-stability result concerns a different notion: the metric regularity of the fitted regressors and classifiers as a function of their input, which is a deterministic geometric obstruction. If nearby inputs are paired with well-separated targets, a regressor or classifier that fits both targets accurately must have a large local Lipschitz constant, independently of how the regressor or classifier was trained. 

Dimensionality reduction and geometric preservation. Results such as the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma Johnson and Lindenstrauss [1984] show that low-dimensional embeddings can approximately preserve pairwise geometry, but this does not by itself quantify achievable prediction accuracy or ambiguity in the input-target relation. 

Positioning of the present work. We use a feasible-set geometry to derive computable, deterministic, non-asymptotic lower and upper bounds on the best achievable empirical error on a realized, finite, constructed dataset. The bounds are model-class independent and apply to upper hemicontinuous compact-valued regressors and classifiers, a class containing single-valued, continuous regressors and classifiers. The input space has a pseudo-metric and the target space a metric, so the construction is not restricted to Euclidean regression. The provided distribution dependent results show that, they relate conditional target ambiguity to Bayes risk and to generalization gaps under probabilistic assumptions. 

## 1.4 Outlook and impact 

An important practical feature of the proposed framework is that the bounds on empirical error on the constructed dataset DM ′ from Assumption 1.2 are computable before model optimization and training. As the bounds depend on the dataset, the chosen metric structure, and the finite 7samples used in the analysis, they provide task-dependent information prior to model selection and optimization. The framework can therefore help answer questions such as: Is the available data sufficiently informative for the task under the chosen evaluation metric? and How much improvement remains possible before the observed empirical error reaches the model-independent floor induced by the chosen input space pseudo-metric and finite feasible sets? The bounds quantify this remaining room for improvement in empirical error, but they do not imply that additional tuning, a different architecture, or a particular optimization method will attain it. Although the proof of Theorem 2.9 is constructive and provides an explicit variational formula for the regressor obtaining the best achievable empirical error, this regressor is computationally expensive as it requires solving an optimization problem for each input. The analogous formula and conclusion apply to classification. This makes the bounds potentially useful for dataset assessment, model selection, and training diagnostics. More broadly, they provide a principled way to distinguish limitations imposed by the data from limitations imposed by a specific learning method. 

## 1.5 Outline 

The remainder of the paper is organized into three logically distinct parts. First, we develop deterministic finite-sample bounds on empirical loss and accuracy for a constructed dataset derived from feasible-set geometry. Second, under additional probabilistic assumptions, we relate the distributional analogue of the average kernel size to Bayes risk and generalization gaps. Here Section 2 develops the framework and assumptions and presents the main theoretical results, including the algorithm used to compute the accuracy bounds. Third, we provide experiments illustrating the behavior of the deterministic construction across several datasets and choices of evaluation metric. Here Section 3 presents the experimental results. Proofs are deferred to the end of the manuscript, while additional material for reproducibility, including implementation details and descriptions of the datasets considered, is provided in the Appendix. Our code is publicly available at this GitHub repository.

Remark 2.3 (Loss convention for set-valued predictors) . Our loss uses the Hausdorff distance defined with the supremum over the prediction set and therefore measures average prediction error over pointwise worst-case distances. Remark 2.4 (Level of generality) . We deliberately adopt this level generality to accommodate practical data representations. The distance dX may be a pseudo-metric because distinct inputs can become indistinguishable after preprocessing or finite-precision representations. Multisets are used because multiplicities of admissible targets affect the feasible-set construction and em-pirical averages. The Heine-Borel assumption on ( Y, d Y ) is the compactness condition needed for existence of minimizers; here finite-dimensional normed spaces are a standard special case.

In the following we provide an outline of experiments and implementation, however, compre-hensive details on the implementation and considered datasets are in the Appendix in Section 9.1. We applied the framework to a range of tabular and image-regression tasks and image-classification tasks. For each experiment, we sampled finite feasible sets, constructed the dataset 

DM ′ , computed the average kernel size, and used Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11 to obtain model-independent bounds on the optimal empirical regression loss and classification error, re-spectively. The experiments are intended as illustrations of the deterministic construction. They address three empirical questions: i) whether the lower bound is nontrivial across domains, ii )how reported or measured model performance compares numerically with the resulting inter-val, and iii ) how sensitive the bound is to the threshold and parameters used for the feasible set construction. We compared the theoretical intervals with the error and accuracy of several regressors and classifiers evaluated on both the original datasets and, where the models are available, on constructed datasets. We used a synthetic regression example to study how the accuracy bounds interact with the accuracy-stability trade-off from Proposition 2.5. Across all experiments, the threshold-selection rule was as follows: when an input-noise model or target/label measurement-noise model was available, we set τX and τY equal to conservative worst-case noise magnitudes implied by that model. If such knowledge was not available, for the input threshold τX we followed a practical empirical heuristic based on quantiles detailed in the Appendix in Section 9.1.2 and assumed ideal, noiseless targets with τY = 0. For τY = 0 the target criterion in the ”or”-condition Algorithm 1 was not included. We used a wavelet-based metric with a conversion to float32 for all image inputs described in the Appendix in Section 9.1.1; for images represented in float64, this induces a pseudo-metric. All details on the datasets, wavelet-based metric and feasible set sampling are in the Appendix in Section 9.1. 

## 3.1 Theoretical assumptions and guarantees versus experimental pro-cedure

For smaller datasets, ( M ≈ 1k), we computed the bounds on the full dataset and did not compute these separately on the train and test splits. For large and high-dimensional datasets, such as Tropical Cyclone and RESISC45, with a full dataset size in the range M ≈ 100 k and/or inputs with higher dimension, we computed the bounds on random subsets of anchor samples with size K = 1 k or K = 3 k from the train or test splits over 20 different random seeds. For even larger datasets M ≫ 100 k where a sample number K ≈ 3k is not representative of the dataset, the implementation of Algorithm 1 using nearest neighbors may not be feasible. Extending Algorithm 1 to be applicable to this scale is beyond the scope of this work. 

Comparison to theoretical assumptions and guarantees: Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11 only provide bounds on the error, respectively accuracy, on the constructed dataset DM ′ from Algorithm 1. We explicitly computed the accuracy on the original and constructed dataset to validate the bounds for the synthetic regression example, MNIST, CIFAR-10 and EUROSAT. For the remaining datasets we did not compute the regressor errors or classifier accuracies on the constructed dataset DM ′ from Algorithm 1 and instead report errors and accuracies from benchmark dataset challenges and published state-of-the-art models on the same datasets. In these cases, for CIFAR-10 and RESISC45, differing evaluation protocols make published results not directly comparable to the DM ′ -based bounds and thus not violations of them. We did not compare the average kernel size against empirical conditional variance as a general baseline, as it is only directly estimable with additional assumptions, such as i.i.d. samples, in Euclidean regression settings with repeated or conditionally repeated observations. For the same reason, 17 we did not compute the conditional mean, which is optimal according to Proposition 2.17.

# 4 Discussion 

The deterministic bounds from Theorem 2.9 and Corollary 2.11 hold for the dataset constructed in Algorithm 1. Where a priori knowledge of the input and target noise is unavailable, we selected input thresholds so the average kernel size remained stable with changes in feasible set size. In these cases, for both regression and classification datasets, our stability analysis across quantiles for choosing input threshold τX based on a practical empirical heuristic and feasible set size demonstrated that the bounds can be chosen to be relatively stable. We emphasize that the resulting kernel-size accuracy intervals are not an intrinsic property of the dataset alone, but rather, a property of the triple ( DM , d X , d Y ), and its interpretability depends on how well 

dX captures task-relevant indistinguishability and which evaluation metric dY is used. After all, the evaluation metric can be optimized to obtain arbitrarily good accuracy Redmon and Farhadi [2018]. We chose a wavelet-based pseudo-metric for all image-based tasks to ensure comparability across tasks; however, other metrics may be more relevant to object classification tasks, such as MNIST or CIFAR-10. 

Regression accuracy bounds: Table 2 shows that the framework is applicable to a range of datasets of different sizes and ranging from image to tabular regression tasks. The bounds in Theorem 2.9 and the regressor errors in Table 3 show that the proposed intervals can be nontrivial and, in several cases, are numerically comparable to the performance reported for strong benchmark and published models. Figure 4 illustrates the accuracy-stability trade-off predicted by Proposition 2.5. The high-accuracy models have training errors smaller than half of the average kernel size, but this comes at the cost of significantly larger test errors and higher empirical Lipschitz constant estimates, bL, substantially exceeding the Lipschitz constant of the noiseless function generating the dataset. The models that satisfy the prescribed accuracy interval on the training set from Theorem 2.9, achieve lower test errors while maintaining bL close to the true regularity of the underlying data-generating function, see Table 6 in the Appendix. 

Classification accuracy bounds: Table 4 shows that the framework is applicable to a range of datasets of different sizes and image classification tasks. The accuracy bounds in Corollary 2.11 and the classifier accuracies in Table 5 show that the proposed intervals can be nontrivial and, in several cases, provide a descriptive reference scale, but benchmark accuracies on DM , especially under different splits or protocols, are not directly comparable to the bounds on DM ′ . The train and test accuracies of the best model for the task of mapping images (I) to random classes (RC), reported in [Zhang et al., 2016, Table 1], illustrate the lower bound on the generalization gap for classification in Proposition 2.18. 21 Average kernel sizes and aleatoric uncertainty: For the synthetic regression example, Figures 5 and 6 show that the average kernel sizes increase with increasing input and target noise. Combined with the model-independent bounds on empirical error in Theorem 2.9, this indicates that the average kernel sizes capture increasing aleatoric uncertainty in the regression task. For MNIST and EUROSAT, Figure 9 shows that the average kernel sizes increase with increasing label noise. Together with Corollary 2.11 and Figure 7, this suggests that, under the chosen pseudo-metric and feasible-set construction, the resulting accuracy bounds capture a data-dependent component of aleatoric uncertainty. Here we note that, in the examples provided whenever we assumed ideal, noiseless targets with τY = 0, our results may underestimate the aleatoric uncertainty of the corresponding tasks. 

# 5 Limitations & Future Work 

As shown above, the proposed framework can provide realistic accuracy bounds for a wide range of applications and tasks. Nevertheless, several open points limit its functionality in certain aspects and could be investigated further in future work. First, the resulting bounds depend on the pair ( DM , d X ) and the chosen evaluation metric 

dY . The interpretability of the feasible sets and the resulting average kernel size depend on whether the chosen pseudo-metric dX captures task-relevant indistinguishability. A poor metric may either merge unrelated inputs or fail to detect genuine ambiguity. In this work, we found that relatively simple metrics can already yield nontrivial bounds. For image data, metrics induced by transforms need not align with semantic similarity. Investigating different kinds of metrics, universal feature extractors (e.g., pretrained foundation models), or expert knowledge-based feature extractors could enable targeting the task-relevant features for the bounds more explicitly. Second, the feasible set construction depends on the threshold parameters τX and τY . Ideally, these can be selected from prior knowledge of input and target noise and otherwise, alternatives are selecting the input threshold τX with a practical empirical heuristic based on quantiles or selecting the thresholds in order to guarantee a sufficiently small lower bound to the Lipschitz constant in the accuracy-stability trade-off from Proposition 2.5; however, Assumption 2.6 may be difficult to verify in practice and further properties or rules for selecting the thresholds in the absence of prior knowledge on input and target noise should be evaluated at a larger scale. Third, Algorithm 1 provides finite-sample approximations of the feasible sets. If the dataset under-samples rare but valid targets for a given input, then the empirical average kernel size may underestimate the true spread. Hence, the lower bound is data-dependent and may be conservative when the dataset significantly under-samples rare but valid targets. An impor-tant next step is to provide construction diagnostics with guarantees, including feasible-set size distributions, singleton fractions, in order to distinguish ambiguity arising from local input neigh-borhoods from ambiguity induced primarily by the target tolerance. Here a theoretical direction for future work is to bridge the gap between the ideal distributional analogue quantity and the finite-sample average kernel size. This includes consistency of Algorithm 1 as τX → 0 with grow-ing local sample counts, and an accuracy-stability frontier obtained by minimizing empirical loss over predictors with prescribed Lipschitz constant. Fourth, the optimal accuracy intervals, as in Theorem 2.9,  12 Kersize( DM ′ , p )K , Kersize( DM ′ , p )K

,can be non-sharp in some applications where additional assumptions that yield sharper bounds are satisfied. While these provide a nontrivial model-independent accuracy range, they may not always identify the exact optimal empirical error due to the reasons outlined above. The proposed bounds quantify only one component of prediction difficulty, namely the ambiguity of the input-target multiset in the chosen (pseudo-)metric spaces. They do not account directly for optimization difficulty or finite model capacity. The optimum regressor or classifier is explicit in some special cases, in which we provide the corresponding error and accuracy in the Appendix, yet the bounds remain applicable in general metric settings where the anchor-wise minimization need not admit a closed form and may require solving a separate optimization problem for each anchor. 22 6 Conclusion 

We introduced a geometric framework for deriving deterministic, non-asymptotic bounds on achievable empirical error and accuracy in regression and classification on general metric and pseudo-metric spaces. The central quantity, the average kernel size, measures target dispersion within finite feasible sets and yields a model-independent reference scale for empirical prediction difficulty on the constructed dataset. A complementary deterministic comparison bound relates empirical loss on the constructed dataset to empirical loss on the original dataset through a graph-based reassignment error, showing how the discrepancy is controlled by the thresholds, the predictor regularity, and the induced reference weights. Together with the stability result, the framework shows that fitting ambiguous targets below this scale entails increased local in-stability. Under additional probabilistic assumptions, the distributional analogue average kernel size is related to Bayes risk, implies positive generalization gaps below the ambiguity-compatible level, and admits a finite-sample concentration guarantee under an ideal conditional-sampling design. The average kernel size computed by Algorithm 1 should therefore be interpreted as a computable geometric surrogate for this ideal quantity unless further assumptions justify a sta-tistical consistency claim. Important directions for future work include task-dependent pseudo-metric design and bridging the gap between the ideal sampling theory and the finite-sample construction used in practice.

## 9.1 Experiment details 

9.1.1 Pseudo-metric on image data 

Across all image-based tasks we used the same metric to ensure comparability of image-based features. The metric on images was the composition of the ℓ2 norm with discrete wavelet features 

g. The image feature was obtained by the concatenation of the four subbands obtained from a single-level two-dimensional discrete wavelet transform (DWT) applied independently to each channel Daubechies [1992]. Given an image x ∈ RC×H×W , the resulting feature vector is denoted by gwav (x) ∈ RD . The DWT was computed using a chosen wavelet basis; in all experiments, we used the Haar wavelet basis. Specifically, for each channel, the transform decomposes the image into an approximation subband, denoted by LL (low-low), and three detail subbands: LH (low-high), HL (high-low), and HH (high-high). These subbands correspond to the responses of low- and high-frequency filters applied along the two spatial dimensions. The four resulting subbands were flattened and concatenated to form the final feature, gwav (x). For wavelets with standard dyadic downsampling and even spatial dimensions, the resulting feature dimension is 

D = CHW . The image similarity was measured by the metric 

dX (x, x ′) = ∥g(x) − g(x′)∥2 . (9.1) More generally, this construction can be viewed as a feature-induced pseudo-metric on the origi-nal image space: if the feature map g is not injective, distinct images may have identical feature representations and therefore zero distance. For the specific implementation considered here, all four subbands of the Haar DWT are retained, so the transform is invertible under the cor-responding DWT conventions. The pseudo-metric terminology is therefore primarily relevant to the general feature-map formulation, particularly when g is replaced by a potentially non-injective feature encoder.

The quantile parameter q for choosing the threshold τX (q) in (9.2) determines the locality scale at which samples are treated as indistinguishable with respect to the pseudo-metric dX : smaller values of q enforce more local feasible sets, whereas larger values increase sample support but may merge points that are no longer similar. To focus on local neighborhoods we restricted the quantile to q ∈ [0 .01 , 0.1], that is, to the lower tail of the nearest-neighbor distance distribution. Within this range, q was chosen by a bandwidth stability heuristic rather than by an optimality criterion: we selected the smallest q lying in a region where the estimated average kernel size changed only mildly across neighboring values of q. We identified such a region by requiring both small relative changes in the average kernel size and approximately small local curvature, and then choose the smallest q in that plateau. This favors the most local scale at which the estimated ambiguity is not unduly sensitive to the threshold choice. We emphasize that this is a practical empirical heuristic for selecting a stable locality scale, not a claim of statistical optimality. To make the dependence on q transparent, we reported full sensitivity curves in Section 9.2 below, which contains additional ablation studies, and verified that the average kernel size remained qualitatively stable throughout the selected range.
