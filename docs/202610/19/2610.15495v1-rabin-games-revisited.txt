Rabin games have their roots in Michael Rabin’s seminal work on automata over infinite trees and form a classical model for infinite-round games with ω-regular winning conditions. They play a central role in automata theory and temporal synthesis: translating an LTL specification into an equivalent deterministic Rabin automaton reduces synthesis to solving a Rabin game. In practice, however, Rabin games are typically solved indirectly, via parity games. One possible reason is that many direct algorithms have a factorial dependence on the Rabin index k, the number of Rabin pairs. In this work, we revisit Rabin’s 1972 recursive algorithm. The algorithm is implicit in Rabin’s proof and intertwined with finite-state determinacy. First, we “excavate” it as an explicit arena-based solver using standard reachability- and Büchi-game solving as basic building blocks, thus avoiding the exponential dependence on the alphabet in Rabin’s original presentation. Second, we show that memoization removes the factorial term from the recursion-tree analysis. Finally, we introduce a variation that recurses on the Rabin index. These results provide a modern analysis of Rabin’s recursive method and a basis for studying direct Rabin-game solvers for temporal synthesis. 

# 1 Introduction 

Rabin games originate in a central early development of automata theory. In his proof of the decidability of the monadic second-order theory of infinite trees, Rabin introduced acceptance conditions that com-bine recurrence and eventual avoidance requirements [18]. This decidability result, known as Rabin’s Tree Theorem , had a major impact on logic, automata theory, and infinite-duration games. Rabin’s origi-nal decision procedure was nonelementary, and much subsequent work was devoted to obtaining sharper algorithmic bounds. In particular, Hossley and Rackoff later gave an alternative elementary decidability proof for the emptiness problem of Rabin automata on infinite trees [8]. Rabin gave a more direct treatment of Rabin automata on infinite trees and Church’s synthesis prob-lem [19]. Although presented in a different form, his proof contains an implicit recursive procedure for solving Rabin games. A Rabin game is played on a finite arena by two players. Its winning condition is given by pairs (Li, Ri), and Player 0 wins a play if, for some pair, states from Li are visited infinitely often while states from Ri are visited only finitely often [1]. Thus, Rabin conditions express the simultaneous need to visit good sets infinitely often and to eventually avoid bad sets. The algorithmic problem of solving Rabin games is to compute the states from which Player 0 has a winning strategy. Emerson and Jutla showed that this problem is NP-complete [5]. Beyond its theoret-ical importance, the problem gained renewed relevance through the development of temporal synthesis initiated by Pnueli and Rosner [16]. In temporal synthesis, the goal is to automatically construct a system that satisfies a temporal specification against all possible behaviours of the environment. This problem can be reduced to solving Rabin games, with the system and the environment viewed as the two players, by converting an LTL formula to an equivalent deterministic Rabin automaton on infinite words [16]. Although Rabin conditions arise naturally in the automata-theoretic solution of reactive synthe-sis [16], modern synthesis pipelines often avoid solving Rabin games directly. Instead, they reduce the 102 Rabin Games Revisited 

problem to parity games and then apply parity-game solvers [10, 12]. This approach is well established and has led to efficient tools [14, 9], but it may also obscure the structure of the original Rabin condition. Indeed, it is explicitly observed that current Rabin-game solvers are not sufficiently efficient in practice [10]. This suggests that direct Rabin-game solving remains an important underexplored direction. One possible explanation is that most known direct algorithms for Rabin games have a complexity bound containing a factorial dependence on the Rabin index (the number of Rabin pairs) k. This leaves open whether a different algorithmic view of Rabin games can yield a different complexity tradeoff. In particular, Rabin’s 1972 algorithm has received comparatively little direct analysis. One reason is that the algorithm is not presented as a standalone solver: it is intertwined with the proof that whenever Player 0 wins a Rabin game, Player 0 also has a finite-state winning strategy. In addition, the original formulation yields an exponential dependence on the alphabet, to simplify the description of the algorithm. The goal of this paper is to contribute to the theory of Rabin games by first revisiting this original recursive construction. We start by “excavating” the algorithm implicit in Rabin’s 1972 proof and pre-senting it as an explicit solver for Rabin games. The resulting algorithm works directly with the Rabin condition. Our first observation is that the exponential dependence on the alphabet in Rabin’s original presentation can be eliminated. By formulating the construction directly over arenas and using standard reachability- and Büchi-game solving, the algorithm can be analyzed in terms of the size of the game representation rather than the size of the explicit alphabet expansion. Our second observation concerns memoization. A naive recursive implementation may solve the same normalized subproblem many times, leading to a factorial-size recursion tree. However, if recursive calls are memoized, then each distinct normalized subproblem is solved only once. This removes the explicit factorial term from the recursion-tree analysis, at the cost of an exponential dependence on the space of state-set configurations. Finally, we show that the recursion need not follow Rabin’s original parameter, which is the Rabin number, namely the number of states appearing in the right-hand sides of Rabin pairs. We offer a novel variation that recurses on the Rabin index, eliminating one Rabin pair at a time. This is significant for temporal synthesis, where the Rabin index can be much smaller than the Rabin number, and in particular may be logarithmic in it. Overall, this paper provides a modern presentation and analysis of Rabin’s recursive method, together with memoized state-based and index-based solvers. The purpose is not to claim dominance over existing parity-based or progress-measure algorithms, but to clarify a different algorithmic route for Rabin games. We believe this opens the door to future experimental work on whether direct Rabin-game solving can serve as an algorithmic basis for temporal synthesis.

Rabin objectives are expressible as Emerson–Lei objectives, but our construction does not proceed by building the Zielonka tree. Instead, it follows a Rabin-specific reduction: Rabin states or right-hand sides of Rabin pairs are declared unsafe, deleted from the condition, normalized, and already-winning states are folded into the reachability target. Thus the approaches solve overlapping classes of games, but their recursive structure and complexity tradeoffs are different.

# 4 Rabin’s Algorithm for Solving Rabin’s Games 

In this section, we describe the algorithm implicit in [19]. The description there is not as explicit as the description provided here for two reasons. First, Rabin’s presentation combines the description of the algorithm with a proof that Rabin games enjoy the positional-determinacy property (cf. [6]); that is, whenever a player has a winning strategy, it also has a memoryless winning strategy, represented by a function s : V → Σ0. Second, the framework in [19] dispenses with the alphabets Σ0 and Σ1 by absorbing them into the state set V of the arena. This simplifies the notation, but makes it more difficult to pinpoint the impact of alphabet size on the running time of the algorithm. Since in the reactive-synthesis applications studied in [19], as well as in many later works, e.g., [16], the alphabet is exponential in the size of the temporal-specification formula, we choose here to treat the alphabet explicitly rather than implicitly, in order to study its impact on the complexity of the algorithm. The explicit description of Rabin’s algorithm will lead to an improved algorithm in the next section. 

4.1 Rabin Games and Extended Rabin Games 

In a Rabin game, the winning condition is specified by a sequence ρ of pairs (Li, Ri) of subsets of V ;each pair is called a Rabin pair . A play satisfies a Rabin pair (L, R) if L is visited infinitely often and R is visited only finitely often along the play. We can assume wlog that L ∩ R = /0. A play satisfies the Rabin condition ρ if it satisfies at least one Rabin pair in ρ. The Rabin index of the game is the length of ρ.Here we also consider extended Rabin games, whose winning condition is specified by a tuple 

⟨U, F, B, ρ⟩, which combines unsafe states U, a reachability goal F, a Büchi goal B, and a Rabin condi-tion ρ. A play π satisfies this winning condition if it satisfies the reachability goal, the Büchi goal, or the Rabin goal, while avoiding unsafe states. Without loss of generality, we can assume that for each Rabin pair (L, R) we have (L ∪ R) ∩U = /0. We can also assume that no Rabin pair (L, R) has R = /0, since in this case we can just drop this pair and replace B by B ∪ L. Recall also that v0 cannot occur in the winning condition ⟨U, F, B, ρ⟩.

# 6 Conclusions 

We "excavated" here the Rabin-game-solving algorithm that is implicit in Rabin’s 1972 paper [19]. We obtained an explicit solver based on standard reachability- and Büchi-game solving. This perspective yields three main observations. First, the algorithm can be presented without the exponential dependence on the alphabet that appears in Rabin’s original formulation. Second, memoization removes the factorial term arising from the recursion-tree analysis by ensuring that each normalized subproblem is solved only once. Third, the recursion can be parameterized by the Rabin index rather than the Rabin number, yielding an alternative solver whose complexity depends on the number of Rabin pairs. We leave it to future work to compare the empirical performance of the two algorithms studied here. It also remains an interesting direction for future work to investigate symbolic implementations of these recursive procedures and to evaluate them experimentally. In particular, Rabin conditions arise naturally in automata-theoretic approaches to reactive synthesis, where an LTL specification is translated into a deterministic Rabin automaton. Nevertheless, current synthesis pipelines typically proceed by reducing the resulting Rabin game to a parity game. It would therefore be valuable to study the structure of the Rabin conditions that arise in practice and to assess whether direct Rabin-game solving can provide an effective algorithmic basis for temporal synthesis.
