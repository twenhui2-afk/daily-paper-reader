Qiao Zhang · Ying Wang · Xiao Li ·

Pardalos Abstract We study the restricted inverse optimal value problem on shortest paths under the weighted l∞ norm on trees (RIOVSPT ∞): adjust edge weights at minimum worst-link cost so that the shortest root–leaf path is lower-bounded by a given value D, a restriction on a prescribed path P0. Unlike the l1 version minimizing total adjustment cost, the l∞ objective controls the worst link, the criterion relevant in bandwidth pricing. We characterize feasibility through the minimum cost shortest path interdiction problem (MCSPIT ∞), whose optimal solution ˆ w splits the instance at the first edge of P0 into two independent subproblems. For (ISP) under the l2 norm, Burton and Toint [1] designed a solution algorithm. It builds upon the Goldfarb–Idnani method for convex quadratic programming and assumes mutually independent edge costs. They later generalized the algorithm to correlated edge costs [2]. Under the l1 norm, Zhang et al. [3] formulated (ISP) as a linear program of special structure and solved it by column generation whose columns are produced by shortest-path algorithms. Xu and Zhang [4] observed that the feasible adjusted weight vectors form a polyhedral cone. They described its extreme directions algebraically and graphically and related the problem to the minimum cutset problem. Under the Hamming distance, Zhang and Wang [5] established NP-hardness by a polynomial-time reduction from the knapsack problem. A different setting arises in the inverse optimal value problem on shortest path 

(IOVSP) , where the information given in advance is a target objective value rather than a desired solution. It has drawn less attention and comparatively is harder to solve than the problem (ISP) . Formally, on the weighted graph G = ( V, E, w ), the problem (IOVSP) aims at adjusting the weight vector to minimize the total adjustment cost under some measurement such that the length of the shortest path equals a prescribed value D. The weighted l∞ measurement adopted in the problem (RIOVSPT ∞) is also well motivated in the bandwidth pricing scenario in Fig.1 [9]. In practice, repricing a link is never free. Changing the quoted price of a link e by one unit Restricted Inverse Optimal Value Problem on Shortest Path 5

incurs a penalty c(e). This penalty reflects the sensitivity of the existing contracts through that link. It also captures the risk of customer churn and the attention from regulators. Minimizing the total cost may then pile a drastic change onto a few links. Such a change forces the renegotiation of the affected contracts, or even violates service-level agreements. What the provider really cares about is therefore the worst-affected link rather than the total bill. This makes the l∞ objective 

C( ˜ w) := max e∈E c(e)| ˜w(e)−w(e)| the natural choice. It controls the worst weighted adjustment directly and guarantees that no single link is repriced beyond tolerance. It need not equalize the adjustments, nor does it impose an externally specified threshold unless one is added separately. The pricing requirements are unchanged. Every side route must be priced at least D. A cheaper one would undercut the reference price and open an arbitrage. The designated route P0 is reserved for emergency communication and must be priced at exactly D. This keeps it as cheap as any alternative, so that it remains usable when an emergency occurs. Under the worst-link objective, these requirements no longer decouple from the cost. Adjusting the edges of P0 toward this target directly moves the worst weighted deviation on the shared links. Resolving this coupling is precisely the task of (RIOVSPT ∞).

Zhang et al. [9] solved the problem (RIOVSPT) under the weighted l1 norm with an O(n2) algorithm based on minimum cost cuts. This approach suits the aggregate cost criterion. But the total cost does not control the largest single-edge deviation: a drastic change may hide in one link. We therefore study the weighted 

l∞ objective, which controls the maximum weighted adjustment while the bounds and path constraints stay unchanged. The change of objective is more than a modeling nuance. On the modeling side, a fixed maximum-adjustment budget truncates the feasible interval of every edge. It is not a separable per-edge cost. On the algorithmic side, the l1 method works through minimum-cost cuts precisely because a total cost decomposes edge by edge, whereas the worst-case objective does not. New machinery is therefore necessary. In this paper, we solve the problem exactly in O(n log n) time. We analyze the structural properties of the problem, reduce the hard case to a parametric subproblem, and locate the optimal value by a binary search over the edge-bound 6 Q. Zhang, Y. Wang et al. 

breakpoints. The same machinery applies verbatim to the full tree, yielding a uniform algorithm with the same complexity. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the details of the solution in two ways. Section 2.1 introduces the notation, recalls the feasibility conditions, and establishes the decomposition used in the case analysis. Sections 2.2– 2.4 derive the optimal values and solution constructions for the three cases relative to an auxiliary interdiction solution; the budget characterization is developed in Section 2.4. Section 2.5 applies this characterization directly to the full tree and gives the uniform algorithm. Section 3 reports numerical experiments on correctness, running time, and case-specific behavior. Section 4 concludes the paper.

4 Conclusion 

We studied the restricted inverse optimal value problem on shortest root–leaf paths under the weighted l∞ norm on trees. The prescribed path must have length D and remain a shortest root–leaf path, while the maximum weighted edge adjustment is minimized. The feasibility conditions lead to two O(n log n)algorithms. The first uses a decomposition and a three-case analysis relative to an auxiliary interdiction solution; its hard case is solved through a parametric greedy subproblem. The second applies the budget-dependent feasibility margins directly to the full tree. Their continuity, monotonicity, and affine behavior between edge-bound breakpoints allow the optimal budget to be found by binary search followed by an exact threshold calculation. In the experiments, both algorithms returned the same optimal value as the LP solvers HiGHS and Gurobi on all 599 test instances, and they were faster than both solvers on all timing instances, typically by a factor of 5 to 10 and by more than an order of magnitude on large deep trees. Neither algorithm dominates the other: Algorithm 2 is faster when the instance is settled before the hard case, and Algorithm 3 is faster in the hard case with V (0) > D .Several questions remain open. First, this work is confined to trees. It would be interesting to find wider graph classes that still admit polynomial-time algorithms, such as series-parallel graphs or bounded-treewidth networks. Second, both of our algorithms run in O(n log n) time. Whether a linear-time algorithm exists remains open. Third, the model admits several variants. For example, one may relax the exact length requirement on P0 to an interval, prescribe several paths at once, or consider other underlying problems such as minimum spanning trees.
