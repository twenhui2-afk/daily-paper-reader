# Planar Disk Filters and Real Chromatic Roots at Beraha Numbers 

Reza Mactabi ∗

3 October 2026 

Abstract 

We construct simple plane triangulations whose chromatic polynomials have real roots accumulating at every noninteger Beraha number Bℓ = 4 cos 2(π/ℓ ); the integer values 0 , 1, 2, 3are roots of K4. The construction rests on the observation that a triangulated disk with four boundary vertices acts, inside a larger triangulation, as a transfer operator lying in a commutative three-parameter algebra. Rotating such disks by a quarter turn and composing them in series moves their eigenvalue triples around this algebra in a controllable way, and for every fixed 

q ∈ (B10 , 4) one can manufacture a finite disk whose two competing local eigenvalues are strictly dominated by the third — a physical filter . Closing a grid of such disks between two poles produces a sphere triangulation, and a fused Temperley–Lieb trace identity accounts for its chromatic polynomial completely, sector by sector, with no appeal to semisimplicity at the root of unity itself — generic semisimplicity enters only through polynomial identities continued from 

δ > 2 — and no singular higher Jones–Wenzl projectors. In the one-cap sector a Krein-type argument shows that every sweep word has exactly one expanding eigenvalue; Gram-form inertia controls every other sector uniformly in the width. At odd Beraha indices the trace coefficient of the expanding eigenvalue vanishes; at even indices two copies of that eigenvalue cancel between adjacent sectors, and a short real-variable lemma converts either situation into genuine real roots converging to the target. Indices below eleven are treated by elementary families, exact outward-rounded integer certificates, and one explicit disk at B10 combined with Tutte's golden identity. All graphs are ordinary, simple, unweighted plane triangulations. 21 The problem and the strategy 

Write χG(q) for the chromatic polynomial of a finite graph G. A simple plane triangulation is a simple connected graph embedded in the sphere with every face a triangle; choosing any face as the outer face gives the equivalent plane formulation. The numbers 

Bℓ = 4 cos 2 πℓ , ℓ = 2 , 3, 4, . . . 

are the Beraha numbers [Be]. We address the planar real-root conjecture in the precise formulation of [JS, Conjecture 1.3]: for every ℓ ≥ 2 and every ε > 0, a simple plane triangulation has a real chromatic root within ε of Bℓ. The integer values are already roots of K4; the substantive accumulation claim concerns the noninteger values. Theorem 1.1. Every noninteger Beraha number — Bℓ with ℓ = 5 or ℓ ≥ 7 — is an accumulation point of real chromatic roots of simple plane triangulations: for every ε > 0 some simple plane triangulation has a real chromatic root in (Bℓ − ε, B ℓ + ε) \ { Bℓ}. At the integer values B2, B 3, B 4, B 6 = 0 , 1, 2, 3,which are roots of χK4 , no accumulation statement is made or needed. 

Two boundary remarks delimit what is and is not new in the statement. Royle [R] proved that real chromatic roots of plane triangulations approach 4; the endpoint is therefore not claimed here. Perrett and Thomassen [PT] proved density of real chromatic roots for the much wider class of planar graphs, away from a small interval containing B10 ; their graphs are not triangulations, and the localization demanded by Theorem 1.1 — a root near each prescribed Bℓ, within the triangulation class — does not follow from their result. The quantum-group approach to Beraha accumulation predates this paper by decades. Saleur [S] analyzed the relevant lattice systems and explicitly stopped short of a full rigorous proof; Kauffman and Saleur [KS] later described that accumulation argument as heuristic. Jacobsen and Saleur [BCP] distinguished vanishing amplitudes from sector crossings and explained exact finite-width coincidences through representation theory — mechanisms that are genuine antecedents of what happens below. Biggs [B1, B2] developed compatibility matrices and representation-theoretic decompositions for cyclic graph families; neither transfer matrices nor representation theory is claimed as new here. Agol and Krushkal [AK] used Temperley–Lieb positivity to bound chromatic values of planar triangulations at Beraha numbers; a bound on values does not by itself produce the real roots required by Theorem 1.1. The author's earlier manuscript [M] addresses particular finite targets and is not a prerequisite here. The proposed contribution of this paper is the disk construction at a variable target, the uniform all-width sector analysis of the complete closure, and their assembly through real sign-change lemmas into the full index range. Theorem 1.1 concerns prescribed Beraha limit points. No claim is made that these exhaust the possible real accumulation points of plane triangulations. 

1.1 The strategy in one page 

It helps to see the whole arc before the details begin. The engine of every transfer-matrix approach to this problem is the same: build a one-parameter family of graphs Gt by repeating a gadget t times around a cycle, so that χGt (q) becomes a finite sum ∑

First difficulty: dominance . For raw gadgets the "entropic" eigenvalues — the ones counting bulk colorings — tend to beat the structured eigenvalue whose amplitude vanishes. Section 2 3deals with this head-on. We show that triangulated disks with four boundary terminals form a commutative three-parameter calculus under serial composition, with a quarter-turn rotation acting as an algebraic involution-like move (Lemma 2.1 through display (D6)), and that inside this calculus one can steer: for every fixed q ∈ (B10 , 4) there is a finite disk whose eigenvalue triple ( u, v, e ) satisfies 

u < 0 < v, e < −u (Proposition 2.2). Once each repeating cell is such a filter , both competing local channels contract and the global dominance question can be addressed using the carrier and Gram-form arguments of Sections 5 and 6; local contraction alone does not settle it. Second difficulty: completeness of the spectral accounting . It is fatally easy to analyze some sectors of a transfer operator and silently postulate that no others contribute. Section 4 derives, from the Fendley–Krushkal chromatic algebra map and the standard quantum trace, a single boxed identity (T6) expressing χGt as an explicitly weighted sum of traces over fused link-state sectors, with bases constructed directly and no division by vanishing quantum integers. The identity is checked symbolically on nine complete graph polynomials in Appendix B, and its derivation is diagrammatic. Third difficulty: what happens at the root of unity . At q = Bℓ the Temperley–Lieb algebra is not semisimple, and standard-module Gram forms degenerate. Rather than fighting this, the proof uses it. Section 5 shows that the one-cap sector carries, for every sweep word, exactly one expanding eigenvalue — a Krein-space argument on a tridiagonal form H (Lemma 5.1), with the eigenvalue given in closed form (H4) for the ascending sweep. Section 6 then controls every other sector by Gram inertia: positive-definite sectors give operator norms at most one; the single semidefinite sector has a one-dimensional radical on which everything acts trivially; and at even indices the celebrated reflection structure of Ridout and Saint-Aubin produces a second copy of the carrier inside the adjacent sector, whose quotient we prove positive by a derivative argument (Proposition 6.2, displays (G6a)–(G6b)). Fourth difficulty: real roots rather than complex ones . The Beraha–Kahane–Weiss theory [BKW, BO] produces limit points of complex zeros; it cannot by itself produce real zeros, and the distinction is not pedantic — fm(x) = x2 + 1 /m has complex zeros converging to the real point 0 and no real zero at all. Section 7 therefore proves two self-contained sign-change lemmas tailored to the exact hypotheses established earlier: one for a single vanishing amplitude (the odd-index mechanism), one for a cancelling pair of amplitudes attached to branches that may coincide at the target (the even-index mechanism, where no slope-separation assumption is available or needed). The assembly is then short. Section 8 proves Theorem 1.1 for every ℓ ≥ 11, both parities. Section 9 handles 2 ≤ ℓ ≤ 10: the integers via K4, B5 via bipyramids, B7, B 8, B 9 via the elementary disk and exact interval certificates, and the golden boundary B10 via one explicit composite disk with 419 ,495 internal vertices. Section 10 proves the produced roots are distinct from their targets — by a conjugate-in-(0 , 1) argument at most indices, and by Tutte's golden identity at B10 . 2.1 The local three-dimensional algebra 

Let D be a graph embedded in a disk with boundary cycle ( l, x, r, X ), where boundary-adjacent colors are required to differ. We think of l and r as poles and of x, X as the input and output of a path that the disk will join. For a fixed proper boundary coloring, let WD count its extensions to proper colorings of the internal vertices. 2.2 Rotation and serial composition 

Two operations generate everything we need. A quarter turn of the entire disk interchanges the roles of the two opposite boundary pairs; on parameters this swaps B and C while fixing D0, and on eigenvalue triples it acts by 

R(u, v, e ) = (B − D0, B + ( a − 1) D0, B − D0 + ( a + 1) C). (D6) 

Serial composition glues the output path ( l, X, r ) of one disk to the input path of the next; summing over the shared middle color multiplies the transfer operators, and since both lie in the commutative algebra generated by (D3), their eigenvalue triples simply multiply componentwise. The calculus is thus a semigroup of triples, acted on by the rotation R.The basic generators are the fans . Remark (fixed-disk discipline). The disk produced depends on the target q∗ and is then held fixed: its triple ( u, v, e ) consists of polynomials in q, the inequalities (D8) hold at q∗, hence on a neighborhood by continuity, and the scale g = −u entering (T6) is the true polynomial eigenvalue of this fixed disk. The limiting construction is never allowed to vary the graph with q inside a sign-change argument. # 10 Distinct roots and completion of the proof 

It remains to show the roots produced above differ from their targets. First, for any connected loopless graph, χG is nonzero on (0 , 1) with sign ( −1) |V |− 1: by deletion–contraction, a bridge contributes χG = ( q − 1) χG/e , while for a nonbridge the deletion stays connected and the deletion and negated contraction terms share the asserted sign; parallel edges arising from contraction are harmless; the one-vertex case starts the induction. For odd ℓ = 2 n + 1 ≥ 5, write Bℓ = 2 + ζ + ζ−1 with ζ = e2πi/ℓ . Since gcd( n, ℓ ) = 1, the algebraic conjugate 2 + ζn + ζ−n = 2 − 2 cos( π/ℓ ) lies in (0 , 1); a chromatic root is an algebraic integer whose conjugates are all chromatic roots of the same polynomial, so Bℓ cannot be one. # 12 Discussion and relation to prior work 

One way to summarize the mechanism: the single angle θ = π/ℓ controls both the quantum-integer coefficients in the trace and the locations of Gram degeneracies. At odd indices a coefficient vanishes; at even indices a reflected radical plants a second copy of the carrier whose coefficient cancels the 30 first. That is the precise role the trigonometric parameter plays here — no more, and no less. The contributions of this paper are: the disk realization of Proposition 2.2; the all-width use of Gram inertia, including the derivative argument (G6a)–(G6b); the carrier dominance lemma; and their assembly through the real cancellation lemmas into the full index range. The representation-theoretic determinant, the reflection theorem, the chromatic map, the quantum trace, and the golden identity are credited inputs, not novelty claims; nor is the general contrast between vanishing amplitudes and exact inter-sector coincidences claimed as new — see [BCP].

The filter construction changes the local disk instead of keeping a fixed lattice cell. Its local bounds must then be combined with the complete trace, carrier dominance, and Gram inertia. In particular, a local bound larger than one for an elementary cell does not by itself prove that a competing global sector dominates. The fused construction divides only by the nonzero loop parameter and uses standard modules at the target; it does not require singular higher projectors. These are the specific mathematical claims to be checked against prior work and under independent review.
