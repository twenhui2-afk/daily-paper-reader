Abstract 

Knowledge acquisition from black-box large language models (LLMs) offers a practical approach to adapting smaller student models. However, many existing methods rely on pre-defined queries or student feedback to guide acquisition, leaving autonomous expansion of knowledge targets an important challenge. We propose MapSteal , a student-independent framework for structured recursive knowledge acquisition. Starting from a small set of ini-tial roots, MapSteal represents acquisition tar-gets using structured states and recursively ex-pands them through teacher-proposed direc-tions. Question realization, proposal admission, and deduplication constrain the acquisition pro-cess, while root-aware scheduling organizes ex-ploration under a fixed QA budget. The result-ing question–answer pairs are used to adapt stu-dent models without requiring student feedback during acquisition. Experiments with Qwen3.5 students at 2B, 4B, and 9B scales show compet-itive downstream performance across six medi-cal and cybersecurity benchmarks. On the 4B medical evaluation, Full MapSteal improves av-erage accuracy by 1.17 percentage points over a simplified recursive acquisition variant. Cross-domain evaluations show that gains depend on the domain and evaluation panel. These results demonstrate the feasibility of struc-tured, student-independent recursive acquisi-tion while highlighting the distinction between generating acquisition targets and obtaining useful training supervision. Our code is avail-able at https://github.com/LabRAI/Mapsteal. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) provide a valuable source of supervision for adapting smaller models to specialized domains and tasks. When a strong teacher is accessible only through a black-box API, a student can collect teacher-generated responses and use them for training without accessing the

teacher’s parameters or original training data. This paradigm has been extensively studied in model extraction and knowledge distillation, from func-tionality stealing in classifiers (Orekondy et al., 2018; Krishna et al., 2020) to capability extraction from modern language models (Birch et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a). However, acquiring supervision from a black-box teacher can be expensive, and the space of possible queries is too large to explore exhaustively. Un-der a limited query budget, the central challenge is therefore to acquire knowledge that improves downstream student performance efficiently. Prior work addresses this challenge through sev-eral complementary approaches. Query-efficient extraction methods select informative examples based on uncertainty, diversity, or disagreement (Pal et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2023; Baykal et al., 2023). Data-free approaches synthesize queries to expose student–teacher discrepancies (Kariyappa et al., 2022; Truong et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). Student-aware methods further adapt gen-erated supervision to the learner’s observed weak-nesses (Liu et al., 2024), while other approaches improve how acquired responses are used for train-ing (Liang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Although these methods improve query se-lection, generation, and student adaptation, they raise a complementary question: what knowledge should become queryable next as acquisition pro-ceeds? Selecting informative queries is insufficient when potentially useful knowledge targets are not represented in the current candidate space. Constructing such targets introduces additional challenges. First, an evolving acquisition process must represent not only individual questions but also their knowledge objects, contextual conditions, and relationships to previously acquired informa-tion. Without an explicit acquisition state, recur-sive question generation can be difficult to organize. Second, deriving acquisition targets directly from arXiv:2610.25626v1 [cs.CL] 19 Oct 2026 Student failures can be unreliable. An incorrect prediction may reflect question-specific difficulty rather than a transferable knowledge deficit. In our preliminary Medicine diagnostics, 129 of 179 ini-tially incorrect examples were answered correctly after independent reformulation (Appendix F). This observation motivates an alternative that does not require explicit Student failure attribution. To address these challenges, we propose Map-Steal , a structured framework for recursive knowl-edge acquisition from black-box LLMs. MapSteal represents each acquisition target using five com-ponents: Object, Facet, Condition, TargetSpec, and Decision (O/F/C/T/D). Starting from a small set of domain roots, the framework maintains an evolving knowledge map of answered and pending acquisi-tion states. Each executed query allows the teacher to provide both an answer and candidate child tar-gets. Typed expansion operators modify the struc-tured states, while admission rules filter invalid or redundant proposals. A root-level fair sched-uler allocates acquisition opportunities across the growing frontier under a fixed query budget. The resulting question–answer pairs are collected to adapt the student after acquisition. Unlike student-feedback-driven approaches, MapSteal does not require online student training or explicit diagnosis of student weaknesses during acquisition. We evaluate MapSteal across multiple student scales and knowledge domains. The results show that structured recursive acquisition provides use-ful downstream supervision under limited QA bud-gets. Controlled ablations on Medicine show that the full structured formulation improves the three-benchmark average by 1.17 percentage points over a simplified Object–Facet variant, while removing condition-based expansion reduces performance by 1.05 points. Additional analyses examine sensitiv-ity to initial acquisition roots and characterize the trade-off between recursive depth, semantic nov-elty, and local coherence. 

Our contributions are threefold: 

• Explicit acquisition-state formulation. We formulate budget-constrained knowledge ac-quisition as the construction and execution of an evolving space of structured knowledge targets, distinguishing query-space expansion from selection within the available frontier. • Structured recursive acquisition. We intro-duce MapSteal, which combines O/F/C/T/D acquisition states, teacher-guided typed ex-pansion, proposal admission, and root-level fair scheduling to support traceable recursive knowledge acquisition without online student failure attribution. • Empirical evaluation and analysis. We eval-uate the framework across student scales and domains, investigate its structured compo-nents through controlled ablations, and char-acterize the effects of acquisition roots, query budgets, and recursive exploration behavior. 

2 Methodology 

Overview. MapSteal acquires domain-oriented su-pervision from a black-box teacher under a finite query budget. Instead of diagnosing student errors online, it maintains an explicit frontier of structured knowledge-acquisition targets. Each successful teacher interaction answers the selected target and proposes possible successors. The successors are validated, represented as new states, and scheduled for later execution. The student is adapted only after acquisition is complete. Figure 1 summarizes the procedure.

2.2 Structured Knowledge Acquisition Map 

Each acquisition target is represented by an explicit schema consisting of five components, 

s = ( O, F, C, T, D ), (2) where O identifies a knowledge Object , F its Facet ,

C a contextual Condition , T a TargetSpec describ-ing the information sought, and D the correspond-ing Decision intent. Optional fields may be empty. These fields specify an acquisition request, not ver-ified domain facts; in particular, T is distinct from the final surface-form question. MapSteal represents acquisition targets as struc-tured records rather than isolated generated ques-tions. We define the acquisition map as Mt =(Vt, E t), where Vt contains admitted acquisition states and Et records directed parent–child links. Each state maintains its source root and parent provenance, allowing recursive expansion to be traced explicitly. A single ordered frontier Ft ⊆ Vt

contains states awaiting execution; successfully ex-ecuted states are recorded in the acquisition trajec-tory. Rejected proposals remain available in au-dit records with their rejection reasons but are not placed on the executable frontier. Neither the graph edges nor the schema assert ontological, causal, or otherwise verified relationships between domain entities. For example, a target concerning multiple-sclerosis clinical features and diagnosis may pro-duce a child concerning the same target during pregnancy. The child preserves the knowledge ob-ject while changing its condition and target speci-fication. This transition is recorded as acquisition provenance. A syntactically valid combination of schema fields is not automatically a useful target: new states enter the frontier through teacher propos-als and explicit admission checks, not exhaustive cross-products or object–object joins. 

2.3 Teacher-Guided Recursive Expansion Question realization and teacher interaction. 

For a selected root state, MapSteal uses the ques-tion supplied in the frozen root manifest. For a non-root state, a deterministic realizer converts its TargetSpec and Decision intent into a knowledge-seeking question, including condition context when needed. Realization failure causes the state to be discarded before any teacher call. The prompt for a successfully realized state includes the current structured state and root-specific direction-registry context. In one successful teacher interaction, T

returns an answer, an explanation, and typed child-state proposals; the teacher proposes states , rather than directly supplying the final surface questions for those children. The direction registry records prior teacher-proposed directions for that root and informs the prompt, but not the scheduler. 

Typed state transitions. Each proposed child is constructed using one of four operators. Table 1 gives their field-level effects. Every operator sup-plies a new TargetSpec and Decision intent; an inherited Condition is a field of the state, not a cross-root resource. Validation and admission. The interaction has three distinct safeguards. First, the returned teacher response must satisfy its schema; an invalid response may be retried once with the same request. Second, proposed children undergo operator-specific validity and admission checks, including required fields, valid Decision intents, parent/ancestor TargetSpec constraints, Decision– TargetSpec compatibility, and duplicate identities. Deduplication uses normalized exact matching, in-cluding across roots, rather than embedding simi-larity. Third, question realization is checked only when an admitted child is later selected for execu-tion. Proposal-level rejection does not consume a successful QA budget slot. Accepted children are appended to the frontier and linked to their executed parent; rejected proposals and reasons remain in the trajectory audit. This separation be-tween proposing, admitting, and executing targets makes recursive acquisition explicit and traceable.

Each successfully acquired record contains a ques-tion, answer, and explanation. Offline QC removes malformed supervision and duplicate question texts. If cold-start supervision is supplied, it precedes ac-quired records in the QC input; consequently, a later acquired duplicate of an existing seed ques-tion is removed. 3 Experiments 

3.1 Setup Models and Training. We use GPT-4.1 as the black-box Teacher and Qwen3.5-2B, 4B, and 9B as Students (Team, 2026). Students are adapted using LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) after knowledge acquisition. Unless otherwise noted, we report mean ± standard deviation over three seeds. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix A. 

Benchmarks and Evaluation. We evaluate knowledge transfer in two domains: Medicine and Cybersecurity. For Medicine, we use MedM-CQA (Pal et al., 2022), MedQA (Jin et al., 2020), and MedXpert (Zuo et al., 2025). For MedMCQA, we evaluate on a fixed subset of 500 questions from the official validation (dev) split, as the test labels are not publicly available. For Cybersecurity, we use CyberMetric (Tihanyi et al., 2024), SecEval (Li et al., 2023), and CTI-Bench (Alam et al., 2024). We report classification or multiple-choice accu-racy (%) on the corresponding evaluation splits. For domain-level comparisons, we compute the un-weighted mean across the three benchmarks within each domain. This aggregation gives equal weight to each benchmark rather than to each individual evaluation question. 

Baselines. We compare MapSteal with the fol-lowing methods. Pristine denotes the original Stu-dent checkpoint without task-specific adaptation. We additionally compare against EvoKD (Liu et al., 2024) and QEDKS (Li et al., 2026a), which serve as knowledge acquisition and distillation baselines. 

MapSteal is our structured, Teacher-guided recur-sive acquisition method. All adapted methods are evaluated with the same Student model family and corresponding benchmark protocols. 

Acquisition Budget. We use a nominal budget of B = 500 QA supervision instances. Both prede-fined root questions and recursively generated ques-tions require Teacher responses and count toward this budget. Further protocol details are provided in Appendix A. Teacher token usage and estimated acquisition costs are reported in Appendix E.2.

3.3 Ablation Study 

We investigate three aspects of MapSteal: the con-tribution of structured acquisition, sensitivity to the number of initial acquisition roots, and the effect of increasing the acquisition budget. Unless otherwise stated, experiments use Qwen3.5-4B on Medicine with the same Student adaptation configuration.

4 Related Work 

Black-Box Model Extraction and Distillation. 

Model extraction trains surrogate models through black-box query access. Early studies demon-strate functionality stealing for image classifiers and NLP models using selected or synthetic queries (Orekondy et al., 2018; Krishna et al., 2020). Recent work extends this setting to black-box LLMs. Model Leeching acquires task-specific supervision (Birch et al., 2023), while LoRD (Liang et al., 2024), GAD, and SODA de-velop strategies for improving Student adaptation from Teacher responses (Ye et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026) MapSteal focuses on the preceding ac-quisition stage: how to autonomously construct and expand knowledge targets before Student training. 

Query Selection and Generation. Query-efficient model extraction commonly uses active learning to select informative samples, as in ActiveThief and subsequent text-classification methods (Pal et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2023). Data-free approaches instead synthesize queries to expose differences between the victim and surrogate (Kariyappa et al., 2022; Truong et al., 2021). Adaptive Knowledge Acquisition. Iterative distil-lation methods adapt acquired supervision as Stu-dent capabilities evolve. EvoKD generates new ex-amples from Student predictions (Liu et al., 2024), while INTERACT supports multi-turn acquisition of unseen concepts (Kendapadi et al., 2025). Re-lated approaches use uncertainty, model errors, or capability-driven task generation to guide fur-ther learning (Kessler et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). State-aware information-gathering methods, including BED-LLM and Dual-Graph, also organize exploration through explicit belief or knowledge states (Choudhury et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). MapSteal in-stead maintains structured acquisition states and expands Teacher-proposed targets from predefined roots, separating autonomous knowledge acquisi-tion from subsequent Student adaptation. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced MapSteal, a student-independent framework that recursively expands structured knowledge targets under a fixed QA budget. By explicitly representing acquisition states and their relationships, MapSteal organizes teacher-guided exploration without requiring Student feedback dur-ing acquisition. Its structured operators enable acquisition to branch across knowledge objects, facets, and contextual conditions while preserving the provenance of generated questions. Across three Student scales and two domains, MapSteal achieves the highest mean accuracy in 12 of 18 settings, including three medical benchmarks at the 4B scale and the highest SecEval accuracy at every Student scale. Ablations show that replac-ing the full formulation with a simplified Object– Facet variant lowers the three-benchmark average by 1.17 points, and removing condition-based ex-pansion lowers it by 1.05 points. Varying the num-ber of initial roots changes recursive depth substan-tially, from a mean of 5.27 with one root to 1.87 with 35, while downstream accuracy remains close across 1 to 20 roots. Increasing the QA budget from 40 to 500 raises the Medical3 average from 47.27 to 48.86. Qualitative analyses further illus-trate how recursive acquisition generates context-specific knowledge targets from a limited set of roots. Cross-domain gains vary by domain, mo-tivating future work on assessing and prioritizing acquisition targets to improve the effectiveness of recursive knowledge transfer. Limitations 

Acquisition variability. GPT-4.1 responses may vary across API calls even under identical prompts and settings, so identical roots and configurations do not guarantee identical acquisition trajectories. We use one Teacher acquisition trajectory per con-figuration with multiple Student training seeds, so the reported standard deviations do not capture vari-ability across independent acquisition runs. 

Acquisition quality and scope. Our acquisition analysis measures structural and semantic prop-erties of generated questions, not independently verified knowledge quality or domain coverage. The main evaluation covers medical and cybersecu-rity benchmarks with Qwen3.5, and cross-domain gains vary by domain and evaluation panel. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies structured knowledge acquisi-tion from black-box language models for model distillation and small-model adaptation. Such tech-niques can support reproducible research, but they also present dual-use risks: recursive acquisition could be used to imitate capabilities of proprietary models without authorization. The use and redis-tribution of Teacher outputs may be subject to API terms, licensing conditions, and intellectual prop-erty restrictions, and researchers applying these methods should respect applicable access permis-sions, service terms, and data-use requirements. Our experiments include medical and cybersecu-rity domains, where incorrect acquired supervision could produce unreliable downstream predictions; the resulting Student models should not be treated as clinically validated or as suitable for security-critical deployment without further evaluation. We release implementation materials to support repro-ducibility and evaluation, and we encourage re-sponsible access to and use of black-box model interfaces.

D.4 Interpretation 

The cross-domain results distinguish recursive ac-quisition activity from the downstream usefulness of acquired supervision. Admission rates, recur-sion depth, and semantic novelty do not consis-tently predict performance changes. For example, Medicine shows relatively low sequential novelty but a positive acquisition gain, whereas Law main-tains recursive expansion without a corresponding downstream improvement. Domains differ in root construction, evaluation panels, and adaptation con-figurations, and most use a single Teacher acquisi-tion trajectory. Overall, structured recursive acqui-sition expands domain-specific knowledge targets, and its downstream benefit depends on the acquired supervision and the evaluation setting. 

E Baseline and Resource Details 

E.1 Run-to-Run Variability of EvoKD 

Table 11 reports the individual EvoKD results us-ing the Qwen3.5-2B Student on the medical and cybersecurity benchmarks. Unlike MapSteal, which uses three training seeds on one fixed acquisition trajectory, EvoKD uses three independent acquisition and online adap-tation runs. Its reported variation therefore com-bines changes in acquisition and Student training. The 2B results exhibit substantial run-to-run vari-ation, particularly on MedMCQA, MedQA, and CTI-Bench. In Medicine, seed 42 produces lower accuracy than the other two runs on MedMCQA and MedQA. In Cybersecurity, seed 43 shows the largest decline on CyberMetric and CTI-Bench, whereas seed 44 obtains the lowest SecEval accu-racy. All three recorded runs are included in the reported results, without excluding low-performing trajectories. Since acquisition and online adapta-tion vary jointly, these observations do not isolate the source of the variability.

F Preliminary Failure Attribution Diagnostic 

Before developing MapSteal, we examined whether incorrect Student predictions consistently indicate transferable knowledge deficits. In a pre-liminary Medicine diagnostic with Qwen3.5-4B, we evaluated error persistence under independent question reformulation.

Of the 195 initially incorrect examples, 16 am-biguous or unsuitable cases were excluded. Among the remaining 179 examples, only 50 (27.9%) re-mained incorrect after reformulation, while 129 (72.1%) were answered correctly. These results suggest that individual Student errors may be sen-sitive to question formulation rather than reflecting stable knowledge deficits. This preliminary diag-nostic motivates Student-independent acquisition but does not establish that Student-aware methods are ineffective.
