# Multiple propagating modes in random polycrystals

Multiple-scattering theory predicts that the coherent elastic wave in a statistically isotropic poly-crystal is not a single wave. Its dispersion equation admits multiple roots of a given polarization, but these extra modes have never been observed. Here, using grain-scale finite-element simulations of three-dimensional polycrystals, we resolve the coherent field in the frequency–wavenumber plane and directly observe the predicted multiplicity for both transverse and longitudinal excitations: a single mode at low frequencies gives way to two in the geometric regime as the wavelength approaches the grain correlation length. The measured modes follow the roots of the dispersion equation, which always carries a third root whose attenuation exceeds that of the others. It is this attenuation hier-archy, not the root count, that dictates how many modes propagate and can be observed. Coherent wave propagation in random media is thus inherently multimodal in the geometric regime, which we reach here for the first time in polycrystals. 

An elastic wave traversing a random medium loses en-ergy from its coherent, ensemble-averaged component to the scattered field, and the surviving coherent wave is conventionally described by a single complex effective wavenumber per polarization [1–3]. Yet a statistically homogeneous random medium can carry more than one wave of a given polarization. Biot predicted this first for fluid-saturated porous solids, whose coupled solid–fluid motion supports fast and slow compressional waves [4, 5], confirmed ultrasonically later by Plona [6]. Brillouin-scattering measurements on colloidal suspensions then revealed two longitudinal modes once the wavelength be-came comparable to the sphere size [7]. These were ex-plained as quasimodes [8], and were later mapped in detail by pulsed ultrasonics [9, 10]. Most recently, for random particulate composites, it has been proved that the ensemble-averaged field is a superposition of infinitely many effective waves rather than one wave [11, 12], and an independent variational treatment found that a layer of random composite supports exactly two mean plane waves [13]. In all of these systems, however, the multi-plicity is tied to the presence of two constituents. Single-phase polycrystals pose the question in its purest form. There is no second constituent, and the only disorder is the random orientation of elastically anisotropic grains. For scalar waves in continuous ran-dom media, Frisch [14], following Tatarskii and Gert-senshtein [15], gave the mean Green’s function as two terms with distinct wavenumbers, whose attenuations and amplitudes become comparable at high frequency (see also [16]), and Calvet and Margerin [17] showed that the second root is demanded by causality, because a sin-gle mode with the asymptotic velocity and attenuation of the geometric limit cannot satisfy the Kramers–Kronig relations. For elastic waves, the far-field approximation of Rokhlin et al. [18], like the second-order theory of Stanke and Kino [19], yields two longitudinal and trans-verse roots. In polycrystals, however, this multiplicity has remained purely theoretical: no additional mode has ever been observed, and it has not been known whether the extra roots are physical waves or artifacts of the ap-proximations that produce them. In this Letter we supply that observation for statis-tically isotropic polycrystals. Grain-scale finite-element simulations, which solve the elastodynamic equations di-rectly and invoke no scattering theory, with the coherent field examined in the frequency–wavenumber plane, re-solve two transverse modes and two longitudinal modes, emerging when the wavelength becomes comparable to the grain correlation length. The simulations also show the mechanism directly: the injected plane wave is ex-tinguished within about one correlation length, and the coherent field beyond is rebuilt by forward multiple scat-tering, whose distinct longitudinal and transverse chan-nels support distinct modes. Comparison with theory then completes the picture. The dispersion equation ad-mits three roots at all frequencies, but only in the short-wavelength geometric regime do two of them attain com-parable attenuation while retaining distinct phase veloc-ities, becoming separately observable. The third root attenuates faster throughout and cannot be observed at any frequency. This means that the number of propa-gating modes is set not by the number of roots but by their attenuation hierarchy. We demonstrate these find-ings in two representative cubic materials of common and extreme anisotropy, and the material choice only shifts the frequency at which the geometric regime occurs. We first consider transverse excitation in statistically isotropic polycrystalline iron, formed by cubic symmetry grains with elastic constants c11 = 219 .2, c12 = 136 .8and c44 = 109 .2 GPa, density ρ = 7860 kg/m 3, Zener anisotropy index A = 2 .65 and universal anisotropy in-dex [20] AU = 1.23. Theory and simulation agree closely. The phase ve-locities of both modes are reproduced across the en-tire simulated frequency range, and the attenuation co-efficients agree wherever a single mode propagates. In the geometric regime the measured attenuation deviates from the prediction. The deviation stems not from the Lorentzian fitting but from the theory, which is used here at 2 αa C ≳ 1, beyond weak scattering. Theory and simulation explain the multiplicity. At low frequencies where 2 k0T aC < 9, mode 1 propagates marginally slower than V0T , sampling the two transverse channels equally in an effective fashion, and its attenu-ation lies orders of magnitude below that of the other roots. This explains why conventional ultrasonic mea-surements observe one transverse wave, and only one. Beyond 2 k0T aC ≃ 9 the geometric regime is reached, and propagation is better pictured as rays [31] that travel between scattering events predominantly in one of the three channels of L, T1 and T2, with mode conversion linking them. The three roots inherit the three channel velocities, with mode 1 riding predominantly the slow transverse channel, mode 2 the fast one, and mode 3 the longitudinal channel. Crucially, in this regime modes 1 and 2 have attenuation coefficients of the same order, as seen in Fig. 2(c) from both theory and simulation, so both survive over comparable distances and both appear in the spectra. Mode 3 is different in kind. The repeated L–T conversion it requires, involving large-angle deflec-tion and high-contrast transmission at grain boundaries, keeps its attenuation above the others across the entire frequency range, and this is the reason why it is never visible in our numerical experiments. Longitudinal excitation completes the picture. However, theory and simulation follow the same profile over the whole frequency range for both phase velocity and attenuation: they both show clear onset of the geo-metric regime. That an approximate theory retains this much at AU = 17 .67, an anisotropy an order of magni-tude beyond that of iron, indicates that the multimodal structure it predicts is robust and not an artifact of weak scattering. These results repeat the transverse story with the roles rescaled by anisotropy. At low frequencies, mode 1 trav-els near V0L , with a larger offset than in iron commen-surate with the higher anisotropy, and it is the only weakly attenuated root, so the effective-medium descrip-tion holds and one longitudinal wave is seen. The transi-tion now occurs at 2 k0L aC ≃ 3 ( λ0L ≃ 4aC), earlier than in iron due to the stronger anisotropy. In the geometric regime, mode 1 speeds up toward the fast propagation paths, mode 2 is slowest with its propagation dominated by the slow transverse channel, and the two attain com-parable attenuation, making both observable. Mode 3 propagates at an intermediate velocity but again attenu-ates faster than the others. Two conclusions follow. First, the multiple roots of the effective dispersion equation are physical. An approxi-mate theory built on the first-order smoothing approxi-mation correctly predicts, for both polarizations and at both anisotropies, which modes appear, where in fre-quency they separate, and their dispersion. The extra roots are not artifacts of the approximation, and the agreement across the two very different materials indi-cates that the multimodal structure is a generic property of statistically isotropic polycrystals. The observation equally vindicates the causality argument of Calvet and Margerin [17] that in the geometric regime, a single-mode description of the coherent field is not merely inaccurate but forbidden, and the modes that restore causality are in principle observable waves. Second, the number of ob-servable modes is governed by the attenuation hierarchy 5of the roots. Equation (1) has three roots at most fre-quencies. One mode is always observable, the second be-comes observable in the geometric regime as the attenua-tion for both modes becomes comparable, while the third decays faster at all frequencies and is unobservable. This answers, for polycrystals, the question raised for particu-late materials [11]: of the many mathematically admissi-ble effective waves, those that survive are the ones whose attenuation permits propagation over many microstruc-tural lengths. The mechanism identified here, in which distinct forward-scattering channels carry distinct coherent modes once the wavelength resolves the microstructure, invokes nothing specific to elasticity. The Dyson equa-tion and its dispersion relation govern coherent propaga-tion in all random media [2, 14], and comparable multi-modality should arise for electromagnetic waves in ran-dom dielectrics and for high-frequency seismic waves in the heterogeneous Earth [32] and other planetary bod-ies; the result bears on theories of high-frequency energy transport [26]. In summary, grain-scale simulations resolve the coher-ent wavefield of statistically isotropic polycrystals into its constituent modes and reveal it to be multimodal in the geometric regime. The single effective elastic wave of the classical description holds through the Rayleigh and stochastic regimes, which cover most practical measure-ments, and it is in the geometric regime, reached here for the first time, that the remaining modes appear.
