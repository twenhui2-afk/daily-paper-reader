# SparseTokenGS: Gaussian-based Sparse-View CBCT Reconstruction via Reusable Token Pool 

Zihan Deng, Zhenzhen Gong, Yujie Liu, Jiaxin Gong, Dingkun Liu, Kexian Xie, Yixing Huang, Peng Jin, Tianye Niu, Zhisheng Wang, Member, IEEE ,Fenglin Liu, Member, IEEE , Hongming Shan, Senior Member, IEEE , and Lequan Yu, Member, IEEE 

Abstract — Sparse-view cone-beam computed tomogra-phy is widely used for fast, low-dose volumetric imaging and lesion localization in dentistry and image-guided in-terventions, but it introduces severe artifacts. Feed-forward Gaussian methods transfer the reconstruction cost to train-ing and decode each coordinate from projection-aligned features and neighboring anchors. However, information from the whole scan is written only when the field is constructed and cannot be retrieved during coordinate de-coding, leaving the volume inconsistent within the scan. This is a common limitation of such methods: once the field is fixed, decoding uses only local features and recon-struction quality drops substantially. We propose Sparse-TokenGS, a Gaussian-based method for sparse-view cone-beam computed tomography reconstruction via a reusable token pool. Anchor Token Fusion and Point-Token Atten-tion query this shared pool at the anchors and at the query coordinates, respectively. The local Gaussian prior is retained, and the volume is reconstructed in one for-ward pass, without per-scan optimization or a projection-domain loss. On three public chest and dental datasets, SparseTokenGS achieves the highest reconstruction per-formance among the compared methods. On clinical dental scans, reconstruction from only six projections reaches 29.51 decibels in peak signal-to-noise ratio and a structural similarity of 0.842 against the full-view reference, and off-the-shelf tooth-and-pulp segmentation keeps these struc-tures separable for root-canal localization. Experiments on volcanic rock and chicken show performance after same-object adaptation. A reusable token pool gives feed-forward reconstruction a mechanism that keeps scan-wide context

I. Introduction 

# CONE-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides a volume from one gantry rotation for dental imaging, image-guided intervention, and selected head-and-neck exami-nations [1]. When the orbit is densely sampled, reconstruction is typically stable and sharp. Commercial protocols often acquire hundreds to thousands of projections. Sparse angular sampling is nevertheless used to localize lesions quickly, shorten reconstruction time, or reduce radiation burden. The inverse problem is then underdetermined: points in the field of view still reach the detector in every acquired view, but the angular constraints are too few, and direct Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) reconstruction leaves conspicuous streak artifacts [2]. Iterative methods, such as the simultaneous algebraic reconstruction technique (SART) and total-variation (TV) penalized variants, can reduce the data residual without a training set [3], [4]. In three dimensions, however, they are slow, and penalties that suppress streaks also tend to remove low-contrast texture. Deep networks are now widely used to restore corrupted FDK volumes or sparse sinograms and to predict attenuation fields directly [5]–[8]. Gaussian representations and neural fields are attractive for compact local modeling, efficient computation, and strong expressive power [21], [23]. Existing work falls into two groups. One repeatedly optimizes a density field or radiative Gaussian model on the projections of a single scan. Reconstruction quality improves with iteration, yet each patient requires a separate optimization, which is difficult to reconcile with rapid lesion localization or real-time intraoperative guidance [12]–[14], [16], [32]. To amortize reconstruction over training and improve generalization to unseen cases, the other group trains feed-forward models on large cohorts, so that one forward pass maps sparse projections to a volume [10], [11], [15], [17], [18]. These methods encode multi-view evidence into local anchors, voxels, or triplanes and then decode intensity at each coordinate. Once that representation is fixed, coordinate decoding relies mainly on local features and cannot retrieve the appearance of the whole scan, leaving the volume prone to internal inconsistency, residual streaks, and loss of fine boundaries. Learnable tokens that predict Gaussians are a useful precedent, but those tokens emit primitives and are not retained as a conditioning pool at decoding time [25]. These methods have the following property in common: once a feed-forward Gaussian field is formed, the appearance of the whole scan is no longer accessible at the coordinates being decoded. With sparse angular sampling, fine detail and consistency within the volume are then difficult to preserve together. To address this limitation, we propose SparseTokenGS, the first feed-forward Gaussian framework that couples the context written into the anchors with the evidence retrieved at the query coordinates through one reusable token pool. A shared encoder writes a fixed-length, pose-free pool for the current scan, and the pool is read twice because the anchors and the queries need that same evidence at different stages of decoding. Anchor Token Fusion adds a zero-initialized residual to the anchor features, with geometry taken from local projection features alone, and Point-Token Attention reads the same pool at each query together with the view and neighboring Gaussian features. Volumetric supervision alone, without a projection loss or per-scan optimization, lets one forward pass return the volume. We assess this design on simulated chest and dental cohorts and on held-out clinical dental scans. There, an off-the-shelf tooth and pulp segmenter still separates these structures, which supports lesion localization for root-canal treatment. Ablations and the volcanic rock and chicken experiments then show how the pool is used and how the model generalizes. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows. 1) We propose SparseTokenGS, the first feed-forward Gaussian framework with dual-level conditioning through one reusable, pose-free scan-level pool. One forward pass returns the volume under volumetric su-pervision alone. 2) We design token-conditioned Gaussian anchors and a hybrid point query. Anchor geometry comes from local projection features, the pool conditions appearance, and each query combines the view, five neighboring anchors, and a coordinate-dependent token feature. 3) We verify the method on extensive simulated and clin-ical data. The studies support rapid lesion localization through tooth and pulp segmentation and show general-ization to other objects. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III specifies the pool and two reads. Sections IV–V report comparisons, ablations, clinical scans, and other objects. Sections VI–VII interpret and con-clude. 

II. Related Works 

A. Learning-based Reconstruction 

In this line of work, learning-based reconstruction replaces a hand-crafted penalty with a data-driven prior, so a volume can be obtained in one forward pass or a few unrolled steps. Image-domain networks such as FBPConvNet correct an FDK volume after it has been formed [5]. Their cost is low, yet the input already contains streaks, and fine structure is easily smoothed away. Methods that use projections and images jointly keep a measurement-consistency constraint. DRONE exchanges information between the two domains [6], and LEARN and learned primal-dual reconstruction learn the successive solver updates [7], [8]. These methods still unroll several steps, so runtime grows with the step count and the learned updates often depend on the training geometry. A sampling-diffusion model covers several sparsity levels with one network, but its sampling remains iterative [9]. Cone-beam acquisition extends the problem from slices to three-dimensional coordinates. DIF-Net, C 2RV, and geometry-aware attenuation learning place projection information at the corresponding coordinates [10], [11], [20]. Once global context is written into this representation, later coordinate queries rely mainly on local features. DeepSparse adds cross-scale pretraining and target-domain finetuning, which still requires large-scale data from many sources [19]. An off-the-shelf tooth and pulp segmenter can separate structures used for root-canal localization only when the input boundaries are already reliable [33]. 

B. Neural Fields, Gaussians, and Tokens 

The methods above still reconstruct on a discrete grid, or write global context only once during field construction. Neural fields and Gaussians represent the volume by a contin-uous function or a small set of primitives and do not store a dense voxel grid, which reduces computation and strengthens local structure. Neural radiance fields represent a scene with a coordinate network [21]. NAF, SAX-NeRF, and NeAT fit an attenuation field to a single scan [12], [13], [22], and DiffNR adds a diffusion prior [32]. Each patient still requires a separate optimization. Radiative Gaussian splatting, R 2-Gaussian, and DGR like-wise optimize these primitives for each scan [14], [16], [24]. Among feed-forward methods, DIF-Gaussian predicts Gaus-sian features from a volumetric loss, with the projection loss used only as optional test-time refinement [15], and X-GRM regresses a Gaussian at each voxel in one Transformer pass [17]. In these designs the appearance of the scan is written into the anchors or voxels when the field is constructed, so later queries can only interpolate the local features. An individual Gaussian describes only its neighborhood, so evidence from the acquired views must first be aggregated. Recent methods compress this evidence into tokens and then build the Gaussian field from them. After X-LRM lifts the tokens into a triplane, coordinate queries no longer access them [18]. Novel view synthesis in computer vision likewise uses learnable tokens to predict 3D Gaussians. TokenGS predicts Gaussian centers from tokens and does not keep them as a conditioning pool for later queries [25]. Once the field is formed, neither the anchors nor the query coordinates can read the original tokens. 

III. Methodology 

After a feed-forward Gaussian field is generated, the ap-pearance of a scan is difficult to recover at a later coordinate query if it has been stored only as dispersed local features. SparseTokenGS therefore introduces a reusable scan-level to-ken pool that preserves the global information until decoding. This section first describes the two reads of the pool, at the Gaussian anchors and at the query coordinates, and then explains the construction of the pool, the parameterization of the anchors, and the training of the hybrid query with volumetric supervision alone. 

A. Dual-Level Token Conditioning 

Given projections {Iv }Vv=1 and their geometry, SparseTo-kenGS predicts a normalized field bρ(x) for x ∈ [0 , 1] 3. Its central operation reuses one scan-specific token matrix T at two levels: 

F ′ = F + W0 Aa(F, T, T ),ztoken (x) = LN( q(x) + Ap(q(x), T, T )) . (1) Here F contains the local Gaussian-anchor features, W0 is a zero-initialized linear map, q embeds the query coordinate, and Aa and Ap are cross-attention blocks with separate parameters. In each block the first argument is the query, and the two copies of T supply the keys and the values. Layer normalization (LN) is applied to the point-token update. The first read updates the anchor features that are later aggregated over spatial neighbors. The second read does not pass through that aggregation. It supplies a contextual feature that depends on the query coordinate. The two reads share the pool T but not their attention parameters. Fig. 2 shows the same flow. In (a), the weight-shared encoder keeps a full-resolution map for view alignment and a downsampled map for the anchors and the pool. Fuse reads the pool at the anchors, and Attend reads it at the query. In (b), each anchor is one row of the attention map and each token is one column. In (c), a coordinate multilayer perceptron (MLP) embeds x, and MLP dec maps the view, Gaussian, and point-token features to one intensity. In the figure, T denotes T , zG denotes zgs , and MLP ρ denotes 

MLP dec .Both reads use the same encoder. The U-Net [26], with weights shared across views, produces two maps for each view. The full-resolution map Fv ∈ R128 ×H×W is used for view alignment, and the bottleneck map Bv ∈ R1024 ×H/ 16 ×W/ 16 is used for the token pool and the anchors. Let πv (x) denote the detector coordinate of x under the known geometry. Bilinear sampling at πv aligns each map with a three-dimensional position. The prediction target is normalized image intensity, and linear attenuation is not calibrated.

IV. Experimental Setting 

A. Simulated Experiments and Datasets 

We conducted quantitative evaluation on the chest computed tomography (CT) dataset LUNA16 [28] and the dental CBCT datasets ToothFairy [31] and ToothFairy4 [30]. LUNA25 [29] is shown with these cohorts in Fig. 8. Each quantitative dataset is split into training, validation, and test scans at a ratio of about 8:1:1 . Before projection, chest intensities are clipped to [−1000 , 1000] Hounsfield units (HU) and dental intensities to [−1000 , 3000] HU. The volumes are then normalized to 

[0 , 1] and resampled to 256 3. Cone-beam projections are simulated from these normalized volumes for chest and dental anatomy. Display windows follow the anatomy: chest slices use [−1000 , 1000] HU and dental slices use [−800 , 1200] HU. For each quantitative dataset, we generate 50 projections along a fixed 180 ◦ circular arc that starts at 0◦. The chest geometry uses source-to-isocenter and source-to-detector dis-tances of 800 mm and 1200 mm, a 256 2 detector, and 2.4

mm pixels. The dental geometry uses source-to-isocenter and source-to-detector distances of 900 mm and 1000 mm, with detector spacings of about 0.209 mm and 0.543 mm. Every angular subset is taken from this same arc. We trained Sparse-TokenGS models at 3, 6, 10, 14, and 18 views. During training, projection indices are drawn at approximately uniform spacing with a random starting offset. At test time, the indices are fixed and the offset is zero. 

B. Baselines and Evaluation Protocol 

In the quantitative comparison, we include FDK, SART, FBPConvNet, DIF-Net, feed-forward DIF-Gaussian without test-time refinement, and X-GRM [2], [3], [5], [10], [15], [17]. Fig. 8 additionally shows the per-scan methods DGR, DiffNR, and R 2-Gaussian [14], [16], [32]. The learned feed-forward baselines are trained separately for each dataset and each view count. SART runs for 20 iterations with the simulator projector. FBPConvNet is a residual U-Net trained for eight epochs on axial FDK slices from 80 scans. We compute the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), SSIM, and root-mean-square error (RMSE) with a data range of 1. The evaluation SSIM uses a 73 uniform window on the reconstructed volume, which is separate from the sample-order term used in training. 

C. Clinical Data and Other Objects 

We also evaluate the method on clinical dental CBCT and on volcanic rock and chicken. The retrospective dental scans use a LargeV SMART3D system at 100 kV and 6 mA. The nominal source-to-isocenter and source-to-detector distances are 420 

mm and 640 mm. The detector has 1212 × 1392 pixels at 0.1

mm spacing, and the scanner metadata lists 676 projections per rotation. The archive stores projection indices. The full-view reconstructions from the scanner, originally 488 × 488 ×

332 voxels at 0.25 mm, are resampled to 256 3. The resulting spacing is about 0.477 mm in-plane and 0.324 mm along the longitudinal axis, and we have obtained the relevant ethical approval for these retrospective scans. Volcanic rock and chicken are included to examine general-ization to objects outside the chest and dental anatomy. Both acquisitions use a source-to-detector distance of 800 mm and a detector element size of 0.139 mm. The volcanic rock scan has 

1440 projections and 2531 detector columns, and the chicken scan has 1441 projections and 2568 detector columns. The reconstruction reports an isocenter voxel size of 0.062 mm. The exported ROI reference volumes use isotropic sampling of 

15 .6375 μm for the rock and 19 .1125 μm for the chicken. This experiment uses eighteen views. Adaptation uses augmented copies and reference volumes of the same two specimens. 

D. Implementation and Computational Cost 

We implement SparseTokenGS and the learned comparison methods in PyTorch. We perform training, validation, and testing on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8558 (4.00 GHz) cen-tral processing unit (CPU) and eight NVIDIA H100 graph-ics processing units (GPUs). In the simulated experiments, SparseTokenGS and the feed-forward baselines DIF-Net, DIF-Gaussian, and X-GRM are each trained for 600 epochs, with one checkpoint for every dataset and every view count. SparseTokenGS uses a batch size of 2 and stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum 0.98 . Its learning rate starts at 0.01 and decays to 5 × 10 −4 by the last epoch, with weight decay 3 × 10 −4. FBPConvNet is trained for eight epochs. The per-scan methods are optimized case by case. In the six-view experiments, DGR, DiffNR, and R 2-Gaussian use 300 , 12000 ,and 30000 steps, respectively. The network uses 512 tokens, three attention blocks, four heads, at most 2048 memory rows, a 14 3 anchor lattice, and five neighbors at each query. VI. Discussion 

A. What Dual-Level Conditioning Adds 

Under sparse views one scan supplies few measurements, and if appearance is stored only in local anchors, a later coordinate query sees it after neighborhood interpolation, so structure supported by only a few views fades in the average. SparseTokenGS compresses the current projections into afixed-length scan-level token pool and keeps it until decoding. The anchor read updates the local Gaussian features from the pool, and a Gaussian kernel then aggregates five neighboring anchors, so nearby positions share the same scan evidence and smoothly varying structures such as the chest wall, the lung boundary, and the dental arch form a continuous whole. The point read skips this aggregation and attends directly to the same pool, so a query can emphasize a token that the five-neighbor average has already discounted. Zero initialization lets the Gaussian branch follow the existing local estimate while the point branch reads the pool, so scan-level context enters in one forward pass. Removing the point-level read lowers LUNA16 by about 6 dB in the ablation table, the largest drop among the rows. That drop shows the two reads are not substitutes: after aggregation, only the point read still sees the pool. Keeping both reads lets a smooth outline and a fine boundary be recovered together from the same sparse scan. 

B. Interpreting the Reconstruction Evidence 

In Table II, SparseTokenGS attains the highest PSNR and SSIM among the compared entries on every dataset and at every view count. As more views are added, the encoder and the token pool receive fuller measurements, and the local features and the scan-level memory become richer together, so the scores rise accordingly. In Fig. 4 the chest-wall and lung boundaries move closer to the reference, and Fig. 8 preserves the broad anatomical outline on both the chest and dental cohorts. Once the feed-forward baselines write their anchors or voxels, that scan-level context stays fixed, so the added views cannot be read again at the query coordinate. Each view count uses its own checkpoint, while the pool length stays fixed. Additional views therefore enrich the same memory, and the advantage over the feed-forward baselines remains from three to eighteen views. The decoder and the anchor grid stay the same, so the gain comes from richer tokens in the same field. The ablations show that these stages contribute together. The full checkpoint stands above the variants that remove anchor fusion, the token pool, or the similarity term. The largest gap appears when only the anchor read is kept: both LUNA16 and ToothFairy fall well below the main reference in Table III. Neighborhood aggregation at the anchors forms the smoothly varying structure, and the point-level read supplies the scan-level evidence that this average does not deliver, so removing either one closes a distinct information path. Removing anchor fusion leaves the point-level read in place, and the score then falls by less than when the point-level read itself is removed. The similarity term holds neighboring samples together, so removing it costs more than removing only the anchor residual. In Fig. 6(b) the 14 3 grid has the highest validation score: too few anchors leave the field insufficiently covered, while too many anchors make each anchor harder to fit, so 14 3 is the operating point between coverage and learnability. 

C. Clinical Interpretation and Other Objects 

On the clinical dental scans, neighborhood aggregation at the anchors joins the dental arch and the mandible into a continuous outline, and the point-level read keeps the tooth and pulp boundary from depending only on that average. In Fig. 9, the six-view reconstruction stays consistent with the scanner’s full-view reconstruction along these outlines. The larger ab-solute error still follows the tooth and cortical boundaries, where few angles leave those edges underdetermined, and the continuous arch remains available to the segmenter. In Fig. 10, TIPs separates tooth and pulp without retraining. The same volumes reach 29 .51 dB against the scanner reconstruction, and the pulp Dice on the three displayed cases stays above 

50% , so intensity agreement and segmentation jointly confirm the preservation of tooth and pulp. The pool is rewritten from the current scan, while the encoder and the attention weights come from the training distribution. This same split between a smooth outline and a fine boundary also explains the results on other objects. On rock and chicken, a short same-object adaptation updates those weights, and the pool then organizes appearance according to 10 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON MEDICAL IMAGING 

the new object’s reference. The advantage on both specimens in Table IV therefore comes from reusing this conditioning after the weights have seen the new intensity and geometry, with no redesign of the architecture. Feed-forward baselines lack a scan-level memory that remains readable at decoding, so once their local features are fixed they are hard to adjust. 

VII. Conclusion 

In this work, we propose SparseTokenGS, a Gaussian-based method for sparse-view cone-beam computed tomography reconstruction via a reusable token pool. A pose-free pool is read at the anchors and at each query in one forward pass. This token-pool idea prepares a route for a family of feed-forward methods that retain a scan-level memory until coordinate decoding. Extensive experiments verify that it outperforms the compared feed-forward methods in image quality from three to eighteen views on three public datasets. Six-view clinical reconstruction supports tooth and pulp separation for lesion localization. In the future, we will develop it for rapid clinical lesion localization.
