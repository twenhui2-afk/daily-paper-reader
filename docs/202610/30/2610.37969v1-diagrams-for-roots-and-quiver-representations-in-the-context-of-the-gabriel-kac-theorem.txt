# Diagrams for Roots and Quiver Representations in the context of the Gabriel-Kac Theorem 

Jacob Canel Department of Mathematics, Pennsylvania State University, jmc8684@psu.edu October 30, 2026 

Abstract 

Using the data of the Dynkin diagrams, we build graphical calculi for representing the positive roots of Kac-Moody algebras and the rep-resentations of quivers. Once developed, we use these to analyze the asymptotic size of the root and representation spaces, and discuss how this relates to the Gabriel-Kac theorem, the correspondence between these two structures. We give a refinement of this correspondence in terms of the trichotomy of growth rates for Kac-Moody algebras de-pending on if they are of finite, affine, or indefinite type, which is done in terms of what we call “tree like” representations.

# 1 Introduction 

There is an intimate relationship between the Kac-Moody algebra defined on a simply-laced graph and the representation theory of any quiver produced by picking an orientation on that graph. In particular, Gabriel [9] showed that roots of an ADE Lie algebra correspond 1 − 1 with representations of such a quiver, and Kac [13] showed that existence of a root corresponded 2to existence of an indecomposible quiver representation in the case of ar-bitrary Kac-Moody algebras. Nakajima developed this line of reasoning further by using homological constructions on his quiver varieties to pro-duce representations of the Kac-Moody algebra. In the same paper where he demonstrated his part of the correspondence between roots and repre-sentations (which we will call the Gabriel-Kac theorem), Kac conjectured that the constant part of the polynomial count of absolutely indecomposable quiver representations was the multiplicity of the associated root, which was eventually proven by Hausel [11] using Nakajima’s quiver varieties. We add to this discussion by presenting a graphical calculi suitable to representing both roots and quiver representations, which differ minimally, and use them to illustrate this relationship more intuitively. We introduce a language for generating Kac-Moody roots and use it to give a simple proof of the growth-rate trichotomy for Kac-Moody algebras [12]. This language is defined entirely in terms of the Dynkin diagrams, and reflect their com-binatorial structure. We further introduce a similar language for generating quiver representations (over C), and discuss the families of representations thus generated which naturally correspond to the roots, as in Kac’s con-jectures. These languages are given solely from the data of the Dynkin diagrams, which is the conceptual contribution of this work. In §2, we build the visual calculi for both sets of objects in the corre-spondence. In §3, §4, and §5, we discuss the diagrammatics and asymptotic behavior of simple, affine, and wild-type Kac-Moody algebras respectively. In §6, §7, and §5, we discuss the diagrams and asymptotic behavior of quiver representations for the finite, affine, and wild-type quivers respectively. Fi-nally, we make some concluding remarks in §9. 

# 2 Defining the Graphical Calculi 

2.1 Building the Graphical Calculus for Kac-Moody Roots 

For background on constructing Lie algebras from Dynkin diagrams, please consult the appendix A. Every element of E (the positive part of the Lie algebra) can be written as an ad-polynomial, meaning a sum of terms like [ein , [ein−1 , [ein−2 , [..., e i1 ]... ]]]]. We can simplify this by just writing their subscripts in sequence (in reverse order i1, i 2...i n) or by representing them using Dynkin diagram vertices, which is what we will do. If two generators have commuting labels, we can just as well write them one on top of the other, since we can move one past the other freely. If two generators in adjacent positions don’t commute, there is at least one edge between them 3in the Dynkin graph. An edge should be drawn between them in our diagram to indicate this. We will therefore write elements of the Lie algebra as graphs sorted into levels. Since ad-polynomials have some initial vector upon which repeated ad-actions are taken, there is only one vertex in the initial level (which we write to the left). We can also move every vertex in the graph towards the left until it reaches something with which it does not commute, so we can assert that every vertex in every other level must have a line to something in the previous level. We will call the a-valence of a set of vertices the sum of the number of directed edges that go from those vertices (counted with multiplicity) to the vertex a in the Dynkin diagram and counting edges labeled ( x, y ) with the x towards the a as a directed edges moving towards a. If the number of a-vertices in level n + 1 exceeds the a-valence of level n, it is possible to move at least one a vertex to level n − 1, so we will insist on disallowing such arrangements. These rules do not provide a complete description of a basis for E, as not all relations are applied when they could be, but it does reduce the search space greatly, especially in the semisimple and near semisimple cases. The sections 3, 4, and 5 are devoted to following a method which proceeds thusly: 1. Iteratively create new candidate elements of E using the results above 2. Apply the relations to reduce the number of candidate elements 3. Describe the resulting long-time behavior The resulting diagrams, when viewed as a set of instructions for how to build the corresponding element of the Kac-Moody algebra, provide half of the story about the roots. Namely, they provide an exhaustive list of candidates for different root vectors. This only gives an upper bound for the root space dimension, and evaluating the list of candidates using: 1. the Serre relations which assert that specific generators commute, which are entirely manifest in this description, 2. the other Serre relations, which are partially manifest in this descrip-tion, 3. the Jacobi identity, which is not manifest in this description, 44. and anticommutativity of the Lie bracket, which here shows up as the fact ad x1 ad x2 ...ad b(a) = −ad x1 ad x2 ...ad a(b), which leads to a simple combinatorial move in this description. That a particular root space has non-zero multiplicity can be validated us-ing the Gabriel-Kac theorem. Indeed, this graphical calculus lends itself naturally to generating the quiver representations.

# 4 Affine Lie Algebras: Repeating Diagrams for Imaginary Roots 

The second part of the trichotomy are the “Affine Lie Algebras,” with posi-tive semidefinite Cartan Matrices, The first example is the ˜An, which should be read as “affine An”. In this and the third part of the trichotomy, there appear “imaginary roots,” which have nonpositive norm according to the bilinear form DA . Real roots, which is to say those with positive norm, have corresponding root spaces of dimension one. Hence it makes sense to discuss “the” root vector corresponding to a root, or at least a choice of basis subordinate to the root spaces obviates a need to make an additional choice of representative. The root spaces of imaginary roots, on the other hand, have dimension (multiplicity) which is greater than one. Furthermore, the 10 linear dependencies among different constructions of root vectors do not lend themselves to a canonical choice of basis for each root space. The long-time behavior of root multiplicities along imaginary directions does depend on if the norm is negative or zero, see for example [8]. For now we specialize to the Affine Lie algebras, for which DA is of positive semidefinite type. These have polynomial growth rate in the number of generators used. The easiest way to produce a semipositive definite generalized Cartan matrix is just to close the path of an An to a loop. This includes, formally, as a subclass the loop on two elements, i.e. the graph ˜A1, which is a pair of vertices connected by two undirected lines. 

Figure 10: The Affine A Algebras In these cases we see the two primary features particular to Affine al-gebras on clear display. The imaginary roots have presentations which are patterns that repeat some number of times, and may be extended indefi-nitely. We also see, for the first time, that we will not be able to deform some patterns with the same number of each generator in to some others. For example, if we take the algebra ˜A2, we could take the element given by the pattern: 1 2 3which would be independent with either 2 3 1or with 1 3 2and the latter two would be independent of one another. However, there would be a linear dependence between all three, which is given by the Jacobi 11 identity. This gives a root space multiplicity of two for the root given by one of each generator. It is a general phenomenon with Affine algebras (given by connected graphs) that the imaginary positive root spaces all have the same dimension. This is manifest in the construction by loop algebras in chapter seven of Kac’s book [12]. Aside from graphs containing loops, there are a few ways to create infinitely extensible patterns. We now present the Dynkin diagrams and their associated infinitely extensible patterns. 

4.1 Two Special Features 

In the finite case, the presence of what we called a “special feature,” namely a double edge or a vertex of degree three, allowed us to bounce back and repeat our steps of adding vertices in reverse. The imaginary roots of these algebras are given by iterations of a repeating pattern using this bounce. To remain affine, the graphs with two special features must have them at each end of the path, and the features must be no more complicated than either of these options. If we order the vertices in the path, there are nine ways to create graphs with the features we want, but three are redundant when the orders are reversed. This leaves six families. The first three of these are untwisted, meaning that they can be obtained directly from simple algebras via central extensions of the loop algebras, but the remaining three require a more complicated construction involving twisting the loop algebra by automorphisms of the Dynkin graphs. # 6 Quiver Representations for Finite Type Roots 

6.1 An Quivers 

The simplest case, as always, is the An case. Indecomposable representations correspond to intervals of 1 , 2, 3, ..., n with the usual order. Given such a sequence i, i + 1 , ..., i + j and an orientation of each edge, set Vm = C with basis element em, and set f{m,m +1 }em = em+1 or f{m,m +1 }em+1 = em as appropriate to the choice of orientation. Such a representation is clearly indecomposable, as any nontrivial decomposition would have to put some 

em and its image in unconnected subspaces. This corresponds precisely to the positive roots we find for the An case, which can all be represented in our scheme by the subpaths of An.

6.2 Dn Quivers 

The indecomposable representations of the Dn quiver correspond to specific fragments of the maximal root diagram for Dn. Namely, any connected fragment of the Dn diagram itself, using coefficients of one as before, but if 18 more than one vertex n − 2 is present, both the n − 1 and n vertices must be as well (and the maps from each to the vector space generated by the 

n − 2 vertices must be linearly independent as maps from C to that space), furthermore one ‘tail,’ i.e. the connected component of the subgraph on 1, ..., n − 2 for each instance of n − 2, must be longer than the other. This requirement is manifest in the roots in that we require the initial segment to be longer than the final for the root to be nonzero.

# 8 Binary Coding in the Quiver Setting 

There are two ways in which the binary coding argument replicates itself in the setting of quiver representations. The first is by counting dimensions of representation spaces, and the second by constructing families of tree-like representations. The proposition 5.2 is true when restricted to the simply laced diagrams, so we will restrict our attention to the least complicated simply laced diagrams extending those. Our attention will therefore be on those which extend affine ADE diagrams by a single vertex connected by a single edge. For the sake of convenience, we will assume that this edge is oriented out from the new vertex. More classical candidate notions might be hyperbolicity (the diagram containing no proper induced subgraphs which are not finite or affine type) or Minkowskian signature of the (diagonalized) Cartan matrix, but these are less informative areas of extension as our tools are based on the combinatorial order ≤c, which reduces to the subgraph relation in the case of simply laced diagrams. If x is connected to a vertex in the affine diagram which is associated with a generator j, the number of arrows n from x to J in the modified Jordan quiver will be equal to the multiplicity of the generator j in the smallest imaginary positive root in that direction. This will also be the number of Jordan blocks into which the associated vector space may be divvied, while the size of those Jordan blocks will be the integer scaling from this smaller imaginary positive root to the larger one in question. The representation space will therefore be determined by three numbers, n the number of times the generator appears in the smallest imaginary positive root, k the size of the Jordan block, which is also the aforementioned scaling, and r the rank of the vector space x. We may assume that r ≤ nk , as otherwise the map from the vector space associ-ated with x to that associated with j would have a nontrivial kernel, which could be amputated from x in a direct sum with no loss of information. Thus, the representation space is equivalent to the set of maps from a space of dimension r to a space of dimension nk . More interesting (and the reason why we write it as a modified Jordan quiver) is that once we have fixed that all the vector spaces from the original affine quiver are in the form discussed in the previous discussion, the remaining freedom we have in choosing a basis for the new quiver is given by acting with any invertible r × r matrix on the right and a matrix which acts on subspaces corresponding to Jordan blocks as k × k upper triangular Toeplitz matrices on the left. By this ob-servation, it is manifest that any such representation is described by a set of n distinct k × r matrices, up to the action of r × r invertibles on the right and k × k upper triangular Toeplitz matrices on the left. Assuming that the 

r × k matrices chosen collectively form a generic nk × r matrix before that equivalence is made, the first r-columns of it may be made into the r × r

identity matrix by fixing the r × r matrix freedom. A choice of action by upper triangular Toeplitz matrices can be made to eliminate at most k − 1remaining degrees of freedom via row operations. Thus the dimension of the quotient space is given by max( rkn − r2 − k − 1, 0), which is linear in each variable (keeping in mind the upper bound on r), which is commensurate with an exponential number of options (in this analogy the “base” of the exponentiation would be C). If we examine the tree like representations, the representation restricted to the affine part of the quiver must also be tree-like (although it need not be connected, but this imposes additional conditions on how the x-vertices are connected in the graphical notation). In the graphical calculus, tree-likeness requires that each x-vertex not be attached twice to linked Jordan 35 block subspaces. If we assume that the affine representation is indecomposi-ble, then the number of possible distinct trees is the number of j-vertices (nk ) choose the number of x-vertices r. Meaning, the number of total trees with at most nk x -vertices is 2 nk , which gives the aforementioned expo-nential growth again, and recovers the graphical representations from the Kac-Moody algebra case if the underlying affine algebra is A or D type. If it is one of the E-types, then it becomes a question of whether each appro-priate vertex in each iterate of the repeatable quiver representation pattern is marked by adjoining an x or not. In the case where the Affine quiver representations are not indecomposi-ble themselves, the number of tree-like representations is equivalent to the number of solutions to the following problem: 

Problem 1. For each connected Jordan block subspace, create a vertex la-belled a. For each for each j vertex corresponding to a basis element of a Jordan block subspace, number those vertices ji for i ranging between one and the total number, and connect it to the corresponding a vertex. Finally, for each integer between 1 and r, create a vertex labeled c. How many distinct trees can be made by connecting j and c vertices? 

While a closed form solution to this problem is not known to the author, the exponential estimate given by assuming the underlying affine represen-tations are indecomposible does still show an exponential growth rate. This allows us to state the following theorem: 

Theorem 8.1. Let G be a simply laced graph. A quiver Q constructed by orienting it will have a number of sub-generic tree-like representations which is 1. Finite, if the Kac-Moody algebra associated to the graph is simple (in this case a 1 − 1 correspondence holds with positive roots) 2. Infinite but grows as a polynomial function in the dimension vector, in the case that the Kac-Moody algebra associated to the graph is affine 3. Infinite and grows exponentially, if the Kac-Moody algebra associated to the graph is of indefinite type. 

36 9 Conclusion 

In this paper we have generated diagrams following two procedures: 

• For roots of Kac-Moody algebras: we add successive applications of simple roots until the process terminates or repeats. 

• For quiver representations: we assume generic sizes of all definable subspaces and work from there to pick a basis which is maximally rigid, ideally then finding the generic indecomposable representation and from there relaxing the constraint to pick out the tree like repre-sentations. The data here illustrate the Gabriel-Kac theorem, and indeed the root space multiplicity part of Kac’s conjectures, by putting them in a common com-binatorial language. Indeed, in the case of quiver representations at indivisible imaginary roots, which is the case for which Crawley-Bovey’s argument using deformed preprojective algebras [7] works, the combinatorial approach we illustrate gives an easy illustration for how to obtain a representation for multiples of that root: 1. Start by finding the generic indecomposable for an indivisible imagi-nary root. It will have at least one loop in its minimal loop represen-tation 2. For each loop, concentrate all parameters on a single edge by scaling basis elements 3. Pick one edge which still has a parameter. 4. Replace all vertices with vector spaces Cn

5. Replace all other edges with matrices Id 

6. Replace the selected edge with a Jordan block Jλ

the resultant representation is indecomposable and has the desired dimen-sions. If a representation has n-loops with parameters which may each be freely chosen, which we might think of as being in analogy with independent loops of π1 of a graph, or independent features in the 1-homology groups, that 37 representation is naturally a member of an n-dimensional family of repre-sentations. In particular, if those representations are all absolutely indecom-posable, then they contribute a term of ( q − 1) n to the count of absolutely indecomposable representations over Fq, albeit with some redundancy be-tween such families, and where setting any of those parameters to zero is taken to relegate it to a smaller family. In particular, in this interpretation it is entirely clear that the tree-like representations are those which contribute to the constant term, and thus correspond to the root multiplicity. It is clear from the accounting done in the previous six sections that the tree-like representations obey the same asymptotic growth rate trichotomy as the root multiplicities, so may be an approximate algebraic model for them. 

9.1 Future Goals 

The combinatorial structures presented here illustrate a part of the the-ory which, while deep, is relatively primitive compared to more modern developments, including the constructions of the representation theory of Kac-Moody algebras using Nakajima’s quiver varieties [15][11], and con-structions of enveloping algebras using Hall algebras of quiver representation categories [4]. To the author’s knowledge, neither these more advanced re-sults nor Kac’s conjectures have been approached combinatorially using just the Dynkin diagrams before, and it would be of interest to see what comes of that line of inquiry. In both the Kac-Moody algebra case and the quiver representation case, we have managed to produce a binary coding argument, but have not produced a computationally tractable combinatorial model for either. The long-term goal of this project is to produce such a model, and pursuing these other lines of inquiry may help in doing so.

• Ad kei (x) = 0 

• Ad jx(ei) = 0 and that the Lie algebra is freely generated besides these constraints, for the sum s = e1 + e2 + ... + en, we have

# B Facts about the Affine Algebras 

The Affine Kac-Moody algebras are a restricted subclass of Kac-Moody algebras which are “one step beyond” the simple Lie algebras. They are the most well studied class of infinite dimensional Kac-Moody algebras and the most useful in applications, to the extent that in the physics literature they are sometimes simply referred to as “the Kac-Moody algebras.” 

Fact B.1. Given a Kac-Moody algebra g, the following are equivalent: 

• g is an affine algebra 

• The Cartan matrix A of g is symmetrizable and DA is positive semidef-inite. 

• g is infinite dimensional and has polynomial growth 

• Each irreducible direct summand of g can be constructed as a central extension of a twisted loop algebra over a simple Lie algebra (a process appropriately called “affinization”) 

• g arises as the current algebra in a Wess-Zumino-Witten model 

The first four points can be found in Kac’s book [12], and for the fifth one (which is basically the fourth one, but highlights the relevance of these algebras in physics) should probably consult Di Francesco et al. [17]. Given a simple Lie algebra a, the untwisted loop algebra is a ⊗ C(t). Twisting here means that rather than sections of S1 × a × S1, the sections are of the bundle given by ([0 , 2π] × a/ ∼=ϕ) → S1, where ∼=ϕ is the equivalence relation identifying (0 , a ) ∼=ϕ (1 , ϕ (a)) for ϕ an automorphism of a. This immediately gives both infinite dimension-ality and polynomial growth. Showing that this implies positive semidefi-niteness is an exercise in finding a system of simple roots for this algebra.
