Abstract 

Decomposable graphical models are usually adopted for their computational convenience rather than derived from a data-generating mechanism. We show that a special class of them arises naturally from the irreversible accumulation of events along a branching history. Each event is attached to an edge of a latent rooted tree and is inherited by everything below that edge; an observation records which events have occurred at a randomly chosen node, not necessarily a leaf. When the tree is a path, this is the cumulative coding of an ordinal variable, whose indicators form a Markov chain; in the general tree case the Markov chain is replaced by a junction tree. The conditional independence graph of the event indicators is always decom-posable and, in fact, is the line graph of the tree (maximal cliques correspond to nodes where two or more edges meet and separators are the single events on the edges joining two such nodes). The graph determines the tree up to the choice of root. A connected graph arises in this way if, and only if, it is chordal, any two of its maximal cliques share at most one vertex, no vertex is in three maximal cliques, and the graph is such that any two maximal cliques share at most one vertex. This provides a simple graphical test for compatibility with the mechanism (necessary but not sufficient, as irreversibility also constrains the distribution’s support). The structure breaks down if we look at just the leaves, and measurement error destroys the exact screening and leads to a denser graph rather than simply a weaker dependence. We discuss inference, provide diagnostics for departures such as recurrent events, illustrate them on some simulated data and discuss their application in the context of mutation accumulation and lineage tracing where both ancestral and descendant types are sampled together. 

Keywords: decomposable graphical model; junction tree; event accumulation; irre-versibility; conditional independence; perfect phylogeny; cancer progression; lineage trac-ing.

21 Introduction 

Multivariate binary data generated by the accumulation of events along historical trajec-tories arise in a wide range of scientific areas. Classic examples include the presence or absence of mutations in molecular evolution, somatic mutations in tumour progression, and the transmission of cultural or linguistic traits. In such systems, events typically occur irreversibly and are inherited by everything that descends from the point at which they occurred, producing strong and highly structured dependence patterns among the collected variables. A common approach to modelling dependence in multivariate binary data is through undirected graphical models, and within this system decomposable (or chordal) graph-ical models play a central role. Their importance stems largely from computational factors: likelihoods factorise according to cliques and separators, statistical inference becomes tractable, and model-selection procedures can be implemented efficiently (Lau-ritzen, 1996; Edwards, 2000; Abreu et al., 2010). For these reasons decomposable models are often adopted as convenient approximations or as structural restrictions. However, considerably less attention has been paid to the question of when decomposability should arise naturally from an underlying data-generating mechanism. The present work considers this question in the context of irreversible event accumu-lation along tree-structured trajectories. We consider a general probabilistic model in which each event may occur at most once on a latent rooted tree and, once realised, is retained by every trajectory that continues past it. Each observation is a single lineage ,sampled at an arbitrary stage of its progression, and records the presence or absence of the accumulated events. This abstract formulation incorporates essential features of a broad class of cumulative historical processes while remaining independent of any given application domain. 

The model in standard statistical terms. Because the vocabulary of the appli-cations may be unfamiliar, we state at once what the model is when the vocabulary is removed. A lineage is a directed path in the tree beginning at the root (i.e., the history of one unit) and, since such a path is determined by its endpoint, a lineage is identified with its endpoint node . The model therefore draws a single node Z of a tree and reports, for each of the p arcs, whether that arc lies on the root-to-Z path. Equivalently, it reports the indicator vector of a laminar (nested) family of events, of which the tree is nothing but the Hasse diagram. When the tree is a path, this is exactly the cumulative (“thermometer”) coding of an ordinal variable, and the main theorem reduces to the fa-miliar fact that cumulative indicators form a Markov chain; a general tree replaces the 

ordinal variable by a hierarchical one and the Markov chain by a junction tree. The observation scheme is likewise standard: each unit is inspected once, at a random point in its progression, which is cross-sectional (current-status) sampling of an irreversible progressive process, here with a branching rather than a linear state space. Section 2.2 makes these statements precise; no knowledge of the applications is required at any point before Section 9. Our main result (Theorem 3.1) characterises the conditional independence structure induced by this model. The Markov graph of the observed variables is necessarily de-composable and exhibits a highly constrained clique structure: maximal cliques intersect in at most one variable, and each variable belongs to at most two maximal cliques. In particular, the graph admits a junction-tree representation with singleton separators. 3Decomposability is therefore not simply a convenient modelling assumption in this set-ting, but a structural consequence of irreversible event accumulation on a tree. Beyond the structural theorem, these results establish a direct correspondence be-tween the topology of the event tree and the structure of the induced graphical model. Maximal cliques correspond to the nodes of the event tree at which two or more edges meet, while the arc-events joining two such nodes act as separator variables linking ad-jacent clique blocks. Thus, the resulting junction tree reproduces the event tree with its nodes of degree one removed; when every internal node branches, this is exactly the branching skeleton of the accumulation process. We complement this with a converse 

(Theorem 7.3): the three structural properties of Theorem 3.1 are not only necessary but also sufficient. They therefore characterise exactly the class of graphs realisable by accumulation trees, and turn these conditions into a decidable criterion of graphical compatibility. 

A point of modelling emphasis. It is tempting to model each observation as a 

complete lineage, that is, a root-to-leaf path. We show in Section 2 that this apparently natural choice is in fact degenerate : complete lineages satisfy exact flow constraints which collapse the induced graph and destroy the clique structure described above. Proposi-tion 2.5 makes the collapse explicit: under leaf-only sampling, the Markov graph reduces to a disjoint union of cliques formed by sibling arcs pointing at leaves, and every other variable becomes an isolated vertex. Allowing each lineage to be observed at an arbitrary stage of progression, so that intermediate states carry positive probability, removes the degeneracy and yields the structure we describe. It is also the more faithful description of applications such as mitochondrial lineage tracing, in which the sample may contain lin-eages caught at intermediate stages, not only at their endpoints (Amorim, 2019; Ludwig et al., 2019). See Section 9. These results suggest a theoretical explanation for the empirical success of tree-based and decomposable graphical models in systems driven by cumulative events. They also suggest natural statistical diagnostics: deviations from the structural properties derived here may indicate mechanisms such as recombination, recurrent events, mixtures of pop-ulations, or observation noise (Didelot and Maiden, 2010). 

Related work. The model sits at the confluence of a number of established frame-works, and stating the relationship sharpens the contribution. When each event occurs at most once and is inherited by all descendants, the units-by-events matrix is a perfect phylogeny in the sense of the infinite-sites model (Gusfield, 1991): the events are binary characters gained once and never lost, and their compatibility is what forces a tree. The same “accumulate, never lose” assumption underlies a large family of tumour-progression models: oncogenetic trees (Desper et al., 1999; Szabo and Boucher, 2002) restrict accu-mulation to a rooted tree; conjunctive Bayesian networks (Beerenwinkel et al., 2007; Beerenwinkel and Sullivant, 2009; Gerstung et al., 2009) generalise this to a partial order; and mutual hazard networks (Schill et al., 2020) replace hard constraints by pro-moting and inhibiting pairwise rates. Direct cross-sectional inference of such progression models is provided by CAPRESE and CAPRI (Olde Loohuis et al., 2014; Ramazzotti et al., 2015) (the former for tree models, the latter for more general progression DAGs) and the family is surveyed, with software, by Díaz-Uriarte and Herrera-Nieto (2022); a complementary line infers recurrent trajectories and exclusiveness patterns from cohorts 4of already-inferred tumour mutation trees (Luo et al., 2023). These methods are con-cerned chiefly with inferring the order of events and their waiting-time dynamics from cross-sectional data (or from inferred trees). The present work asks the complementary, structural question, given a tree, what conditional-independence graph does it impose on the events?, and answers it with a decomposability theorem and its exact converse (Theorems 3.1 and 7.3). The graphical signature thereby becomes a falsifiable, decidable criterion complementing those estimation methods (Section 10). 

Staged trees and chain event graphs. Event trees also underlie staged trees and chain event graphs (Smith and Anderson, 2008; Collazo et al., 2018), a class of discrete models for processes that unfold asymmetrically. There, the event tree lists the possible developments of a unit, the root-to-leaf paths are the atoms of the sample space, each non-leaf vertex carries a probability distribution over its outgoing edges, and vertices with identical transition probabilities are grouped into stages. Conditional independence, typically of a context-specific kind, is encoded by the stage structure and read off the graph through separation theorems (Smith and Anderson, 2008); distinct staged trees defining the same statistical model have been characterised by Görgen and Smith (2018), and a decomposable subclass, with rational maximum likelihood estimators, has been identified by Alexandr et al. (2024). The present model fits into this framework, but the question it asks is different. Attaching an additional “stop” edge to every non-leaf node of T yields an event tree whose root-to-leaf paths correspond one-to-one to the nodes of T . Under Assumption 2.3, the law of Z is then a probability tree on this augmented tree, with strictly positive transition probabilities and no stage identifications; this accounts for the p free parameters found in Section 8.2. Without the stop edges, the probability tree on T itself describes the leaf-only sampling of Section 2.4. Rather than reading independence from a stage structure, we fix one particular vector of random variables, the p arc indicators, and determine its ordinary undirected Markov graph. Theorem 3.1 shows that this graph depends only on the topology of T and not on the transition probabilities, provided they are positive; in particular, imposing any stage structure on the augmented tree leaves it unchanged. This is special to the accumulation setting: for a general event tree the topology does not by itself determine conditional independence, which is carried by the stage structure. Similarly, where Görgen and Smith (2018) ask which trees define the same model, Proposition 7.1 and Remark 7.2 answer the coarser question of which trees define the same Markov graph: exactly those sharing the same undirected tree with the same assignment of events to edges. Finally, decomposability is not built into the model class here, as it is in Alexandr et al. (2024); it follows from the accumulation mechanism for every positive distribution on the nodes.

Section 7 treats identifiability and proves the realisability converse. Section 8 discusses consequences for inference and structure learning. # 2.1 The event tree 

Let T = ( N , A) be a finite rooted directed tree, where N denotes the set of nodes and 

A ⊆ N × N the set of directed edges, oriented away from the root. Following the usual convention for directed graphs we call the elements of A arcs rather than edges, reserving “edge” for the undirected graphs of Section 3; an arc a = ( u, v ) has tail u and head v,and we say that a leaves u and enters v. The out-degree d(v) of a node is the number of arcs leaving it; every non-root node is entered by exactly one arc, written ain (v). For

A node is a leaf if d(v) = 0 and internal otherwise; it is a branching node if it is the root or has d(v) ≥ 2, that is, if it is a point at which the history either starts or divides. Let {E1, . . . , E p} be the event types. Each event Ei is associated with a unique arc 

ai = ( ui, v i) ∈ A ,

and distinct events are attached to distinct arcs. We take i 7 → ai to be a bijection between 

{1, . . . , p } and A, so that every arc carries exactly one observed event and p = |A| (the 

one-event-per-arc convention; it is what yields |N | = p + 1 in Remark 2.4 and the separation arguments below, in which every intervening arc must carry an indicator). Forgetting the orientation of the arcs gives the underlying undirected tree T u, of which each event indexes an edge. We write Si := S(vi) for the subtree below the head of 

ai. Since the arc ai is the only way into vi, any history that reaches vi has necessarily traversed ai and hence realised Ei; and having realised Ei, it cannot subsequently un-realise it. These two facts are what we mean by persistence and irreversibility : an event, once realised, is retained for the remainder of the history and is inherited by everything below the point at which it occurred. In the model there is no “forgetting” step and no arc that removes an event; formally, irreversibility is the statement Yi = 1[Z ∈ Si] of (1) below, that the indicator of Ei is the indicator of a subtree , rather than of an arbitrary set of nodes. 

# 2.2 Terminology: what a lineage is 

The word lineage is used throughout in exactly one sense, which we now fix. 

Definition 2.1 (Lineage) . A lineage is a directed path in T beginning at the root. It is the history of a single unit: the ordered list of events that unit has undergone, in the order in which it underwent them. A lineage is complete if it ends at a leaf; otherwise it is a history still in progress. Three consequences of Definition 2.1 orient the reader who has no background in the applications. 6(i) A lineage is identified with its endpoint node. A directed path beginning at the root is determined by its endpoint. Specifying a lineage is therefore the same as specifying the node z ∈ N it has reached, and “observing a lineage at an arbitrary stage of its progression” means nothing more than drawing a node. The whole probabilistic content of Section 2.3 is a distribution on N . See Figure 1(a). 

(ii) Y is the indicator vector of a laminar family. The subtrees S1, . . . , S p form a laminar (nested) family (Lemma 2.6), and Yi = 1[Z ∈ Si]. Conversely, T is precisely the Hasse diagram of {N , S 1, . . . , S p} ordered by inclusion. The tree is thus a repre-sentation of how the events nest, not an additional assumption, and the question the paper answers may be stated with no tree at all: what conditional independence structure does the indicator vector of a laminar family of events acquire under a single draw? See Figure 1(c). 

(iii) A lineage is not a class of descendants. Some of the applications use “lineage” loosely for the set of all continuations below a node. We do not: that object is the subtree S(v), and we call it one. A lineage is a single path, and an observation is a single lineage. 

Remark 2.2 (Two familiar special cases) . Both the model and the sampling scheme are standard objects in unfamiliar dress. 

The linear tree is an ordinal variable. Let T be a path z0 → z1 → · · · → zp with ai =(zi−1, z i), and identify Z with its index k ∈ { 0, 1, . . . , p }. Then Yi = 1[Z ∈ Si] = 1[k ≥ i],which is the cumulative, or “thermometer”, coding of an ordinal variable with p+1 levels. Theorem 3.1 specialises to the familiar fact that the cumulative indicators of an ordinal variable form a Markov chain, and G(T ) is the path Y1 − Y2 − · · · − Yp (Remark 6.6). A general tree replaces the ordinal variable by a hierarchical (nested-category) one, and the Markov chain by a junction tree. This is the whole of the generalisation. 

Assumption 2.3 is cross-sectional sampling. Each unit is inspected once, at a random point in its progression, and one records which events have occurred by then. This is 

current-status data on an irreversible progressive process, with a branching rather than a linear state space. The root-to-leaf model of Section 2.4 corresponds instead to inspecting only those units that have run to completion, a design under which the intermediate indicators become deterministic functions of the terminal one (Proposition 2.5); clearly this is a degenerate design.

Definition 2.7 (Markov graph induced by T ). The Markov graph induced by T is the undirected graph G(T ) = ( V, E) with vertex set V = {1, . . . , p }, in which distinct vertices

Remark 2.8 (Structural zeros and the Markov properties) . The law of Y is never strictly positive on {0, 1}p: irreversibility forces Yi = 1 ⇒ Yj = 1 for every j with vj ⪯ ui, so the support is a proper subset of the cube. The classical route from the pairwise to the global Markov property via the intersection property (Lauritzen, 1996, Thm. 3.7; Pearl and Paz, 1987) is therefore unavailable in this model , whatever the observation scheme. The proof in Section 5 accordingly proceeds in the opposite direction: it establishes the clique factorisation directly, and reads off the Markov properties from it. This is the reason for the structure of the argument, and it is worth stating explicitly. 

# 3 Main theorem 

Theorem 3.1 (Structure of the induced Markov graph) . Let T be a finite rooted event tree with p ≥ 2 arcs and one event per arc, and let Y be generated as in (1) under Assumption 2.3. Then the induced Markov graph G(T ) satisfies: 1. G(T ) is chordal (equivalently, decomposable); 

10 2. any two distinct maximal cliques of G(T ) intersect in at most one vertex; 3. every vertex of G(T ) belongs to at most two maximal cliques; 4. G(T ) admits a junction-tree representation in which every separator is a singleton. 

(For p = 1 the graph is a single vertex, its only maximal clique, and the assertions hold trivially.) The proof is presented in Sections 4–5. Its main idea is a single screen-ing identity (Lemma 4.1); the remaining steps are the standard passage from singleton separations to a decomposable factorisation. 

Remark 3.2 (The Markov graph is a line graph) . Because each event indexes an edge of the underlying undirected tree T u, the four clauses admit a compact restatement: 

G(T ) = L( T u),

the line graph of T u. Indeed, two event-variables are adjacent in G(T ) precisely when their arcs share a node of T u, which is exactly the identity G(T ) = H proved in Section 5. Conversely, the connected graphs satisfying clauses (1)–(3) are exactly the line graphs of trees. Indeed, a graph is the line graph of a tree if and only if it is a connected block graph (every block is complete) in which each cut vertex lies in exactly two blocks (Harary, 1963; Chartrand, 1968). Block graphs are precisely the chordal graphs without an induced diamond, K4 minus an edge (Bandelt and Mulder, 1986); clause (2) is equivalent to having no induced diamond; and in a block graph the blocks are the maximal cliques, so clause (3) is the condition on cut vertices. This is why those three properties reappear as the realisability criterion of Theorem 7.3. 

Remark 3.3 (Clause (3) is not implied by clause (2)) . The third clause is genuinely independent of the second, and it does the work in the converse of Section 7. Consider the graph consisting of three triangles glued at a common vertex x (Figure 3(b)). It is chordal, its three maximal cliques meet pairwise in exactly {x}, and it even admits a junction tree with singleton separators, yet x lies in three maximal cliques, and no accumulation tree produces it: an arc (u, v ) can belong to at most two blocks, namely as an outgoing arc of u and as the incoming arc of v. Any diagnostic based only on clauses (1), (2), and (4) would wrongly accept this graph.

Remark 5.1 (Non-adjacency, made explicit) . The inclusion G(T ) ⊆ H can also be read off directly. Proposition 7.1 (Recovery of the unrooted tree) . Remark 7.2 (Rooting) . The residual ambiguity is one of orientation , and it is more severe than a choice among blocks. Because G(T ) = L( T u) depends on T only through its underlying undirected tree, every rooting of T u yields the same Markov graph. The root may therefore be any node of T u, including a degree-one node, which corresponds 17 to no maximal clique, and orienting all edges away from it gives a rooted event tree with the same G(T ). # 8 Implications for inference and structure inference 

The structural properties established above have direct implications for inference and model selection, and provide a justification for the use of decomposable graphical models here that goes beyond computational convenience. 

# 8.1 Likelihood factorisation 

Since G(T ) is decomposable with singleton separators, the joint distribution of Y fac-torises as in (4): a product of clique marginals divided by a product of univariate sep-arator marginals. The likelihood is therefore a product of low-dimensional terms, each clique connected to the rest of the model through a single binary variable, and maximum likelihood estimation is available in closed form clique by clique when the estimate is in-terior; with sparse clique tables it may fall on the boundary, where the closed form is the extended (limiting) maximum likelihood estimate. The exponential factor in the cost of exact inference is governed by max a |Ca|, i.e. by the branching degree of the event tree (Corollary 6.5(3)); the total cost grows in addition linearly with the number of cliques and of variables.

rather than in its interior. The gap in dimension is therefore not a regular number of degrees of freedom, and the two models cannot be compared by an ordinary likelihood-ratio test; the appropriate comparison is a test of support compatibility, discussed in Section 10.2. 

# 8.3 Structure inference 

Because the true Markov graph is decomposable with singleton separators, searches over general undirected graphs may be restricted to this class when the accumulation model is taken as given . We stress that this is a modelling assumption and not, as it is sometimes phrased, a restriction “without loss of generality”: the whole point of Section 10 is that the restriction is refutable, and it can only be refuted by a procedure that is allowed to leave the class. In practice, one therefore fits within the restricted class and compares against a wider one. Within the class, the search space is highly structured (though it may still be combi-natorially large). By Theorem 7.3 the admissible graphs are exactly the connected line 20 graphs of trees, chordal, with pairwise singleton clique intersections and no vertex in three cliques; by Corollary 6.5 such a graph is determined, up to relabelling, by its junc-tion tree together with the clique sizes (on a fixed set of variables one must in addition assign the separator and private variables). Forest- and tree-based selection procedures (Edwards et al., 2010; Abreu et al., 2010) are natural here, and the treewidth bound of Corollary 6.5(3) guarantees that scoring remains tractable whenever the branching degree is bounded. 

# 9 Instantiation: mutation accumulation and lineage tracing 

Everything above is independent of any application, and a reader who is content with the statistical formulation of Section 2.2, a single draw from a tree, recorded through the indicator vector of a laminar family, may stop here. This section is the first at which subject-matter knowledge is used, and it exists to answer one question: is there a real setting in which Assumption 2.3 is substantively correct rather than a technical convenience? There is, and identifying it is the point of the section. 

# 9.1 Mapping events to mutations 

Each event Ei corresponds to a mutation at a specific site. The tree T is the mutation tree relating the sampled units, the root being a common ancestral state. A mutation is assumed to arise at most once on the tree and, once it has arisen, to be inherited by every descendant of the point at which it arose, so that the mutations carried by a sampled unit are exactly those on the path from the root to its position in the tree. The binary vector Y = ( Y1, . . . , Y p) recording presence or absence of mutations in a sampled unit is therefore generated exactly by the process of Section 2, and irreversibility and persistence in the sense of Section 2 are the biological assumptions of no reversion and faithful inheritance. 

# 9.2 Why lineages are observed at arbitrary stages 

The essential point concerns what is sampled . In the classical phylogenetic picture, sequences are contemporaneous taxa at the tips of a tree, and each observation is a complete root-to-leaf path. By Proposition 2.5, that sampling scheme destroys the very structure we wish to exploit: the internal arc indicators become deterministic functions of the others and the Markov graph collapses to a disjoint union of sibling-leaf cliques. Leaf sampling is thus the wrong idealisation for the present purpose, and it is worth being explicit that this is a statement about the observation scheme, not about the biology. Lineage tracing has a different structure. A haplotype is the observed combination of mutations carried by a sampled unit, in the notation of this paper, a realisation of the vector Y . The decisive empirical fact is that in lineage-tracing data some sampled haplotypes are ancestral to other sampled haplotypes : the sample can contain units caught at intermediate stages of accumulation, not only at the end. In mitochondrial lineage tracing and in single-cell mitochondrial genotyping, intermediate states are observed directly and routinely (Ludwig et al., 2019); and in mtDNA haplotype networks used in human identification and population genetics, ancestral haplotypes persist in the sample 21 alongside the derived haplotypes that descend from them (Amorim, 2019). In such data the observation point of a lineage is plausibly modelled as a random node of T , internal nodes included, as in Assumption 2.3, though the assumption of positive mass at every 

node is stronger than the mere presence of some ancestral haplotypes in the sample. A similar structure arises for somatic mutation accumulation in tumour progression, where sampled cells may span a range of stages of the progression, and in cultural or linguistic transmission, where conservative and innovative variants of a tradition coexist in the same sample. 

# 9.3 Model assumptions 

The assumptions of Section 2 correspond to standard simplifying assumptions in molec-ular evolution. That each event occurs at most once is the assumption of no recurrent mutation : a site does not mutate twice independently in different parts of the tree. Persistence, an event, once realised, is retained by every descendant, is the absence of 

reversion , that is, of a back-mutation restoring the ancestral state. The restriction to a single tree reflects the absence of recombination : no unit inherits material from two distinct ancestral histories, so every unit has a single line of descent and the histories form a tree rather than a network. Taken together, these three assumptions, no recurrent mutation, no reversion and no recombination, are precisely the infinite-sites model of molecular evolution, under which the data admit a lossless representation as a perfect phylogeny (Gusfield, 1991); the model of the present paper is its single-draw, arbitrary-stage observation counterpart. Assumption 2.3 is, however, stronger than a bare perfect phylogeny: it requires every intermediate genotype to carry positive sampling proba-bility, and, via the one-event-per-arc convention, it resolves several mutations falling between two sampled states into an ordering with intermediate nodes that need not themselves be observed. The present setting is thus an infinite-sites/perfect-phylogeny model equipped with a full-node-support, arbitrary-stage observation scheme. These are idealisations, but they are commonly adopted where mutation rates are low, and recom-bination is negligible, in particular for mitochondrial genomes, chloroplast genomes, and many bacterial genomes (Didelot and Maiden, 2010). In such settings, the dependence structure among mutation indicators is governed by the constraints of Theorem 3.1, and the departures catalogued in Section 10 are typical consequences of failures of these assumptions, although a given departure need not identify which assumption has failed.

# 10.4 Recombination and blockwise models 

The results rely on the assumption that the observed variables are generated by ac-cumulation along a single tree. Recombination and horizontal transfer violate this by merging two distinct ancestral histories into one unit, so that the histories form a net-work rather than a tree (Didelot and Maiden, 2010). One way to accommodate them is through blockwise models, in which different subsets of variables are associated with different underlying trees, with dependence between blocks mediated by a limited num-ber of shared variables. Within each block the conclusions of Theorem 3.1 apply. We 25 emphasise, however, that the global graph in such a model need not be decomposable at all, and that a mixture of decomposable models is in general not a graphical model; the correct statement is that the block-and-separator structure is inherited locally, and that the scale at which decomposability breaks down is itself informative about the scale of recombination. A systematic treatment is beyond the scope of this paper. 

# 10.5 Observation noise 

Suppose the observed vector is ˜Y , obtained from the latent Y by independent misclas-sification, Pr( ˜Yi̸ = Yi) = εi ∈ (0 , 1) , independently across i given Y . It is tempting to assume that noise leaves the conditional independence structure intact and merely weakens the observed dependencies. For the latent vector this is trivially true; for the 

observed vector it is false, and in the wrong direction. The screening mechanism of Lemma 4.1 operates through Yh exactly : conditioning on the true hinge value renders one of the two blocks degenerate. Conditioning on a noisy surrogate ˜Yh does not, since ˜Yh leaves residual uncertainty about Yh, through which dependence continues to propagate. Consequently, the Markov graph of ˜Y is typically 

denser than that of Y , and the singleton-separator structure is the first casualty, not the last. For the tree of Figure 2, for instance, the Markov graph of ˜Y is complete for every common misclassification rate we tried (from 0.02 to 0.1) and for unequal rates, and noise on the hinge Y4 alone already adds six edges. We do not prove that completeness holds generically; what the argument above establishes is that exact hinge screening is in general destroyed. What survives is a matter of magnitude, not of structure: for small εi

the spurious edges carry weak partial associations, and the block-and-separator pattern remains detectable as the dominant signal, but it is no longer exact. The practical implication is the opposite of the reassuring one. A graph estimated from noisy accumulation data will tend to violate Theorem 3.1 even when the mechanism is correct, so the diagnostics of Section 10.1 must be applied with an explicit noise model, or interpreted as tests of “accumulation and clean observation” jointly. Latent-variable treatments that model Y explicitly and integrate out the misclassification recover the exact structure (for example the hidden conjunctive Bayesian network of Gerstung et al., 2009), at the price of an EM or Bayesian computation; we regard this as the natural next step and do not pursue it here. 

# 10.6 Approximate accumulation 

The model assumes strict irreversibility and persistence. In practice these may hold only approximately: events may occasionally be lost, reverted, or misrecorded. Such departures can be viewed as perturbations of the structural assumptions, under which the graphical properties established here describe the dominant dependence structure, with deviations from irreversibility introducing additional and typically weaker inter-actions. When departures are rare, the strong dependencies are still organised into blocks connected by hinge variables. We caution, however, that the exact conditional-independence graph is not a continuous function of the distribution: an arbitrarily small generic perturbation can add every missing edge, so the induced graph need not stay close to a singleton-separator graph. What remains close is the magnitude pattern, the partial associations or conditional mutual information, rather than the exact graph. A 26 formal theory, in particular, rates at which reversion or event loss degrades the graphical structure, would be a natural companion to the noise analysis of Section 10.5. 

# 11 Concluding remarks 

This paper establishes a direct connection between irreversible event-accumulation pro-cesses on trees and decomposable graphical models. The conditional independence struc-ture induced by such processes is highly constrained: the Markov graph is chordal, its maximal cliques correspond to the nodes of the underlying undirected event tree of de-gree at least two (so that G(T ) = L( T u) is the line graph of that tree), no vertex lies in three cliques, and the separators are the singleton arc-events joining two such nodes. A single screening identity, each hinge variable separates its subtree from the rest, deliv-ers the entire structure. The converse (Theorem 7.3) shows that these constraints are not merely necessary but characterise the realisable graphs exactly, so that graphical compatibility with the mechanism is decidable from the estimated graph alone. Three lessons stand out. First, the modelling of the observation scheme matters : lin-eages must be observed at arbitrary stages of progression, not only at their endpoints, or the flow constraints of complete lineages collapse the graph entirely (Proposition 2.5). Second, chordal independence graphs, often introduced for mathematical convenience (because they yield decomposable models with tractable likelihoods), also arise natu-rally as representations of irreversible event accumulation. Third, the graphical model is a relaxation of the accumulation model, of much larger dimension (Section 8.2); the graph captures the conditional independences but not the support restrictions that ir-reversibility imposes. When an inferred graph violates the constraints derived here, the deviation is itself informative. It signals recombination, recurrent events, mixtures of distinct populations, or observational noise. More broadly, the results illustrate how structural properties of graphical models can reflect fundamental constraints imposed by the underlying generative mechanism in any system governed by the irreversible ac-cumulation of events along lineages.
