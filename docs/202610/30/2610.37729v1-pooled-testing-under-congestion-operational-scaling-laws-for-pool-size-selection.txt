Abstract 

Classical pooled-testing rules choose pool sizes to reduce the number of tests. In practice, however, samples arrive over time, pools take time to form, testing capacity is finite, and positive pools create bursts of follow-up work. These operational features can make the test-efficient pool size far from the delay-efficient one. We formulate pooled testing with stochastic arrivals and finite testing capacity as a queueing system, and derive exact performance representations and asymptotic scaling laws for fixed-batch Dorfman testing and a dynamic two-threshold policy. The analysis reveals three distinct design regimes. When test usage is the main concern, the classical Dorfman rule is recovered and the optimal pool size scales with the inverse square root of prevalence. When delay accumulated while the laboratory is busy testing another batch matters, positive pools create service-time variability that grows more rapidly with pool size than the mean retesting burden, and the optimal scale shifts to the inverse cube root of prevalence. When waiting to assemble a pool is itself costly, the optimal fixed pool size remains bounded even as prevalence becomes very small. Under severe capacity scarcity, congestion remains relevant at the classical Dorfman scale: the square-root law survives, but with a capacity-dependent constant. Dynamic pooling separates two operational decisions that fixed pool sizes conflate. Alower threshold determines when routine testing should begin, while an upper threshold lim-its pooling when backlog is high. The lower threshold follows the cube-root scale, whereas the upper threshold primarily acts as congestion control. The results show how preva-lence, capacity, and delay jointly determine pooling decisions and explain why operationally optimal pools can be much smaller than those suggested by test-efficiency calculations alone. 

Keywords: pooled testing, dynamic batching, scaling laws, Dorfman’s rule, low prevalence, cube root, queueing, congestion, service operations. 

# 1 Introduction 

Pooled testing combines individual specimens and tests them jointly. If a pool is negative, all specimens are cleared at once; if it is positive, additional testing is used to identify the positive individuals. The idea dates to Dorfman’s 1943 proposal for syphilis screening Dorfman (1943) and has since been used in blood-bank screening, HIV surveillance, hepatitis testing, malaria monitoring, tuberculosis and influenza screening, and large-scale SARS-CoV-2 testing. These applications share the same basic statistical opportunity: when prevalence is low, most pools are negative and a single assay can resolve many individuals. Pooling can therefore economize

The classical design variable is the pool size . Under Dorfman’s two-stage procedure, a pool of size k is tested once; if it is positive, the k individual specimens are retested. With individual positivity probability p0, expected tests per sample equal 1

k + 1 − (1 − p0)k.

At low prevalence, 1 − (1 − p0)k ≈ kp 0, so minimizing testing effort balances 1 /k against kp 0.This produces Dorfman’s familiar square-root prescription kD ∼ 1/√p0. The rule is simple and powerful: as prevalence falls, larger pools become statistically attractive at a precisely determined rate. A diagnostic laboratory, however, does not operate on preassembled pools. It receives a stream of samples. Samples must wait for enough companions to form a pool; completed pools may wait for equipment; and positive pools create additional testing that consumes capacity and makes service requirements random. The pool-size decision therefore changes both the 

amount of work and the timing of work. This distinction is consequential whenever the value of a diagnostic result decays with delay. Blood products cannot be released before screening is completed, infection-control decisions in hospitals depend on timely surveillance, and the value of infectious-disease testing for isolation, treatment, and contact management deteriorates as turnaround times lengthen. The tension is visible in practice. Cabrera et al. (2023) describe pooled PCR testing in an acute-care hospital where demand exceeded the capacity of individual testing. The reported data imply prevalence of approximately 0 .43%. At that prevalence, the square-root benchmark is about 15 samples and direct minimization of expected Dorfman tests gives a pool size of 16. The laboratory used pools of size 4. From the viewpoint of test counts alone, that choice looks conservative. From the viewpoint of a capacity-constrained service operation, it need not be: the laboratory had to trade reagent savings against formation delay, stochastic retesting workload, and turnaround time. This paper develops an operational theory of that tradeoff. Our central question is not whether pooling is beneficial, but how the optimal degree of pooling scales when time and capacity matter . The answer is not a single correction to Dorfman’s rule. Different operational objectives generate different asymptotic balances and, as a consequence, different scaling laws. Test-efficiency objectives retain the classical inverse-square-root scale. Queueing-sensitive objectives produce an inverse-cube-root scale. Once the time required to assemble a pool receives positive weight, the optimal fixed-pool size remains bounded even as prevalence vanishes. A separate scarce-capacity regime preserves the square-root exponent but changes its leading constant. Pooling is also inherently a dynamic batching decision . Our basic policy has two thresholds, (l, k ). The laboratory can start service once l samples are available. If more samples have accumulated by the time capacity becomes available, it processes all waiting samples up to the cap k. Thus l is a release threshold that governs responsiveness in normal operation, whereas 

k is a maximum pooling threshold that governs how aggressively backlog is absorbed. The familiar fixed-pool policy is the special case l = k.Two-threshold batching structures have appeared previously in related queueing models. Bar-Lev et al. (2007) optimize lower and upper batch thresholds for an incomplete-identification bulk queue, and Benjaafar et al. (2025) report a two-threshold policy for a dynamic extension of their pooled-testing model. Our use of ( l, k ) builds on this operational structure and provides an exact low-prevalence characterization of the thresholds under complete identification. The distinction between l and k is operationally important. A fixed pool forces the labora-tory to use the same number of samples when the system is quiet and when it is congested. The (l, k ) policy separates those situations. Thus congestion can affect pooling in two fundamentally different ways. Under fixed capacity and a delay-sensitive objective, it contributes to a mechanism that selects a new cube-root scale. Under scarce capacity, it remains first-order within the classical square-root scale and changes the optimal constant. The paper builds on several strands of the pooled-testing literature. The classical literature asks how pooling can reduce the number or cost of tests, starting with Dorfman and extending to richer designs that account for heterogeneous infection risks, imperfect tests, prevalence uncertainty, dilution, and alternative assay technologies; see, among others, Wein and Zenios (1996); Aprahamian et al. (2019, 2020); El Hajj et al. (2022); Bish et al. (2024) and the broader survey of Aldridge et al. (2019). Recent large-scale implementations confirm both the practical value of pooling and the importance of prevalence and assay sensitivity in determining useful pool sizes Abdalhamid et al. (2020); Bish et al. (2021); Mutesa et al. (2021); Cleary et al. (2021); Barak et al. (2021). Our results retain Dorfman’s two-stage protocol as the main benchmark, but the object of optimization is different: the testing system is fed by stochastic arrivals, so pool size also controls when service can start and how random retesting work interacts with finite capacity. A second strand places group testing explicitly in a queueing or dynamic setting. Bar-Lev et al. (2007) formulate a bulk-service queue in which the batch size lies between lower and upper thresholds and optimize these thresholds for an incomplete-identification group-testing problem. Their ( m, M ) structure is closely related to the release/cap logic of our ( l, k ) policy, although contaminated groups are not individually resolved in their model. Bhimaraju and Varshney (2022) study the scheduling of group tests over time. More generally, our embedded-chain formulation belongs to the bulk-queue tradition; see, e.g., Chaudhry and Templeton (1983); Bruneel and Kim (1993); Janssen and van Leeuwaarden (2005). The paper closest to ours is Benjaafar et al. (2025). They study a fixed-batch Dorfman system with Poisson sample arrivals and stochastic testing times and make the key operational point that maximizing throughput need not minimize the delay experienced by samples. They derive an exact expression for expected delay, characterize feasible pool sizes, and show numer-ically that the throughput-maximizing pool can generate substantially larger delay than the delay-minimizing pool. They also study a dynamic extension and report numerically that its optimal policy has a two-threshold form. We view these results as an important starting point for the present paper. Our contribution is complementary to their finite-parameter optimization. Rather than asking only which pool size minimizes delay for a given parameter instance, we identify the asymptotic mechanisms that determine how the operationally optimal pool size changes with 4prevalence and capacity. In particular, we establish the cube-root queueing scale, character-ize the scarce-capacity regime in which a congestion-adjusted square-root law survives, derive global optimizer scaling under alternative operational objectives, and obtain exact asymptotics for a two-threshold ( l, k ) policy. The last result provides an analytical interpretation of the two-threshold structure observed numerically by Benjaafar et al. (2025): the lower threshold governs routine release, while the upper threshold acts as a congestion cap. The two-threshold structure itself is therefore not claimed as a new policy class. Our contribution is its analytical characterization. In particular, we identify the low-prevalence scaling of the routine release threshold, explain the role of the upper threshold as a congestion cap, and derive the associated asymptotic laws. Other recent work emphasizes complementary operational considerations. Lee et al. (2022) study pooling on high-throughput and point-of-care platforms with attention to throughput and turnaround. Zismanov et al. (2024) motivate combinatorial pooling partly by the delay created by second-stage retesting. Pagotto et al. (2025) examine pooled PCR testing for hospi-tal surveillance, where timely feedback is central. Augenblick et al. (2022) emphasize repeated surveillance and testing frequency, while Long et al. (2026) incorporate test errors and depen-dence into a distributionally robust pooling model. Relative to this literature, our focus is on exact queueing foundations and asymptotic design principles that reveal which operational mechanism selects the pool-size scale. The paper broadly makes four contributions. First, it provides an exact analytical char-acterization of the two-threshold batching policy, distinguishing the routine release threshold 

l from the upper congestion-control threshold k. The policy structure is operationally equiv-alent to the ( M, Q ) policy studied in Benjaafar et al. (2025). The contribution here lies in deriving its stationary representation and asymptotic behavior. Second, it develops a unified embedded-chain and renewal-reward analysis for state-dependent batching, with fixed batch-ing recovered as a benchmark and an exact root representation available in that case. This exact structure makes it possible to identify the heavy-traffic and low-prevalence mechanisms without relying on queueing approximations. Third, it establishes the operational scaling laws summarized in Table 1, including the square-root law for testing effort, the cube-root law for the busy-testing component Wqueue , the bounded regime under formation-sensitive and total-turnaround objectives, and the capacity-dependent square-root correction under scarce capacity. For the state-dependent policy, the analysis further shows, within the considered cube-root scal-ing family, that the cube-root scale governs the normal release threshold l, whereas k acts as a congestion-control parameter relevant in atypical backlog states. Fourth, the paper trans-lates these analytical results into numerical examples anchored by the prevalence reported in an acute-care hospital application. To separate the operational mechanisms from an arbitrary physical time scale, the capacity examples use the normalized ratio λ/μ = 2 and report delays in units of the mean duration 1 /μ of one testing stage. The numerical exhibits compare classical and operational policies, decompose turnaround time, and show how the different scaling laws translate into finite-prevalence pool sizes. # 2 Model and dynamic batching policy 

Samples arrive according to a Poisson process with rate λ and enter a single capacity-constrained testing facility. A sample cannot be processed on its own immediately upon arrival: it first has to be assigned to a testing batch. For a batch of size b, let S(b) denote the duration of the complete testing cycle, from the moment testing starts until every specimen in the batch has received a definitive classification. The distribution of S(b) is protocol specific. The queueing construction below only requires these batch-level testing-time distributions; the explicit results later in the paper specialize to Dorfman testing with exponential testing phases. The key operational decision is therefore not simply a static pool size. It is a batch-release rule . Fix integers 1 ≤ l ≤ k. The lower threshold l is the smallest batch that the laboratory is willing to release, while the upper threshold k is the largest batch processed in a single testing cycle. The decision is taken whenever the testing facility becomes available. If fewer than l

samples are waiting, the laboratory deliberately waits until l samples have accumulated. If between l and k samples are waiting, it starts immediately and processes all of them. If at least 

k samples are waiting, it starts immediately with a full batch of size k and leaves the remaining samples in queue. This distinction is operationally important. Waiting for additional samples when the system is quiet creates formation delay ; using additional samples that are already waiting does not. The lower threshold l therefore controls responsiveness in normal operation. The upper threshold k

controls how aggressively the laboratory can absorb backlog once congestion has already created a queue. A fixed-pool model forces these two decisions to coincide. Our policy separates them. Fixed batching is recovered as the special case l = k.The policy is deliberately simple. It does not require forecasting future arrivals or solving a dynamic program at each decision epoch. It uses only the queue length observed when testing capacity becomes available and translates that state into a batch size. This makes ( l, k )both analytically tractable and operationally interpretable: l is a release threshold and k is a congestion cap. For a fixed batch size k, the natural traffic intensity is ρk = λE[S(k)]/k . Stability requires 

ρk < 1, because a testing cycle processes k samples while, on average, λE[S(k)] new samples arrive during that cycle. For the state-dependent ( l, k ) policy, the exact embedded-chain drift is governed by the full-batch state: whenever the queue is large, the system repeatedly serves batches of size k. The corresponding stability condition is therefore again λE[S(k)] < k ; see Section 3. We use the notation ρk both for the fixed-batch benchmark and for the dynamic system near this full-batch capacity boundary. For a tagged sample, let ¯W denote the time from its arrival until the batch containing it enters testing. We decompose this pre-testing delay according to the state of the testing facility as ¯W = Widle +Wqueue . In particular, Widle measures the portion of the pre-testing waiting time accumulated while the testing facility is idle (i.e., idle-time batch formation), whereas Wqueue 

is the portion accumulated while the facility is busy testing another batch. It includes both the case in which the tagged batch has already been formed but is waiting behind an earlier batch, and the case in which the tagged batch is still being formed while the laboratory is busy. Therefore, the turnaround time is given by W = Widle + Wqueue + Wtest , where Wtest is the time from the beginning of the tagged batch’s testing cycle until the final classification of the sample. The distinction between the first two components matters. A large pool can save tests 7while increasing the time needed to form a batch; conversely, when backlog is already present, a large batch can be formed without additional formation delay but may create a long or highly variable testing cycle. 

Remark 2.1 (Relation to the decomposition in Benjaafar et al. (2025)) . The pre-testing waiting time ¯W considered here coincides with the total pre-testing waiting time of a tagged sample analyzed in Benjaafar et al. (2025). However, the decompositions are different. In their formu-lation, ¯W = Wform + Wq, where Wform is the time required for the tagged sample’s batch to be formed and Wq is the subsequent waiting time of the formed batch before its testing begins. In the fixed-batch model, E[Wform ] = k−12λ , E[Wq] = E[ ¯W ] − k−12λ . Contrary to that case, the decomposition used in this paper partitions the pre-testing waiting time according to the state of the testing facility. Thus, although the two formulations yield the same total pre-testing waiting time ¯W , their queueing components are defined differently and do not coincide. In particular, the Wqueue used in this paper includes every part of the pre-testing waiting time during which the laboratory is busy testing another batch, including periods during which the tagged batch is still being formed. The quantity Wq in Benjaafar et al. (2025) refers only to the waiting of the tagged batch after its formation. Under fixed batching, all three consequences are tied to the same parameter k. Under ( l, k ), routine formation is governed primarily by l, whereas k matters when backlog is present. One of the central results later in the paper is that, in the low-prevalence delay-sensitive regime, the system chooses a batch of size l with probability tending to one. Thus the asymptotic pool-sizing rule becomes a rule for routine release , while k remains available as a separate congestion-control parameter. The analysis begins with the general ( l, k ) system because this is the primitive operational policy. We first derive exact steady-state expressions without imposing a particular testing protocol or asymptotic regime. We then specialize to fixed-batch Dorfman testing, where the kernel admits a convenient root representation. That benchmark exposes the heavy-traffic and low-prevalence mechanisms particularly clearly. Finally, we return to the state-dependent policy and show how those mechanisms translate into a release threshold and a congestion cap.

# 5 When does congestion modify Dorfman’s square-root rule? 

The previous section shows that queueing delay can select a cube-root pool-size scale even when the testing-effort optimum follows Dorfman’s square-root rule. There is, however, a different question that is equally important for capacity-constrained laboratories: can congestion change the classical Dorfman recommendation while the pool size itself remains on the square-root scale? 

The answer is yes, but only under a particular joint scaling of prevalence and testing capacity. This section isolates that regime. The distinction is useful because it separates two ways in which operations can modify classical pooling. Under fixed capacity and a delay-sensitive objective, the relevant exponent can change from 1 /2 to 1 /3, as established in Section 4 and formalized globally in Section 6. By contrast, when testing capacity itself becomes scarce as prevalence decreases, congestion can enter at first order within the classical square-root domain. In that case the exponent survives but the optimal constant changes.

5.4 Where the square-root regime sits in the broader asymptotic map

At the Dorfman scale k = Θ( p−1/20 ), the expected numbers of pooled and follow-up tests are comparable. With fixed capacity, utilization still tends to zero; with the critical scaling (19), congestion remains first-order and Theorem 5.1 applies. Such scales can be useful as boundary cases, but they no longer describe low-prevalence pooling in the classical sense and do not generate a new optimality result in the present paper. # 6 Operational objectives and optimal scaling 

The previous sections characterize how pooling changes the stochastic dynamics of the testing system. We now turn from performance analysis to the design question that is ultimately relevant for practice: which pool size should a laboratory choose? The answer depends on what the laboratory is trying to optimize. Classical Dorfman pooling minimizes the expected number of tests. Benjaafar et al. (2025) show numerically that replacing this throughput-oriented criterion by expected delay can materially change the recommended pool size. We take the next step and ask which asymptotic scale is selected by each objective. An operational system may care about queueing delay, or may attach explicit costs to testing effort, queueing, and pool formation. Once these objectives are distinguished, the different scaling laws derived earlier acquire a direct decision-theoretic interpretation. This section is therefore not another asymptotic approximation of the queue. Its purpose is to convert the exact results of Sections 4–5 into statements about global integer optimizers .This distinction is important. Balancing two leading-order terms can suggest a candidate scale, but does not by itself exclude much smaller or much larger pool sizes. The arguments below use the exact queueing bounds to rule out such alternatives. Throughout, p0 ↓ 0 while λ and μ remain fixed. For each p0, optimization is over stable integer pool sizes. We write T (k) = 1 /k + pk for expected tests per sample, where pk =1 − (1 − p0)k. Whenever kp 0 → 0, the familiar expansion pk ∼ kp 0 applies. Here we have to distinguish the delay quantities according to the objective under consideration. The queueing results in Sections 6.1-6.4 refer to the renewal-reward busy-testing component Wqueue defined in (9). The turnaround-time decomposition is W = Widle + Wqueue + Wtest .On the other hand, Section 6.5 considers another formation-sensitive objective based on the batch-formation benchmark E[Wform (k)] = k−12λ . As said above, this benchmark is not identical to the renewal-reward idle component Widle , and it is not an additional term in the turnaround-time decomposition. It is used only to study objectives that assign a positive cost to conventional batch formation. The three relevant balances can already be seen from these expressions and from the exact queueing results obtained earlier. Test efficiency balances the pooled-test term 1 /k against the expected retesting term kp 0, suggesting the classical square-root scale. # 7 Conclusion 

Classical group testing asks how many specimens should be pooled in order to save tests. Once pooled testing is embedded in a capacity-constrained service system, that question is incomplete. Pool size also determines how long a laboratory waits before starting, how much stochastic workload a positive pool creates, and how strongly that variability is amplified by congestion. The result is not one universal correction to Dorfman’s rule, but a set of operational regimes tied to different scarce resources. The classical square-root law remains the correct benchmark when testing effort is the objective. It also survives, with a congestion-dependent leading constant, under the unique joint prevalence–capacity scaling that keeps utilization nondegenerate at the Dorfman scale. When the objective prices the busy-testing component Wqueue instead, the rare positive-pool workload enters through the service-time second moment and the relevant scale becomes a cube root. Adding a fixed positive testing cost changes the cube-root constant but not the exponent. By contrast, any positive cost of forming batches prevents the routine fixed-pool recommendation from diverging as prevalence vanishes. These results explain why operationally appropriate pools can be much smaller than the classical test-minimizing recommendation, particularly at very low prevalence. The state-dependent ( l, k ) policy gives this classification a practical interpretation. The lower threshold l determines when the laboratory normally releases a batch, while the upper threshold k determines how aggressively it can absorb backlog. In the delay-sensitive low-prevalence regime, the system selects B = l with probability tending to one and the cube-root law therefore governs routine release. The congestion cap remains a separate control. Fixed batching is useful because it reveals the mechanism cleanly; dynamic batching shows how that mechanism should be implemented. The numerical exhibits are designed to connect these asymptotic statements to finite-prevalence decisions. The turnaround decomposition shows which delay component makes additional pooling unattractive. The prevalence–capacity map identifies where the congestion-corrected square-root regime is relevant. The objective comparison shows how quickly the square-root, cube-root, and bounded rules emerge and how costly it can be to apply Dorfman’s test-minimizing pool size to a laboratory whose actual objective includes time. These cube-root laws are derived under the sequential Dorfman retesting protocol. Extending them to parallel retesting, imperfect tests, dilution effects, or other testing protocols is left for future work.

This appendix provides additional finite-prevalence calculations that complement the asymp-totic results in the main text. The purpose is twofold. First, we compare the pool sizes selected by three distinct operational criteria: testing effort, queueing delay, and total turnaround time. Second, we examine how these choices change with the available testing capacity. This com-parison is useful because the three objectives respond to prevalence and capacity in markedly different ways. At low prevalence, the numerical results closely reflect the three regimes developed in the paper. The testing-effort optimizer follows the classical square-root scale, whereas the queueing-delay optimizer follows the cube-root scale. By contrast, the total-turnaround optimizer remains bounded. In the experiments below, the latter is driven mainly by the capacity ratio and, over the displayed low-prevalence range, follows the simple numerical pattern k⋆W ≈ 2λ/μ . This should not be interpreted as a new asymptotic formula; rather, it illustrates the bounded-pooling conclusion of Corollary 6.7 and the fact that formation time prevents total-turnaround minimization from favoring increasingly large pools as prevalence vanishes. Table 5 consolidates all numerical results. The quantities k⋆T , k⋆Q, and k⋆W minimize, respec-tively, the testing effort T (k), the renewal-reward fixed-batch queueing delay E[Wqueue ], and the total turnaround time E[W ]. The corresponding utilizations show how close the selected policies operate to the stability boundary. For each parameter configuration, we minimize over integer pool sizes 1 ≤ k ≤ Kmax = 400, retaining only stable choices satisfying ρk < 1. The repeated asymptotic-order rows from the separate tables are omitted: as established in the main text, k⋆T ∼ p−1/20 , k⋆Q ∼ p−1/30 , and k⋆W = Θ(1). The final panel considers the more moderate prevalence level p0 = 0 .1. Here capacity affects not only the preferred pool size but also feasibility. For the capacity ratios displayed, stable fixed-batch policies exist for λ/μ = 0 .5, 1, and 1 .5, whereas no stable fixed-batch policy is found for λ/μ ≥ 2. Thus, once prevalence is no longer very small, increasing the pool size need not restore stability: larger pools are positive more often and consequently generate more frequent and longer retesting cycles. Finally, Table 6 illustrates that the obtained optimizers in Table 4 are consistent with the theoretical result established in Theorem 6.3.
