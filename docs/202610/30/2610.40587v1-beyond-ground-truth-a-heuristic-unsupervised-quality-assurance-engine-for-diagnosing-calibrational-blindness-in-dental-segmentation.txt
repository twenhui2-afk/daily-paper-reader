# Beyond Ground Truth: A Heuristic Unsupervised Quality Assurance Engine for Diagnosing Calibrational Blindness in Dental Segmentation 

Fatematuj Johura Mim 

Department of Computer Science & Engineering North East University Bangladesh 

Sylhet, Bangladesh Email: fatematuj.jm@gmail.com 

Abstract —In real-world clinical settings, dental X-ray seg-mentation using deep learning methods suffers from reliability issues. Even when the segmentation quality is bad, deep learning models show high confidence in their predictions (calibrational blindness). Moreover, in real use, doctors do not have ground-truth masks to check their accuracy. To address this problem, we introduce a simple quality-checking engine called HUS-QA with three stages. We train it on the PRAD (Periapical Dental X-ray) dataset to build our SimCLR self-supervised ResNet-18 U-Net model, which generates the segmentation mask and then evaluates its quality. Our HUS-QA checks the segmentation mask quality without a ground-truth mask. Prediction confi-dence, edge sharpness, shape connectivity, and area-size penalty are the simple indicators considered in this research. Results show that standard confidence methods fail (r = −0.1394 ,

p = 1 .7864 × 10 −3, ROC AUC = 0 .4275 ). By contrast, our HUS-QA shows a positive correlation with actual Dice scores (r = 0 .4647 , p = 3 .7510 × 10 −28 ) and achieves a strong ROC-AUC of 0.8405 . Therefore, HUS-QA serves as a practical tool for clinical quality control. 

Index Terms —Dental Radiography, Image Segmentation, Self-Supervised Learning, Quality Assurance, Calibrational Blind-ness, Dental X-ray Segmentation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In modern medical systems, automatically segmenting den-tal X-rays is essential, as it helps clinicians diagnose oral conditions [1], [2]. Deep learning models, especially encoder-decoder models [3], help doctors detect oral diseases and save time by segmenting different dental anatomical structures in images [4]. But in real-world use, calibrational blindness is a major problem. Deep learning models often show high confidence even when their output masks are wrong or in-complete [5]. After deployment, clinicians have no labels to check whether the masks generated by deep learning models are reliable or misleading [6]. We need a quality-control framework that can assess pre-dicted mask quality without ground truth to build trust in Medical AI tools [6], [7]. Standard quality checks only look at basic confidence scores, but high confidence does not guarantee good shape or structure [5]. To solve this issue, we present the Heuristic Unsupervised Quality Assurance (HUS-QA) engine. By looking at four simple visual indicators such as confidence score, boundary sharpness, component connectivity, and anatomical area size, HUS-QA checks the segmentation quality of X-rays. It combines these indicators into one final score to tell doctors if a mask needs manual checking. Also, to lower the cost of expert annotations, our framework uses Self-Supervised Learning (SimCLR) to learn features from unannotated X-ray images first [8], [9]. The primary contributions of this work are:  

> •

We present HUS-QA, a simple approach that checks dental segmentation quality and catches overconfident errors without using ground-truth masks.  

> •

We combine basic confidence scores with structural sig-nals like Sobel edge sharpness, shape connectivity, and area penalties.  

> •

We use a SimCLR self-supervised ResNet-18 [10] U-Net model on the PRAD dataset to get good segmentation results with fewer labeled images.  

> •

Test results show that HUS-QA has a positive correlation with Dice scores ( r = 0 .4647 , p = 3 .7510 × 10 −28 ) and yields a strong ROC-AUC of 0.8405 for separating bad segmentations, which is significantly better than standard confidence methods ( −0.1394 , ROC-AUC = 0 .4275 ). II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Deep Learning in Dental Radiography 

In medical image segmentation, convolutional neural net-works such as U-Net models [3] are standard AI tools [4]. Deep learning methods are widely used for tooth segmentation, cancer detection, caries detection, and anatomical structure analysis in the dental sector [1], [2]. However, training tra-ditional supervised models requires a huge amount of pixel-level annotated data from experts, which is time-consuming and expensive. 

B. Self-Supervised Learning in Medical AI 

The problem of labeled data scarcity can be solved by Self-Supervised Learning (SSL). Contrastive methods like SimCLR [8] learn useful features from unlabeled images by pulling the two augmented views of a single image close together in feature space, while pushing views from other images further

Additionally, recent studies show that self-supervised learn-ing has performed well in learning features without manual labeling in medical imaging [11]. 

C. Quality Control and Calibrational Blindness 

Deep neural networks can show high confidence even if they do not predict correctly. They frequently give very high confidence on noisy data [5]. In real medical systems, 

calibrational blindness can be risky for human beings. To check the prediction quality of models without ground truth, Valindria et al. have proposed reverse classification accuracy (RCA) [6]. Calculating model uncertainty is used by other methods to predict segmentation quality [12] or fix calibration issues [13]. On the other hand, using uncertainty estimation methods can be slower or heavier in real-time use. Therefore, shape rules and boundary metrics can be used to identify bad outputs without heavy uncertainty estimation. Moving forward with the concept, our HUS-QA engine combines confidence, edge sharpness, connectivity, and area limits into a simple framework which is designed to address dental segmentation errors and assess the quality of the segmentation. 

Research Gap. Some methods can check segmentation quality without ground truth, for example, reverse classifica-tion accuracy [6] and uncertainty-based methods [12], [13]. But these were mostly made for general medical images. As per our knowledge, it remains untested whether a model’s confidence can be trusted as a quality score on dental X-rays, although overconfidence is a known problem [5]. Our results show that this is a real problem. On periapical X-rays, confidence does not follow segmentation quality (AUC = 0.4275), and a nearly empty mask can still get 99.96% confidence (Fig. 5, Case C). So we propose HUS-QA, a simple quality check that does not need ground-truth masks. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

The full design of our three-stage framework is shown in Fig. 1. It works as an automatic quality assurance system using the PRAD dataset. During testing, a periapical dental X-ray is first pre-processed and sent through the SimCLR-initialized ResNet-18 [10] U-Net to produce a tooth mask. Next, the HUS-QA module checks the output reliability without ground-truth masks and gives a quality score to help doctors accept or reject the result. 

A. Dataset Setup and Data Splitting: 

We evaluated our system (HUS-QA) on PRAD dataset with separate data splits for self-supervised pre-training and super-vised training tasks. At first, we performed self-supervised feature learning using 2, 500 unlabeled X-ray images in Stage 1. For Stage 2, the labeled dataset was split into three sets. These three sets were 1, 750 image-mask pairs for training,

B. Stage 1: Pre-processing and Self-Supervised Learning: 

Image Pre-processing: At first, we converted dental X-rays into grayscale and enhanced them with CLAHE where the clip limit is 2.0, and the tile grid is 8 × 8 to make details clearer. Then images were saved as 3-channel RGB and resized to 

224 × 224 pixels. 

SimCLR Augmentations: Each image was transformed into two views using random crops (scale = 0 .6–1.0), horizon-tal flips ( p = 0 .5), brightness adjustments ( p = 0 .8), ImageNet normalization, and Gaussian noise ( p = 0 .3, σ = 0 .05 ). 

Self-Supervised Backbone Pre-training: A ResNet-18 [10] encoder with a two-layer projection head was trained on the 2, 500 unlabeled pre-training images using the SimCLR self-supervised learning method. The Normalized Temperature-scaled Cross-Entropy (NT-Xent) loss was used to optimize the encoder: 

LNT-Xent = − log exp( sim (zi, z j )/τ )

P2Nk=1 I[k̸ =i] exp( sim (zi, z k)/τ ) (1) Here, zi and zj are the projected representations of the two augmented views. sim (·) denotes cosine similarity, and 

⊮[k̸ =i] is an indicator function that equals 1 when k̸ = i.

τ is the temperature parameter, set to 0.5 in this work. The encoder was pre-trained for 100 epochs to learn robust structural representations from the unlabeled periapical dental radiographs. 

C. Stage 2: Dental segmentation initiated by Self-Supervised Learning: 

The pre-trained SimCLR ResNet-18 encoder was used as the encoder of a U-Net architecture for supervised dental segmentation. We applied standard medical preprocessing, including CLAHE, to all input images to enhance boundary clarity and resized them to 224 × 224 pixels. The SimCLR self-supervised ResNet-18 U-Net model was trained on 1, 750 

paired images and masks, with model selection guided by the 250 validation pairs. For 25 training epochs, the model received paired data augmentation in the form of random horizontal flipping and rotation ( ±15 ◦). The segmentation network was optimized using a hybrid Dice-BCE loss: 

L = λLBCE + (1 − λ)LDice (2) where λ = 0 .5. The AdamW optimizer with cosine anneal-ing learning-rate scheduling was used to train the network. The model, which achieved the highest validation Dice score on the validation set, was selected for the subsequent quality assessment stage. 

D. Stage 3: Proposed Ground-Truth-Free Segmentation Qual-ity Assessment (HUS-QA): 

Without relying on ground-truth annotations, the proposed HUS-QA module assesses the predicted segmentations across 

500 test samples. To estimate overall reliability, it analyzes both the soft prediction confidences and the corresponding binary masks. Four complementary quality indicators are extracted:  

> •

Prediction Confidence ( Qc): Computed from the confi-dence distribution of the predicted soft mask.  

> •

Boundary Sharpness ( Qs): Estimated using the Sobel gradient magnitude of the predicted segmentation bound-aries.  

> •

Structural Connectivity ( Qstr ): Measured from the con-nected components of the binary segmentation mask to penalize fragmented or isolated predictions.  

> •

Anatomical Area Consistency ( Qarea ): An area penalty factor to suppress anatomically unrealistic segmentation regions. The first three indicators are combined to compute the geometric quality score: 

Qg = 0 .40 Qc + 0 .40 Qs + 0 .20 Qstr (3) We chose the weights (0.40 , 0.40 , 0.20 ) based on 250 

validation pairs only. Edge sharpness ( Qs) and confidence (Qc) were the main factors of the mask quality, which are equally important ( 0.40 ). Shape connectivity ( Qstr ) provided a supporting check, with a lower weight ( 0.20 ). Finally, our HUS-QA score was obtained by including the anatomical area penalty: 

Qfinal = Qg × Parea (4) The range was limited to 0–100 for the resulting score, where higher values indicate more reliable segmentation out-puts. During testing, HUS-QA automatically predicted seg-mentation quality without requiring manual annotations, pro-viding an effective quality assessment mechanism for clinical support. IV. E. Discussion 

Our findings confirm that incorporating structural, boundary, and geometrical priors addresses calibrational blindness in deep learning segmentations without reference masks. HUS-QA exhibits superior correlation ( r = 0 .4647 ) compared to naive confidence ( r = −0.1394 ), providing a reliable safe-guard for automated dental radiography diagnostics. Although we tested our method only on the PRAD dataset, HUS-QA uses basic shape and edge rules. These general rules do not depend on a specific dataset, so the method may also work for other types of medical images. V. C ONCLUSION 

This study proposed HUS-QA, an unsupervised quality as-surance framework designed to detect overconfident segmenta-tion errors in dental AI workflows. Evaluated on dental X-rays, HUS-QA synthesizes confidence, boundary sharpness, struc-tural connectivity, and anatomical constraints without ground-truth masks. The framework achieves a moderate Pearson correlation with actual Dice scores ( r = 0 .4647 , p = 3 .7510 ×

10 −28 ) and an ROC AUC of 0.8405 , outperforming standard confidence baselines ( r = −0.1394 , ROC AUC = 0 .4275 ). As mentioned in Section IV-A, we chose a moderately performing model on purpose to test HUS-QA under difficult conditions. In future work, we plan to test HUS-QA on a better-performing U-Net to see if it still works well when segmentation quality is high.
