(Dated: October 29, 2026) Exceptional points (EPs) exhibit characteristic square-root spectral responses to weak perturbations, with response magnitudes governed by configuration-dependent prefactors that are often relatively small. Modulating these prefactor while preserving the exceptional characteristics offers an additional degrees of freedom for engineering non-Hermitian sensing responses. Here, we report a nonreciprocal non-Hermitian circuit system with gyrator-meditated coupling that supports a continuous family of EP configurations. Unlike reciprocal coupling, which typically yields a unique EP configuration for balanced gain and loss, nonreciprocal coupling allows the directional forward and backward coupling strengths to be varied while maintaining their product and thus preserving the EP condition and yielding a library of EPs. This additional freedom enables continuous control of the frequency-splitting magnitude without modifying the square-root characteristic dependence on absolute perturbation strength. We show that the spectral splitting induced by a given absolute resistance perturbation can be enhanced by appropriately adjusting the coupling asymmetry. Theoretical predictions, circuit simulations, and experimental reflection-spectrum measurements consistently confirms this tunable response. Our results establish nonreciprocal coupling as an alternative approach for engineering and enhancing EP-induced perturbative responses without changing their characteristic branch-point scaling. 

> Keywords: Coupled oscillators, non-Hermitian circuit, non-reciprocal coupling, sensitivity enhancement, gyrator

I. INTRODUCTION 

Resonant sensing relies on converting weak perturbations into measurable spectral responses. Exceptional points (EPs) — branch-point singularities of non-Hermitian systems at which both the eigenvalues and their associated eigenvectors coalesce — offer a distinctive mechanism for producing nonanalytic spectral responses [1, 2]. Near an Nth-order EP, a generic relative or absolute perturbation of strength 

ε induces an eigenvalue splitting proportional to ε1/N , i.e., 

∆ω ∼ Aε1/N , where the characteristic fraction exponent 1 /N

is fixed by the order of the singularity and the prefactor A is set by the underlying modal structure and couplings. This fractional-power scaling stands in contrast to the typically linear response of nondegenerate systems [3, 4] and has motivated extensive investigations of EP-enhanced sensing in optical resonators [5, 6], mechanical platforms [7], and electronics ranging from active gain–loss LRC circuits [8] to higher-order EP sensing in electronic and radio-frequency resonators [9, 10]. In addition, critical-point-based wireless LC sensors have also been demonstrated for detecting weak capacitive perturbations through reflection-spectrum measurements, highlighting the potential of non-Hermitian resonator systems for practical sensing applications [11]. While the scaling exponent is protected by the EP order, the prefactor A remains tunable. Quantitative measures of EP response strength and theoretical constraints on its magnitude have been established [12, 13], together with numerical approaches for extracting spectral response strength from wave simulations [14]. prefactor while maintaining the EP condition thus provides an additional opportunity for engineering and enhancing the spectral response of non-Hermitian sensors [15, 16]. In reciprocal systems, the forward and backward coupling strengths are identical. For fixed gain and loss, the EP condition then fixes a unique coupling value, leaving little freedom to reshape the perturbative response without lifting the degeneracy. Nonreciprocal coupling relaxes this restriction [17–21]: the EP condition can constrain a combination of the directional coupling strengths so that a continuous family of asymmetric configurations can support the same exceptional degeneracy. This additional freedom allows distinct asymmetric coupling configurations to support the same exceptional degeneracy, potentially enabling control of the perturbative response without shifting the system away from the EP. Gyrators furnish a practical circuit-based approach to implementing a nonreciprocal coupling. Gyrator-based resonator systems have been employed to realize nonreciprocal exceptional degeneracies and investigate their singular perturbation responses [22, 23]. In a balanced gain–loss system with asymmetric couplings, the EP condition can be maintained by fixing the product of the forward and backward coupling strengths while varying their ratio. The reciprocal configuration corresponds to a single point on this continuous manifold, whereas nonreciprocity enables access to a family of distinct EP configurations. This raises a fundamental question: Can coupling asymmetry be used to continuously tune the prefactor of the perturbative response while preserving the characteristic square-root scaling of a second-order EP? Here, we exploit this freedom in a gyrator-coupled nonreciprocal gain–loss resonator. By varying the coupling asymmetry while preserving the product of the directional coupling strength, we realize a continuous family of arXiv:2610.39157v1 [physics.optics] 29 Oct 2026 2second-order EP configurations. All members of the family exhibit the same characteristic square-root dependence on the perturbation strength, yet the corresponding frequency-splitting prefactor varies continuously with the coupling asymmetry. Increasing the coupling asymmetry enlarges the frequency splitting induced by a given small absolute resistance change, thereby improving the resolvability of minute resistance change under afixed spectral resolution threshold. Our results identify nonreciprocal coupling as an additional control parameter that enhances the practical sensitivity of an EP sensor without altering its characteristic branch-point scaling.

III. NONRECIPROCITY-CONTROLLED EXCEPTIONAL-POINT RESPONSE

Although all configurations along this family satisfy the same EP condition, their directional coupling strengths differ according to the nonreciprocity parameter η. Consequently, identical perturbations can produce different eigenfrequency splitting even when the unperturbed eigenvalues remain degenerated.

The theoretical predictions, LTspice simulations, and experimental measurements show consistent behavior, with all configurations exhibiting the characteristic square-root dependence on perturbation strength but different splitting amplitudes. Despite the different coupling asymmetries, the response follows the same fractional-power scaling, while the magnitude of the frequency splitting varies substantially among the EP configurations. To quantify this variation, we characterize each EP configuration by the square-root response coefficient 

A defined by ∆ f = A(η)√εR. As shown in Fig. 5(d), 

A increases systematically with η, in agreement with the theoretical prediction A(η) ∝ η1/4 derived in (12). The agreement among theory, simulation, and experiment demonstrates that nonreciprocal coupling enables continuous control of the frequency-splitting amplitude while preserving the characteristic square-root scaling of a second-order EP. The observed response tunability originates from the different roles of the directional coupling product and ratio in nonreciprocal EP systems. The coupling product fixes the exceptional degeneracy, whereas the coupling ratio controls the distribution of interaction strengths between the two directions. A perturbation applied to one coupling channel therefore produces a splitting that depends on the complementary directional coupling, allowing different EP configurations to exhibit distinct response coefficients while retaining identical square-root scaling. We stress that perturbations considered correspond to the absolute resistance changes rather than relative variations. Consequently, an appropriate choice of the initial gyrator resistance configuration can enhance the frequency splitting induced by a given small absolute resistance perturbation. This separation establishes coupling asymmetry as an additional parameter for engineering perturbative spectral responses without altering the underlying exceptional degeneracy.  

> IV. CONCLUSIONS

We have demonstrated that nonreciprocal coupling enables continuous control of the perturbative spectral response of exceptional-point (EP) systems without lifting the exceptional degeneracy. In a gyrator-coupled gain–loss resonator system, the EP condition constrains the product of the directional coupling coefficients, while their ratio remains adjustable. The predicted behavior is supported by analytical theory, LTspice simulations, and experimental reflection measurements. These findings establish coupling asymmetry as an additional design parameter for engineering perturbative responses near exceptional degeneracies. Although the enhanced spectral response does not necessarily translate into improved detection limits [24, 25], the ability to tune the splitting coefficient independently of the EP condition offers a flexible approach to controlling non-Hermitian resonator dynamics. This strategy provides opportunities for designing electronic systems with adjustable spectral responses for sensing and signal-processing applications. A promising extension of the proposed framework lies in wireless sensing and telemetry, where non-Hermitian sensor telemetry and electronically tunable nonreciprocal EP architectures have attracted growing interest [26, 27]. Integrating coupling-asymmetry control into such platforms could enable in situ tuning of the spectral response strength while maintaining EP condition, providing a route toward reconfigurable wireless sensing systems.

b. Circuit simulation and experimental data analysis. 

Circuit simulations were performed in LTspice using the gain–gyrator–loss topology described by the analytical model. For each nonreciprocity parameter η, the directional couplings were adjusted to satisfy the unperturbed EP condition.
