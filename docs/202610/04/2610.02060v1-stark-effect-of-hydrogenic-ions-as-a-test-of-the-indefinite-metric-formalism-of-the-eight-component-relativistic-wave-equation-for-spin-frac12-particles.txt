## Stark effect of hydrogenic ions as a test of the indefinite-metric formalism of the eight-component relativistic wave equation for spin-12 particles 

Paulus C. Tjiang, 1, ∗ Sylvia H. Sutanto, 1, † and Vincentius E. W. Tjia 1, ‡

> 1

Center for Theoretical Physics, Faculty of Science, Parahyangan Catholic University, Bandung 40141, Indonesia 

(Dated: October 2, 2026) The eight-component relativistic wave equation for spin- 12 particles of Robson and Staudte (FV 12 ), a Feshbach–Villars linearization of the Feynman–Gell-Mann equation, reproduces the Dirac hydro-genic spectrum with a doubled solution space and an indefinite inner product. Its exact Stark shifts must equal the Dirac ones; whether its indefinite-metric perturbation theory delivers them is not guaranteed. We compute the first- and second-order Stark shifts of the n = 1–3 levels of hydrogenic ions from H to U 91+ in the Schr¨ odinger, Dirac and FV 12 theories, evaluating all spectral sums, continuum included, by the Dalgarno–Lewis method. The FV 12 and Dirac shifts coincide level by level, to working precision at first order (closed-form roots) and to 45 significant figures at second order. Both ingredients are needed: without the explicit spin–field coupling the first-order roots become complex, and without the negative-norm states the ( Zα )2 coefficient is wrong. Norm sign and parity are locked in the doubled space: a potential lifting the 2 s1/2–2 p1/2 degeneracy needs its own spin–gradient term, and shifts placed by hand on the κ-labeled states give a spurious imaginary linear Stark shift. The relativistic linear Stark effect is smaller by finite factors, but a second-moment sum rule shows the non-relativistic linear Stark strength is conserved, the missing part reappearing in singular ( Zα )−2 second-order terms. For levels with a fine-structure partner, an exact intra-shell/regular split of the second-order shift gives the ( Zα )0 coefficients analytically and relates the π2 terms of the ( Zα )2 coefficients to the singular ones. The Dirac weak/strong-field crossover for n = 2 of hydrogen lies near 2.8 kV cm −1, and the perturbative window set by the fine structure grows as Z5.

I. INTRODUCTION 

The hydrogenic atom is the reference system against which quantum theories of bound states are measured. Its spectrum is known in closed form in the Schr¨ odinger and Dirac theories, and it is measured with an accuracy that few other systems can match: the Lamb shift [1] and a series of recent optical and microwave measurements in atomic hydrogen [2–6] test bound-state theory [7] at the level that fixes the fundamental constants [8], while highly charged hydrogen-like ions probe the same theory in the strong-field regime [9, 10]. The Schr¨ odinger theory gives levels that depend on the principal quantum num-ber n, the additional degeneracy in l being a property of the Coulomb potential [11]; the Dirac theory gives levels that depend on n and on the total angular momentum 

j [12], and this fine structure is confirmed experimentally up to the radiative (Lamb-shift) corrections. A third description is the eight-component relativis-tic wave equation for spin- 12 particles of Robson and Staudte [13, 14]. It is obtained from the second-order (Feynman–Gell-Mann) equation [15] by the Feshbach– Villars linearization of the time derivative [16], and we denote it by FV 12 . It reproduces the Dirac hydrogenic spectrum exactly, although with different state func-tions [13, 14]. It yields the Klein–Nishina cross sec-

Second-order and pseudo-Hermitian formulations of spin- 12 dynamics, and Feshbach–Villars formalisms in general, continue to attract attention [22, 23], as does the wider physics of pseudo-Hermitian and non-Hermitian operators [24–26]. The Stark effect [27], the shift of the levels in a static uniform electric field, is a natural probe of such a formal-ism. Its non-relativistic theory is a classic application of stationary perturbation theory [11]; its relativistic the-ory is well developed, from closed-form polarizabilities based on the Sturmian expansion of the Dirac–Coulomb Green function [28–30] to finite-basis calculations [31] and complex-scaling calculations of the Stark energies and widths of hydrogen-like ions [32]. The relativistic 2s–2 p couplings also provide the Stark input of the field-quenching and anisotropy determinations of the 2 s Lamb shift [33–37]. The structure of the problem differs in-structively between the theories. In the Schr¨ odinger the-ory the whole n = 2 shell is degenerate and the field arXiv:2610.02060v1 [physics.atom-ph] 1 Oct 2026 2produces a linear shift. In the Dirac and FV 12 theories the fine structure splits n = 2 into a j = 12 level, spanned by 2 s1/2 and 2 p1/2, and a j = 32 level; the residual 2 s1/2–2p1/2 degeneracy still gives a linear shift, whereas the 

j = 32 level responds only at second order. The first two orders therefore exercise both the degenerate and the nondegenerate branches of perturbation theory, and both the discrete and the continuum parts of the spectrum. What such a calculation can and cannot test should be stated at the outset. The FV 12 equation is in one-to-one correspondence with the second-order equation, whose solution space in any external field is spanned by solutions of the Dirac equations with masses ±m, which have the same spectrum [14]. The exact FV 12 and Dirac energies in the potential −Z/r + E0z therefore coincide, the exact Stark shifts of the two theories are equal, and any discrepancy would signal an error in the perturbative treatment. What is not guaranteed is that the perturba-tion formalism of the FV 12 theory—indefinite metric, de-generate manifolds of mixed norm sign, biorthogonal left vectors, and an external field that enters twice, through the scalar potential and through an explicit spin–field coupling ⃗σ ·⃗ E that has no Dirac counterpart—delivers these shifts order by order, and it is not known in advance which of its ingredients are indispensable. These are the questions addressed here; it is a stronger test of the for-malism than the agreement of the unperturbed spectra, because it involves matrix elements, spectral sums and norm signs. The comparison also yields results of independent in-terest. We obtain all first-order roots for n ≤ 3 in closed form, show by a second-moment sum rule how the lin-ear Stark strength of the non-relativistic shell survives in the relativistic theories although the relativistic roots are smaller by finite factors, separate every second-order shift exactly into a part internal to the shell and a regular remainder, which fixes much of its Zα expansion analyti-cally, and show how a level shift that lifts the 2 s1/2–2 p1/2

degeneracy must enter the FV 12 theory for it to supply the couplings used in the field-quenching and anisotropy measurements [33–37]. Section II collects the perturbation formulas for positive-definite and indefinite inner products. Sec-tions III, IV and V treat Schr¨ odinger, Dirac and FV 12

theories, respectively, for the n = 1, 2 and 3 levels; Sec. V F treats split levels in the FV 12 theory. Section VI compares the results: it derives the closed forms and a second-moment sum rule, decomposes the second-order shifts, locates the weak-field/strong-field crossover, de-limits the range of validity of the expansion and examines what the Dirac–FV 12 agreement establishes. Section VII concludes. Atomic units ( me = e = ℏ = 4 πε 0 = 1, 

c = 1 /α ) are used throughout; the nucleus is treated as an infinitely heavy point charge Z.

C. The Stark perturbation 

For a static, uniform field along z,⃗ E = E0 ˆez , the elec-tron acquires the potential energy E0z, and the pertur-bation Hamiltonian is given by ˆHI = E0 r cos θ. (12) Equation (12) is in the Schr¨ odinger and Dirac theories, in which a static external field enters the Hamiltonian only through the scalar potential. In the FV 12 theory, the field also enters through the spin term of the Feynman–Gell-Mann equation, which survives the Feshbach–Villars lin-earization as an explicit ⃗σ ·⃗ E coupling (Sec. V A). Three remarks fix the regime. (i) ˆHI is odd under parity; in the Schr¨ odinger and Dirac theories it is spin independent and conserves m (respectively mj ). (ii) Because ˆHI is unbounded below, the bound states become resonances whose widths are nonperturbative in E0 [32]; the pertur-bation series is asymptotic, and for the fields considered here the widths are negligible. (iii) The expansion re-quires |E(2) | ≪ | E(1) | whenever E(1) ̸ = 0. In the rela-tivistic theories the second order is enhanced by ( Zα )−2

whenever the level has a fine-structure partner, which makes this condition restrictive for light ions (Sec. VI F).

D. The role of the continuum 

Table I compares the exact corrections with the sum over bound intermediate states truncated at nmax . The truncated sum converges, but to the total bound-state contribution, which is smaller in modulus than the exact value; the difference is the continuum contribution, which no increase of nmax can recover [42]. For the ground state the whole discrete spectrum supplies 81 .4% of αd, the continuum supplying the remaining 18 .6%. The deficit decreases with n, because the dominant intermediate states then lie closer to the perturbed level, but it re-mains at the percent level at n = 3. At nmax = 3 the sums for n = 3 have the wrong sign or vanish, because only the lower-lying n = 1 and 2 levels, which contribute positively, are then included. A second-order Stark cal-culation restricted to bound intermediate states would therefore be quantitatively wrong, and the Dalgarno– Lewis construction is used throughout.

The FV 12 first- and second-order corrections for n = 1, 2, 3 and all six ions are those listed in Tables II and III. At first order the FV 12 and Dirac roots, both obtained from exact Γ-function matrix elements, agree to all 40 or more digits retained at every level and every Z, including

> VI. COMPARISON OF THE THREE THEORIES

The Dirac and FV 12 shifts coincide at every level and every order computed (Sec. V D), so this section com-pares two descriptions (the nonrelativistic one and the relativistic one shared by the Dirac and FV 12 theories) and returns in Sec. VI G to what the coincidence estab-lishes about the FV 12 formalism. Two results are added 12 to those of Secs. III–V: closed forms of all first-order roots, and an exact decomposition of every second-order shift into a part internal to the shell and a part due to all other states. Together they determine part of Table II an-alytically: the first-order roots, the singular coefficients

> A. Why the theories differ: the degenerate manifolds

The 2 n2-fold degeneracy of the Schr¨ odinger shell re-flects the conservation of the Runge–Lenz vector in the Coulomb field [11]; the electric field mixes all values of 

l in the shell, and the parabolic quantum numbers diag-onalize the first-order problem. Relativity removes this symmetry. What survives in the Dirac-Coulomb prob-lem is the degeneracy of κ and −κ at fixed n and j, as-sociated with the Johnson–Lippmann operator [45, 46], which commutes with the Dirac-Coulomb Hamiltonian and connects κ with −κ; at fixed mj the field therefore acts in manifolds of dimension one or two. The FV 12

theory has the same levels, each carried twice with oppo-site τ5 norm (44), and the roots of its first-order problem are the Dirac roots (Sec. V B). As far as the Stark ef-fect is concerned, the three theories therefore fall into two classes, nonrelativistic and relativistic, and the lim-its Zα → 0 at fixed E0 and E0 → 0 at fixed Zα do not commute: whenever a level has a fine-structure partner, the relativistic first-order roots at Zα → 0 differ from the Schr¨ odinger roots and the relativistic second-order shift diverges as ( Zα )−2. Sections VI B–VI D make this statement quantitative.

c. Necessary ingredients and a computational advan-tage. What makes the agreement informative is the list of ingredients that had to work together: an eight-component solution space, an indefinite metric, a per-turbation that differs from the Dirac one, secular prob-lems of mixed norm sign, biorthogonal left vectors, and negative-norm partners entering with ϵk = −1(Secs. formulation also has a practical merit, inherited from the second-order form of the theory. The Dirac Dalgarno– Lewis operator is unbounded below and is inverted in-side the spectral gap, where Galerkin approximations can produce spurious solutions (spectral pollution [50]) unless the basis satisfies a balance condition [43, 44]. Sec. IV shows that the usual (kinetic-balance) choice is not ade-quate here. The FV 12 equation (49) is of Schr¨ odinger type and bounded below, since ¯ γ′(¯ γ′ +1) = (¯ γ′ + 12 )2 − 14 ≥ − 14

(in the s channels, where ¯ γ′ < 0, the basis r¯γ′+pe−Λr se-lects the Friedrichs extension); it is solved below its es-sential spectrum, where the min–max principle excludes spurious eigenvalues, and a single set of radial functions, without any balance condition, converges exponentially in every channel. 19 

d. What the agreement does not establish. Since the exact FV 12 and Dirac Stark energies must coincide (Sec. I), the calculation cannot discriminate between the two theories: it validates the perturbative formalism of the FV 12 theory and identifies the ingredients it requires. Nor is it a comparison with experiment: within the Dirac model the relativistic linear Stark effect of hydrogen is confined to fields below about 0 .1 kV cm −1 (Table VI), where the shift is of order 10 −8 a.u. and is dominated by the effect of the Lamb shift. What experiment does use are the couplings v1 and v3 of the quench analyses (Sec. V F); the relativistic reduction of v21 by Eq. (54), 1.4% at Z = 18, was included in the analysis of the Ar 17+ measurement [33, 34]. The results do bear on radiative transitions. In the long-wavelength limit and in the length gauge, an E1 rate is fixed by the tran-sition energy and by the coupling of the two levels by a uniform electric field, the latter being the quantity compared above; if the FV 12 rate is defined consistently, through the biorthogonal products combined over the states of both levels, the present results imply that it equals the Dirac rate for the transitions examined here. Robson and Sutanto [18] reported substantial differences between the eight-component and Dirac rates at large 

Z: for the 2 P3/2–1 S1/2 transition of U 91+ , 2 .035 × 10 17 

versus 1 .580 × 10 17 s−1. By contrast, the contribution of the 2 p3/2 level to the static shift of the ground state is the same in the two theories to all figures computed (−0.541 572 791 89 in units of E20 /Z 4, Table VIII). Their rates were obtained in the Coulomb gauge, with the full multipole expansion of the photon field, from the squared modulus of the τ5 matrix element of the interaction. For this transition each level is the only member of its man-ifold, so the mixing of κ-labeled states that accompa-nies split levels (Sec. Whether the reported difference originates in the velocity form of the coupling, in retardation or in the definition of the rate in that gauge is an open question that the for-malism used here is suited to settle. A related consistency check exists for scattering: the eight-component theory reproduces the Klein–Nishina cross section for Compton scattering [17].  

> VII. CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK

We have computed the first- and second-order Stark shifts, and their expansions in Zα , of the n = 1, 2 and 3 levels of six hydrogenic ions from H to U 91+ in the Schr¨ odinger, Dirac and FV 12 theories, with all spectral sums evaluated exactly by the Dalgarno–Lewis method. We have then compared the three descriptions level by level. Our conclusions are as follows. 1. The FV 12 formalism reproduces the Dirac Stark shifts through second order for n ≤ 3. The first-order roots coincide with the closed forms (54) and the second-order shifts agree with the Dirac values to all 45 significant figures retained, for every level and every ion, with vanishing imaginary parts. The agreement also holds for the contribution of every bound intermediate level tested ( n′ ≤ 5), though not for individual states: the two theories share the level-to-level couplings of the field, while their state functions differ. 2. Both ingredients tested, the ⃗σ ·⃗ E coupling and the negative-norm partners, are necessary. Each de-generate FV 12 manifold contains states of both τ5

norms, so the reality of the FV 12 Stark spectrum is a result rather than a premise [Eq. (9)]. Without the ⃗σ ·⃗ E coupling every first-order root acquires an imaginary part of order αE 0; without the negative-norm partners the roots are real but have the wrong (Zα )2 coefficient. 3. Split levels need the same care. Norm sign and par-ity are locked in the doubled space [Eq. (50)], so a change of the electrostatic potential that lifts the 2s1/2–2 p1/2 degeneracy must enter through its FV 12

image, spin–gradient term included [Eq. (52)]; the resulting operator is then diagonal in fixed combi-nations ts, tp of the κ-labeled states [Eq. (53)], and the FV 12 theory supplies the Dirac couplings v1 and 

v3 on which field-quenching and anisotropy mea-surements of the Lamb shift rely. If placed by hand on the κ-labeled states, the level shift would pro-duce spurious imaginary linear Stark term ± 32 iαE 0.The closed form (54) of v1 reproduces the numerical results of Hillery and Mohr [33]. 4. The relativistic and nonrelativistic linear effects dif-fer, but the linear strength is conserved. The rel-ativistic roots are smaller by finite factors ( √3at n = 2) and form a different pattern, because relativity replaces the Runge–Lenz degeneracy of the shell with the degeneracy of ±κ; the missing strength is carried by the singular ( Zα )−2 terms of the second order, as the sum rules (33) and (57) show. The two regimes are the limits of a nearly universal crossover in E0/(Z∆2), whose midpoint lies near 2 .8 kV cm −1 for hydrogen within the Dirac model and beyond the field-ionization threshold for U91+ .5. The second-order shifts have a transparent analytic structure. For levels without a fine-structure part-ner the relativistic correction stiffens the atom, by 44% for the ground state of U 91+ but only 13% and 6% for the nodeless 2 p3/2 and 3 d5/2 states. For the ground state, our values agree with the closed form of Szmytkowski [28] to 45 significant figures. For the other levels the shift separates exactly into an intra-shell part, known in closed form and contain-ing all singular terms, and a regular part; the de-composition derives the ( Zα )0 coefficient of these 20 levels analytically, places the π2 terms of the ( Zα )2

coefficients in the intra-shell part (the remainder being rational to the 26 figures of the identifica-tion), and gives their coefficients in terms of the singular ones [Eq. (63)]. 6. The continuum cannot be omitted. Nonrelativisti-cally, the discrete spectrum supplies 81 .4% of the ground-state polarizability and between 97 .6% and 99 .1% of the n = 3 shifts, so that a bound-state-only calculation would be wrong at the 19% level at n = 1. 7. The perturbative window set by the fine structure widens as Z5 (Table VII); for the heaviest ions field ionization is the limit. Numerically, the Dirac Galerkin basis must respect the small-r behavior of both components (Sec. IV), whereas the scalar FV 12 equation needs no balance condition. Taken together, these results answer the question posed in the introduction. Within the scope tested— first and second order, n ≤ 3, Z ≤ 92—the FV 12 the-ory gives a consistent description of a spin- 12 particle in a static electric field, assuming the completeness of its biorthogonal set of states: its doubled solution space, indefinite metric and biorthogonal structure are the ele-ments through which it reproduces the Dirac response, order by order and level by level. Where the two theories may still appear to differ is in quantities whose definition goes beyond the levels and their couplings. Robson and Sutanto [18] reported radiative transition probabilities that differ from the Dirac ones; the present results imply that, in the long-wavelength limit of the length gauge and with the rates defined through biorthogonal prod-ucts, they coincide for the transitions examined here, so that the origin of the reported difference remains to be identified. Closed-form first-order roots, Zα expansions of all second-order shifts through ( Zα )2 (and ( Zα )4 for the levels without a partner) and the sum rules connect-ing them to the nonrelativistic results are provided as benchmarks. Radiative (QED) corrections, the finite nu-clear size, and the nuclear recoil are not included in the Stark shifts computed here; model level shifts serve to show how such corrections must enter the FV 12 formal-ism. A natural next step is the Zeeman effect. A uniform magnetic field is even under parity, and in Eq. (35) the magnetic spin term enters both blocks with the same sign and a real coefficient, unlike the electric term. The ex-act answer is again fixed—for the ground state the Breit value g1s = 23

1+2 p1 − (Zα )2 [51]—so the Zeeman cal-culation would test a different part of the same formalism (the minimal-coupling term of D2 and the mixed-norm secular problem for a time-odd perturbation) in a sys-tem whose bound-electron g factor is measured with ex-traordinary precision [10, 52]. A second natural step is to compute FV 12 radiative transition rates in the length and velocity gauges, with and without retardation, and so settle the question raised in Sec. VI G.
