# A Monte Carlo Estimator for an Isolated Polynomial Zero via Contour Integral Representations 

## Athanasios C. Micheas ∗

## October 2, 2026 

Abstract 

We introduce a Monte Carlo approach for estimating isolated polynomial zeros through their contour integral representations. For a simple zero enclosed by an iso-lating contour, we show that the zero can be expressed exactly as the expectation of a complex-valued random variable obtained by uniformly sampling the contour. This yields an unbiased estimator without perturbing the polynomial coefficients. We estab-lish its variance and derive finite sample probabilistic error bounds based on concentra-tion inequalities, with the bounds explicitly reflecting the geometry of the contour and its separation from the zeros. The framework also provides a stochastic formulation of contour based root counting and root isolation. Numerical results illustrate the estima-tor’s behavior under varying polynomial degrees, and we further provide comparisons to existing methods. 

Keywords : Argument Principle, Cauchy Integral, Contour Integration, Hoeffding Error Bounds, Monte Carlo Integration, Monte Carlo Simulation, Polynomial Roots 

MSC Classification : Primary: 65C05, 65H04, Secondary: 30E20, 41A60.

In particular, the central idea is to combine the classical Cauchy contour representation of an isolated simple root with randomized quadra-ture, yielding an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator for an individual root. The construction is complemented by probabilistic error bounds and a Monte Carlo formulation of the argument principle for root counting. For some recent applications of Monte Carlo methods see [8], [7], [1] and [14]. To the best of our knowledge, the Cauchy contour representation of an isolated polynomial zero has not previously been formulated explicitly as an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator of the individual zero, with the randomness introduced through contour sampling rather than through perturbation of the polynomial coefficients. The proposed representation provides a direct stochastic function of the polynomial coefficients 

rn ≈ bFn(a0, . . . , a N ), (5) where bFn is a Monte Carlo estimator of the root as a function of the coefficients. In addition, we will obtain finite sample probabilistic error bounds in terms of the geometry of the isolating contour. The construction is complementary to existing polynomial root isolation and randomized root finding algorithms; it does not replace the isolation problem, but provides a statistically interpretable randomized approximation once an isolating contour is available. Specifically, [16] develops randomized root finding algorithms with nearly optimal com-plexity for the zeros of a univariate polynomial. The algorithms can work either from coeffi-cients or from a black-box evaluation oracle and achieve nearly optimal complexity. There is, however, a technical distinction: the algorithms in [16] are Las Vegas randomized algorithms, 2rather than Monte Carlo estimators in the statistical sense. A Las Vegas algorithm uses ran-domness but guarantees correctness (with randomness affecting running time), whereas the Monte Carlo algorithm permits a small probability of an incorrect answer. In [17], the authors investigate polynomial root finding through randomized matrix and polynomial iterations. On the other hand, there is work that studies polynomials whose coefficients are random. For example, [20] develops a Monte Carlo simulation algorithm for studying the distribution of the number of zeros of random polynomials in small intervals. For a review of recent work, along with precise asymptotics of the variance of the number of real roots for a large class of random polynomials see [6] and the references therein. While contour integration, Cauchy’s formula, the residue theorem and the argument principle or randomized polynomial root finding are well studied areas of mathematics, in this paper, we propose the explicit combination of these ingredients in a probabilistic framework in order to: 

• propose an alternative construction from the standard randomized root finding algo-rithms and standard Monte Carlo procedures that propagate uncertainty in random polynomial coefficients, i.e., the polynomial is kept deterministic and randomness is neatly introduced at the contour integration representation of an isolated polynomial root, 

• provide a Monte Carlo representation of an isolated polynomial root as the expectation of a contour sampling random variable, 

• obtain explicit finite sample probabilistic bounds for root estimation based on the contour geometry and the polynomial coefficients, 

• give a Monte Carlo formulation of the argument principle for estimating the number of roots inside a contour, 

• obtain a statistical distribution for the number of roots and the root value inside an isolating contour, and 

• propose a Monte Carlo based algorithm for a probabilistically controlled root isolation. The paper proceeds as follows; in the next section we obtain a form of any simple root of a polynomial in terms of a contour integral and utilize Monte Carlo to obtain an unbiased estimator of rn. We further derive Hoeffding bounds that connect the numerical approxima-tion error to contour geometry, along with a combined isolation and root finding algorithm. In Section 3, we utilize the results to perform a simulation study and comparison with other methods. Concluding remarks are given in the last section.

## 2.3 Choice of circles and root isolation 

The results in the preceding sections assume that isolating contours are available, and therefore the proposed root (and number of roots) approximation depends on the choice of the circle centers cn ∈ C and radii ρn > 0, for all n = 1 , . . . , N. This assumption should not be confused with the problem of finding the roots themselves; however, if no isolating contours are known, an additional root isolation procedure is required. This is a central problem of numerical algebra where we need to determine the locations of all the zeros and, in particular, isolate them, i.e., each disc contains exactly one zero, or when roots are too close to separate reliably in order to obtain a well separated cluster of zeros. The fundamental idea here is to first determine how many zeros lie in a disc, and then subdivide the disc until it contains exactly one zero, or until a well separated root cluster has been identified. A general strategy is to use the argument principle to count the number of roots inside candidate regions. This point of view is closely associated with the classic contour methods of [5] and [12], and has subsequently been incorporated into modern subdivision and certified polynomial root finding algorithms. There are different methods that use classic results such as Pel-let’s theorem (see [18]), which is especially useful for root isolation because it can provide information about both a disc and a surrounding root free annulus. 11 A modification of the R function polyroot can be used to quickly assess if a disc contains a root (this R function implements the Jenkins and Traub method, see [11]). A recent implementation combining such methods in the literature, that provides pseudo-code for all operations, can be found in [2] (in particular, see the CISOLATE algorithm). The root isolation problem can be delegated to these certified algorithms based on subdivision, Pellet-type tests, and argument principle methods (libraries exist written in C++, e.g., MPSolve). In addition, libraries that return pairwise disjoint complex intervals, each containing exactly one root (with multiplicities), like SageMath, require strong assumptions on the coefficients, e.g., the coefficient support is integer, or rational, or Gaussian-rational, or algebraic, i.e., not arbitrary complex coefficients. After an extensive search, the reader will be surprised to find out that there is no R package to perform Pellet-type isolation for polynomial roots. Therefore, we have imple-mented both Pellet’s method for counting roots in discs, as well as a novel probabilistic subdivision method, using R code. The general isolation strategy using discs is summarized in the following algorithm, where we used R code and RCPP for its implementation, and the Monte Carlo estimators of equations (19), (20), (24) and (25). The R and C++ code for this implementation along with all reproducible simulations presented below, are included in the supplementary material. We call the new method MCIsolate.

## 2.5 Method sensitivity and potential problems 

All isolation methods in the literature show similar sensitivities and limitations (see [2], Section 1.2, for an excellent discussion and references to related literature), and the same is true for the proposed Monte Carlo isolation method MCIsolate. We summarize some of these potential problems next, and how they can be addressed. The method proposed is theoretically sensitive to: contours close to polynomial roots, closely clustered roots, high polynomial degree, and poorly chosen or weakly separated isolating contours. In particular, the MCIsolate method heavily depends on having an isolating contour already available for the cleanest root estimation result. The proposed subdivision algorithm addresses this issue, but its finite sample reliability depends on the accuracy of the Monte Carlo root counts; we solved this issue via probabilistic approaches which is one of the novelties of MCIsolate (see Algorithm 3, equations (46) and (47)). Other limitations include that the main unbiased estimator applies directly only to simple roots; multiple root estimation uses a ratio of estimators which is generally biased; the worst case concentration bounds may be extremely conservative; numerical evaluation becomes unstable when PN (z) is small on the contour; and the contour points must avoid roots, since 

P ′ 

> N

(z)/P N (z) is undefined on a contour passing through a root. The MCIsolate algorithm alleviates these issues by construction, and borrows strength from the Monte Carlo frame-work, allowing us to obtain a distribution of these important quantities, that can then be used to quantify uncertainty in a probabilistic framework. Finally, depending on the tolerance τ used to identify zeros, e.g., a point z is a zero if 

|PN (z)| < τ , all such algorithms may miss a root or add additional roots in the final set of roots. # 4 Concluding Remarks 

The construction herein should be viewed as a Monte Carlo contour integration estimator for an isolated root, rather than as a replacement for all existing randomized polynomial root isolation algorithms. Our main contribution is that the CMIsolate algorithm provides an explicit statistical Monte Carlo formulation of the contour integral representation of an isolated root, and in addition, the resulting unbiased estimator. In particular, the principal reported results are theoretical where we presented an ex-act expectation representation for every isolated simple root; an unbiased sample average estimator; an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator and formulation of the argument principle 19 for estimating the number of roots inside a contour; explicit variance and Hoeffding error bounds based on the contour geometry and the polynomial coefficients; simultaneous confi-dence guarantees for all roots; a geometry dependent bound on the estimator magnitude; a recursive Monte Carlo root isolation strategy, and an extension formula for roots with mul-tiplicity. In terms of our computational framework, we provided an algorithm for potential combination of root isolation followed by Monte Carlo contour approximation. The methods presented here can be utilized for finding the zeros of any function via piecewise polynomial approximation, where the polynomial roots approximate the roots of the original function. The function needs to satisfy certain smoothness conditions in this case, e.g.,continuous. These results are of great interest and will be presented elsewhere.
