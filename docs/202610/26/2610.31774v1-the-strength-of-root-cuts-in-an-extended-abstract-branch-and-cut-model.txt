# The Strength of Root Cuts in an Extended Abstract Branch-and-Cut Model ∗Boyang Han Aleksandr M. Kazachkov 

Abstract 

The branch-and-cut paradigm has led to effective solvers for a wide range of mathematical optimization problems. Despite significant theoretical work on pure branching and pure cutting, less is known about their interaction. We study an extended abstract branch-and-cut model to investigate tradeoffs between branching and cutting, including the time to generate cuts. We prove that, under symmetric branching, some minimal-time tree has all cuts at the root, before the first branching node, and that there is an asymmetric setting where some minimal-time tree has no internal cuts, i.e., cuts both preceded and followed by branching. For other settings, although a root-cut tree may be suboptimal and differ substantially in structure from a minimal-time tree, it nevertheless provides a reliable approximation whose tree time does not deviate arbitrarily from the optimum. For constant cut strength and bounded time functions, we show that the best root-cut tree is asymptotically optimal as the target bound grows. In addition, under a single-cut-per-path restriction, we characterize when some minimal-time tree has an internal cut. Lastly, simulations indicate that the best root-cut tree is much closer to a minimal-time tree than our worst-case approximation guarantee suggests. 

# 1 Introduction 

Branch-and-cut (B&C), a hybrid version of the branch-and-bound (B&B) [17] and cutting plane (CP) methods [10–12], is a powerful tree-based framework that is a key ingredient of mathematical optimization solvers. Each node of the tree corresponds to a relaxation of the original optimization problem that is refined by either valid inequalities ( cuts ) or splitting the search space ( branching ). Solvers determine where in the tree to add rounds of cuts from empirical guidelines and typically focus on the root node of the tree. In this paper, we provide complementary theory on cut placement with provable guarantees on the computational cost of the resulting tree, within an extension of the 

abstract B&C model by Kazachkov, Le Bodic, and Sankaranarayanan [15, 16]. Our model assumes that key solution parameters are known: the bound that needs to be proved (e.g., an optimal solution is given, but we need to certify optimality via the tree), the evolution of the bound over iterated branching and cutting operations, and an estimated time to solve each node of the tree. Our perspective (when such information may be available) is a post-optimality analysis of the quality of the best-possible B&C tree for a problem, which can provide beneficial insights when solving the same problem repeatedly. In the original abstract B&C model [16], branching yields fixed (constant) bound improvements, while cuts may have diminishing strength across rounds or increase node processing time. Under symmetric branching and constant cut strength, they prove that a root-cut tree , where all cut nodes are before the first branch node, is sufficient: there always exists a minimal-time root-cut tree, for any function defining node solving time [16, Theorem 23].

Even when the root-cut tree is suboptimal, i.e., when any minimal-time tree requires local cuts (deeper in the tree), we derive an upper bound on how much its processing time can deviate from the minimal-time tree, potentially offering guidance (or validation) for the root-cut dominant approach of solvers. Our contributions are summarized next. 

▷ Section 3.1: We prove that, under symmetric branching, a minimal-time root-cut tree always ex-ists, even when simultaneously considering multiple cut and branching families, or node-dependent cut generation times. 

▷ Section 3.2: We prescribe how to best add a sequence of weak and strong cuts, when the weak cuts cause a relatively smaller increase in node solving time. 

▷ Section 3.3: We identify an asymmetric branching setting where some minimal-time tree has no internal cuts, i.e., all cuts are at the root or at the leaves. 

▷ Section 4.1: We bound the approximation error of the best root-cut tree with respect to the minimal-time tree. 

▷ Section 4.2: We analyze bounded time functions, for which the best root-cut tree is within a constant factor of a minimal-time tree, and for constant cuts, it is asymptotically optimal. 

▷ Section 5.1: We show by example that the minimal-time tree can be structurally complex for asymmetric settings: a local cut may appear at any depth. 

▷ Section 5.2: We characterize conditions that allow the construction of minimal-time trees featuring local cuts under a single-cut-per-path assumption. 

▷ Section 5.3: We study how cut generation time impacts number of cut nodes in a minimal-time tree, proving monotonicity for constant separation times. 

▷ Section 5.4: We empirically assess the quality of the root-cut approximation. 

Related literature. Recent theory has been developed to analyze the strength of the pure B&B process [8, 5, 9, 18], pure CP [6], and comparing B&C, B&B, and CP [1, 2]. Basu, Conforti, Di Summa, and Jiang [1] show that B&C can be strictly superior to B&B and CP. In contrast, and counterintuitively, Shah, Dey, and Molinaro [21] prove that a single cut can make B&B exponentially worse. Related to our work, Jiang [14] identifies—within our model—the fraction of the target bound proved by cut nodes in a minimal-size B&C tree, as a function of how cumulative cut strength grows. Other papers adopt a learning-based perspective: Berthold, Francobaldi, and Hendel [3] propose methods for learning to use local cuts effectively within B&C; Cheng and Basu [4] establish generalization guarantees for robustness of learned B&C policies across problem instances. However, in all this research, the explicit tradeoffs and interplay between branching and cutting decisions remain poorly understood. Our high-level motivation is to systematically study the interaction between branching and cutting within a B&C framework in a stylized (but already nontrivial to analyze) model, through which we can progress towards theoretical guidance for cut placement and selection in solvers. 

# 2 Preliminaries: Notation and Definitions 

Abstractly, B&C involves solving a sequence of tightened subproblems in a tree structure. For exam-ple, in mixed-integer optimization, the B&C process starts from the root node , a convex relaxation of the original problem. The optimal value at the root node is a dual bound for the original problem. Then, the problem formulation (and dual bound) is strengthened by either: (1) partitioning the feasible space and creating two subproblems, or (2) adding a round of valid cuts that are violated by points in the current relaxation but satisfied by all feasible mixed-integer solutions. The B&C tree 2is built by recursively selecting between these two operations until the tree proves a (dual) bound 

matching the primal optimal value (we assume this is given as Z). We largely adopt the notions modeling a B&C tree and its processing time from Kazachkov, Le Bodic, and Sankaranarayanan [16, Definitions 1–5]. The authors define a time function [16, Definition 2] to model that cuts make formulations more complex, thus increasing the processing time of subsequent subproblems. In practice, solvers can choose among different families of cuts 

that have heterogeneous properties, and cut generation time also affects cut selection. We introduce two extended models of solving time: a simple time function (Definition 1) in which solving time of a node in the tree only depends on the number of cuts previously generated from each family, and a generalized time function (Definition 3) that also accounts for the time to compute cuts. We will assume that no cut family strictly dominates another (i.e., when a cut family yields a better bound improvement, it causes slower relaxations). Let VT denote the nodes of a B&C tree T . # 3 When Root Cuts Are (Nearly) Sufficient 

In this section, we identify settings, both symmetric (Section 3.1) and asymmetric (Section 3.3), in which there is a minimal-time tree without internal cuts, i.e., in which all cuts are at the root or at the leaves. We also show (Section 3.2) how to best order a sequence of weak and strong cuts, when weak cuts cause a comparatively smaller increase in node solving time.

3.3 Root-Cut Trees Under Asymmetric Branching 

In Section 3.1, we showed that it is sufficient to consider root cuts in symmetric branching settings with multiple cut families. We now turn to the single cut-family, single branching-family B&C model with constant but nonidentical bound improvements ( ℓ̸ = r), under a simple time function. By Lemma 4, symmetry in a tree shifts cut nodes upward toward the root. Consequently, a natural question is whether symmetry in the tree is necessary, i.e., might one always find, under an asymmetric branching regime, a combination of target bound and time function that forces the inclusion of local cuts within any minimal-time tree. We prove that this is not universally true: there exists an asymmetric setting ( c : ℓ : r in ratio 1 : 2 : 4 ) in which a minimal-time tree without any internal cuts always exists, regardless of the specific form of the (simple) time function. Proving this involves several intermediate results that we present first. Define a branch-then-bothcut node to be a branch node with both children being cut nodes from the same family. The following corollary, which follows directly from Lemma 4, states that a branch-then-bothcut node cannot exist for a single cut family setting. 2

5.2 Adding At Most One Cut Node Per Root-To-Leaf Path 

Typically, solvers limit the number of cut rounds, as successive rounds often yield diminishing bound improvements [7] while substantially increasing problem size and solution time. Moreover, excessive cuts can harm numerical stability and lead to ill-conditioned subproblems that degrade solver performance [22, 20]. In this subsection, we investigate necessary and sufficient conditions under which some minimal-time tree has an internal cut. We restrict our focus to the one-cut 

setting, defined as a configuration where at most one round of cuts is added in each root-leaf path. This assumption simplifies the analysis while preserving key structural insights. Note that if w(1) > 2 under the one-cut setting, no cut nodes would appear in any minimal-time tree, as branch nodes provide both better bound improvements and smaller processing time when w(1) is large. Under this assumption, a branch node in a minimal-time tree cannot have two cut node children. A curious consequence of Example 3 is that increasing w(1) can lead to a minimal-time tree having strictly more root cuts. The underlying reason is that, in our model, cuts affect the time of all nodes below them, so increasing w(1) penalizes having many nodes with exactly one cut above them. # 6 Conclusions 

This paper highlights the structural importance of root cuts within our abstract B&C framework. In particular, we identify settings, both symmetric and asymmetric, in which some minimal-time tree has no internal cuts, even when accounting for cut generation time. More generally, we bound how much the best root-cut tree can deviate from a minimal-time tree, and we prove that, for constant cuts, the best root-cut tree is asymptotically optimal. In addition, we characterize when internal cuts are necessary in a one-cut setting, and we show that cut generation time can move cuts either toward or away from the root. While this model captures a phenomenon commonly observed in state-of-the-art solvers, that cuts are used aggressively at the root, it intentionally abstracts away complex solver interactions. Specifically, cutting and branching interact solely through bound improvement and the slowdown in node processing time, leaving out other mechanisms such as pruning, restarts, primal heuris-tics, and the sequence of fractional solutions that a solver encounters. Consequently, preliminary computational experiments evaluating cut placement only exhibit noise-level differences in perfor-mance. This motivates generalizations of the current model. For example, as branching decisions can be significantly affected by cuts in practice, a natural extension is to incorporate cut-dependent branching decisions.
