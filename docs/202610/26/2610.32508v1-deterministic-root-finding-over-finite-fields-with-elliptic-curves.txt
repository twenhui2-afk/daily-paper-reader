# Deterministic Root-Finding over Finite Fields with Elliptic Curves 

Pierre-Jean Spaenlehauer Université de Lorraine, CNRS, Inria 

Abstract 

Finding the roots of a polynomial over a finite field Fq in deterministic polynomial time is a long-standing open problem. Classical deterministic methods work in the multiplicative group 

F× 

> q

and are efficient only when q − 1 is smooth, which is rarely the case. We propose a method that replaces F× 

> q

by the groups of rational points of an elliptic curve 

E and of its quadratic twist ˜E. The computation splits into an offline phase, which depends only on Fq and outputs a short elliptic preprocessing datum D, and an online phase, which processes the input polynomial. Writing Ξ( D) for the largest prime factor of |E(Fq )| · | ˜E(Fq )|,the online phase is deterministic and finds the roots of any f ∈ Fq [X] of degree n within 

˜O(Ξ( D)2 n log 2 q+nω log q+n log 3 q) operations in Fq . A precursor of our method is an algorithm by Rónyai, which recovers the roots through Galois computations over number fields, relies on GRH, and comes with a polynomial bound of unspecified degree; in contrast, our online phase works entirely over Fq and is unconditional. Moreover, D can be certified deterministically and unconditionally in time polynomial in log q and Ξ( D). We conjecture that every Fq of large enough characteristic admits a datum with Ξ( D) ≤

(log q)O(1) , and support this with a heuristic and with experiments. Constructing such a datum in polynomial time remains open. arXiv:2610.32508v1 [cs.SC] 23 Oct 2026 Contents

1.1 Problem statement 

Problem statement. Let q = pe be a prime power with p > 3 (for p ≤ 3, Berlekamp’s algo-rithm [Ber70] is already deterministic and polynomial-time), and Fq denote the finite field with q

elements. A univariate polynomial f (X) ∈ Fq[X] is separable if it is coprime with its derivative, and split if all its irreducible factors have degree 1. In this paper, we study deterministic algorithms for the following problem: 

Problem 1.1 (Root-finding over Fq). Given a prime power q and a split, separable polynomial 

f (X) ∈ Fq[X], compute the set of roots of f in Fq, i.e., the set of x ∈ Fq such that f (x) = 0 .

This problem lies at the core of polynomial factorization over Fq: indeed, factorization can be re-duced to root-finding in deterministic polynomial time via squarefree factorization and Berlekamp’s subalgebra (see [Ber70] and [vzGG03, Sec. 14]). 

Motivation. Root-finding over finite fields is a central problem in algebraic computation. Al-though efficient randomized algorithms have been known for decades, no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm has been found (see however the recent claim discussed at the end of Section 1.3). Finding one would constitute a step toward the broader goal of derandomizing algebraic algorithms. Root-finding and polynomial factorization over finite fields are subroutines of many algorithms in computational algebra. The absence of a deterministic polynomial-time solution propagates to higher-level algorithms. A telling example is the algorithmic structure theory of finite-dimensional associative algebras over Fq: finding zero divisors, decomposing a semisimple algebra into sim-ple components, exhibiting an explicit isomorphism between a simple algebra and a full matrix algebra, or computing a common invariant subspace of a family of linear maps are all solved by 

f -algorithms , i.e. deterministic polynomial-time algorithms with access to an oracle for factoring univariate polynomials over finite fields [FR85, Rón90, Ebe91]. Factorization is itself a special case of the zero-divisor problem [Rón87]. These problems are therefore exactly as hard as Problem 1.1. Deterministic algorithms can also be used in cryptography, where Fq is sometimes fixed by a standard, so that a field-dependent precomputation can be performed. For instance, constant-time hashing into elliptic curves extracts square roots by a deterministic variant of the Tonelli–Shanks algorithm, which relies on a nonsquare of Fq fixed in advance [FHSS +23, App. I.4]; our preprocessing datum plays a similar role for polynomials of arbitrary degree. 

Context. Polynomial factorization and root-finding over finite fields have been studied for several decades. Berlekamp is often credited with initiating the modern algorithmic study of these prob-lems [Ber67], although some key ideas already appear in the works of Gauss and Legendre. We refer to [vzGP01] for a historical survey of the area. Over the years, fast probabilistic algorithms have been developed, whereas deterministic polynomial-time algorithms are only known in restricted set-tings, often under GRH. Such algorithms exist when the characteristic of Fq is fixed [Ber70, Sho91], when f (X) has a bounded number of irreducible factors [Rón88], or when f (X) arises as the re-duction of an integer polynomial with suitable Galois properties [Rón92], the last two under GRH. Without GRH, polynomials over a prime field Fp can be factored deterministically in time poly-nomial in deg( f ) and p1/2 [Sho90], which is exponential in log p. For general finite fields, the best known deterministic algorithm is that of [Evd94], which runs under GRH in time polynomial in log q and deg( f )log deg( f ). Ivanyos, Karpinski and Saxena interpret the combinatorics underlying 1this algorithm in terms of m-schemes [IKS09], which yield a deterministic polynomial-time algo-rithm under GRH for polynomials of prime degree n such that n − 1 is smooth; this was extended in [AIKS14] to the case where n − 1 has a large r-smooth divisor, in time ( nr log q)O(1) . Ivanyos, Karpinski, Rónyai and Saxena eliminate GRH from most of these results [IKRS12], at the price of a weaker output: for instance, in deterministic time polynomial in log q and deg( f )log deg( f ), their algorithm returns either a nontrivial factor of f (X) or a nontrivial automorphism of Fq[X]/(f (X)). Another approach is given by Camion’s algorithm [Cam83a, Cam83b], which provides a determinis-tic variant of the Cantor–Zassenhaus algorithm, but relies on a precomputed subset of Fq for which no algorithm polynomial in log q is known. 

1.2 Main contributions 

Our main contribution is a deterministic root-finding algorithm for Fq that splits into two phases: an offline phase, which depends only on the field, and an online phase, which takes the polynomial 

f (X) as input. The interface between the two is the following notion.

The online phase is unconditional: it relies on no unproven hypothesis, and in particular not on GRH. This contrasts with the elliptic-curve method in [Rón92], which relies on GRH through Galois-theoretic machinery over number fields. Moreover, apart from factors polynomial in log q,the bound depends on the field only through Ξ( D), so that a datum with small Ξ( D) yields an efficient online phase regardless of the divisibility properties of q − 1. The difficulty is thus displaced to the offline phase, which we address from the angles of verifi-cation, existence, and construction. 

Verification. Preprocessing data are efficiently verifiable: their validity can be checked deter-ministically and unconditionally in time polynomial in log q and Ξ( D) (Proposition 6.4). This distinguishes our approach from Camion’s framework [Cam83a, Cam83b], another deter-ministic method whose precomputation depends only on Fq. There, the precomputed object is 2a subset of Fq required to separate the roots of every input polynomial; we are not aware of any procedure certifying it in time polynomial in log q, so that the precomputation cannot be decoupled from its verification. 

Existence. The existence of good preprocessing data (i.e. with small Ξ( D)) is related to the existence of pairs of smooth orders in the Hasse–Weil interval, which contains the order of E(Fq)for every elliptic curve E over Fq (Hasse’s theorem, see Section 3): 

Proposition 1.4. Let (N, ˜N ) be a pair of integers in the Hasse–Weil interval [q + 1 − 2√q, q +1 + 2 √q] such that N + ˜N = 2( q + 1) and N̸ ≡ 1 mod p. Then there exists an elliptic preprocessing datum D for Fq with |E(Fq)| = N and | ˜E(Fq)| = ˜N .Proof. Set U := q + 1 − N . That N lies in the Hasse–Weil interval means |U | ≤ 2√q, and 

N̸ ≡ 1 mod p means that U is coprime to q. By [Wat69, Thm. 4.1], there exists an elliptic curve E

over Fq with |E(Fq)| = N , and since p > 3 it admits a short Weierstrass model. Pick any nonsquare 

β ∈ F× 

> q

, and let ˜E be the quadratic twist of E with respect to β. The Frobenius trace of E is U ,hence that of ˜E is −U (Section 3), whence | ˜E(Fq)| = q + 1 + U = 2( q + 1) − N = ˜N . Finally, E(Fq)and ˜E(Fq) are finite abelian groups of rank at most 2, so each admits a basis, with the convention that G2 = ∞ in the cyclic case. Proposition 1.4 turns the existence of a good preprocessing datum into a purely number-theoretic question: does the Hasse–Weil interval contain pairs of smooth integers summing to 2( q + 1)? We conjecture that it does. 

Conjecture 1.5. There exist constants c > 0 and p0 such that, for every prime p ≥ p0 and every integer e ≥ 1, letting q = pe, there exists an integer U ∈ [0 , 2√q] coprime to q such that both 

q + 1 − U and q + 1 + U are (log q)c-smooth. 

Section 6.2 identifies this statement as a constrained instance of a question of Erdős and Graham on sums of two smooth integers, and discusses what the standard heuristics predict for the exponent 

c; Section 7.1 reports experiments. Combined with Proposition 1.4, it gives: 

Corollary 1.6. Under Conjecture 1.5, every finite field Fq of sufficiently large characteristic admits an elliptic preprocessing datum D with Ξ( D) ≤ (log q)O(1) .

Construction. There remains the task of producing such a datum deterministically. Section 6 shows that, once a curve with smooth group orders is known, all the remaining components of the datum can be computed in deterministic polynomial time under GRH when q is prime. Constructing the curve is therefore the obstruction: 

Problem 1.7 (Deterministic preprocessing) . Does there exist a deterministic polynomial-time al-gorithm that, on input q, constructs an elliptic preprocessing datum D with Ξ( D) ≤ (log q)O(1) ?

Corollary 1.8. Under a positive answer to Problem 1.7, root-finding over Fq (Problem 1.1) is solvable deterministically in time polynomial in log q and deg( f ).

Our main result can therefore be read as a reduction: deterministic root-finding over Fq reduces to the offline construction of an elliptic curve whose group of rational points and that of its quadratic twist both have smooth order. 3Implementation. Our algorithmic description is sufficiently precise to enable a direct implemen-tation in computer algebra systems. We provide a prototype implementation in Magma, available at [Spa26]. Our experiments show that the algorithm is not restricted to special values of q,in contrast to cyclotomic methods, which rely on smoothness assumptions on q − 1: for general prime powers, our unoptimized implementation performs deterministic root-finding computations in practice, and for instance handles prime fields of size larger than 2 63 on a standard laptop. 

1.3 Comparison with prior work 

Cyclotomic methods. Cyclotomic methods reduce root-finding to computations in smooth sub-groups of F× 

> q

, and their cost is governed by the largest prime factor of q − 1. This line was initiated by [Moe77], for prime powers q such that q − 1 is divisible by a large power of 2, and turned into a general framework by Mignotte and Schnorr [MS88] and by von zur Gathen [vzG87]. so that its GCD with f (X) is obtained from a single binary exponentiation. The construction was extended in [BvzGL01] to fields for which the value at q of some cyclotomic polynomial has no large prime factor. A parallel line of work is based on (generalized) Graeffe transforms: replacing f (X) by the polynomial whose roots are the ℓ-th powers of its roots pushes the roots into a subgroup of index 

ℓ, and iterating over the prime divisors of q − 1 produces a sequence ending at ( X − 1) deg( f ). The difficulty is to trace the roots back through this sequence, which [GvdHL15, GvdHL16, vdHM21] achieve using the tangent Graeffe transform ; a related algorithm is given in [DPP16, Sec. 3.2], whose backward step calls an external root-finding procedure. These methods require q − 1 to be smooth. Our algorithm uses the recursive framework of [MS88] and changes the group it acts upon. Section 2.1 describes the correspondence, and Appendix A restates the cyclotomic algorithm in the formalism used here. 

Replacing cyclotomy by elliptic curves. Substituting an elliptic curve of smooth group order for a smooth cyclotomic group is a classical technique in computational number theory. The best-known instance is the elliptic curve method for integer factorization [Len87], which replaces the smoothness assumption on p − 1 in Pollard’s algorithm by one on |E(Fp)|; another is elliptic curve primality proving [GK86, AM93], which replaces the Pocklington test (and the partial factorization of p − 1 it requires) by a condition on a suitably chosen curve. Beyond these two examples, an elliptic-curve version of the AKS primality test was designed in [EL13]. A more recent instance arises in FFT-based multiplication: multiplying in Fq[X] by the classical FFT requires F× 

> q

to contain many elements of smooth order, and when this fails one must build “synthetic” roots of unity [SS71, Für07, HvdH21]. The works of [BSCKL23] and [CL26] show that elliptic curves with many rational points of smooth order can be used instead. In all of these, as here, the arithmetic of q − 1 is traded for the arithmetic of a group of rational points on an elliptic curve. 4The closest precursor to the present work is the algorithm sketched at the end of [Rón92] for deterministic root-finding over Fp. It may be read as an elliptic analogue of the Graeffe transform: given f (X), a curve E, and a prime ℓ dividing |E(Fp2 )|, it computes the polynomial whose roots are the x-coordinates of the points [ ℓ · (x, y )], where x ranges over the roots of f and y2 = x3 + ax + b.Iterating projects the roots into subgroups of decreasing size, and recovering them amounts to computing points P with [ ℓ · P ] = Q. The resulting complexity is polynomial in log p, deg( f ) and the largest prime factor of |E(Fp2 )|, although the exponents are not made explicit. Our algorithm differs from this one in two respects. First, its root-recovery step proceeds by lifting the problem to characteristic zero and exploiting the Galois structure of the torsion of a lift of E over Q [Rón92, Cor. 5.6]; this requires computing Galois groups of division polynomials, which is polynomial-time (the ℓ-torsion lies in an extension of degree less than ℓ4) but prohibitively expensive in practice, and is only known to be effective under GRH. Our algorithm performs all the computations over Fq: it uses only the intrinsic group structure of elliptic curves over finite fields, and its online phase is unconditional. Second, we track the complexity of every step, which yields the explicit bound of Theorem 1.3, and the resulting algorithm is simple enough to be implemented. Variants using isogenies in place of multiplication maps were proposed in [DPP16, Sec. 3.3]. There the roots are embedded on E using Icart’s map [Ica09], which forces p ≡ 2 mod 3. Inverting the isogeny, however, requires an external root-finding procedure. 

Encoding field elements as points. The lifting of Section 2.2 is a deterministic encoding of Fq into the rational points of a curve and its twist. Such encodings have been considered in cryptography, where hashing into elliptic curves is a well-studied primitive: see [SvdW06] and Icart’s map [Ica09]. Compared with Icart’s map, our method imposes no congruence condition on p, and it is injective, since x is recovered from the x-coordinate of its image; moreover, up to the sign of y, it reaches every affine point of E(Fq) and of ˜E(Fq), except the (at most three) points of order 2 of 

> ˜

E(Fq). Nevertheless, it is not a substitute for a hash into a prescribed curve. 

Other arithmetico-geometric approaches. Root-finding and factorization have also been ap-proached through other objects of arithmetic geometry. Kayal proposed a factoring algorithm based on point-counting on elliptic curves, described in [Wol17], which Poonen extended to abelian varieties [Poo19]. A separate line is based on Drinfeld modules: it originates in [PP89], was rediscovered in [vdH04], and modern fast probabilistic algorithms in this framework are given in [Nar18, Nar19, DNS21]. Root-finding also arises when computing isomorphisms between repre-sentations of finite fields: constructing Fq[X]/P (X) ≃ Fq[X]/Q (X) amounts to finding a root of Q

in Fq[X]/P (X). Elliptic-curve methods for this problem are described in [Pin92]. Finally, one of the first applications of Schoof’s algorithm [Sch85] was the computation of square roots modulo a prime, that is, root-finding for X2 − a, the smallest nontrivial instance of Problem 1.1. 

A recent claim by OpenAI. A recent preprint by OpenAI [Ope26] claims an unconditional deterministic algorithm that factors a polynomial f ∈ Fp[X] in O(((deg( f ) + 1) ⌈log 2 p⌉)10 12 ) bit operations. Its unconditionality rests on a uniform zero-free region for Hecke L-functions established in a companion preprint, and its algebraic part alone is claimed to yield a polynomial-time algorithm under GRH. 2.3 Representing sets of points 

Everything that follows is phrased in terms of divisors; we summarize here the terminology of Definition 4.3. A split divisor is a formal sum D = ∑ 

> i

Asimple divisor is encoded by its ( u, v )-coordinates : the monic polynomial u(X) whose roots are the x-coordinates of the Pi, and the interpolant v(X) of degree less than deg( u) through their

A general split divisor is therefore represented as a sum of simple divisors with disjoint supports, plus a flag for ∞; our algorithms take and return such sums, and process each summand independently. u split separable and v2 ≡ h mod u, recover the corresponding points. It is the elliptic analogue of root-finding, and the object of Sections 2.4 and 2.5.

6.1 Constructing the curve: the CM method and its limitations 

Since Ξ( E) = Ξ( ˜E), searching for a suitable curve amounts to searching over pairs {E, ˜E} of quadratically twisted curves. When 4 p = U 2 +dV 2 with p prime and d small, a general method for constructing elliptic curves over prime fields with prescribed Frobenius trace is the Complex Multiplication (CM) method. We refer to [Mor91, AM93] and [CFA +05, Sec. 18] for background on this method. It relies on algebraic number theory in the imaginary quadratic field Q(√−d). In our setting, we seek values of U such that both q + 1 − U and q + 1 + U have no large prime factors. Given a trace U ∈ J0, 2√pK allowed by the Hasse–Weil bound and not divisible by p, the CM method proceeds by first constructing the Hilbert class polynomial Hd(X) associated with 

Q(√−d), where d is the squarefree integer such that dC 2 = 4 p − U 2 for C ∈ Z. This can be done deterministically in time polynomial in log p and d; see [AM93] and [Mor91, Sec. 4]. An elliptic curve with Frobenius trace U can then be obtained from a root of Hd(X) mod p. By Dirichlet’s class number formula, the degree of Hd(X), which equals the class number of Q(√−d), is 

O(√d log d). Therefore, when d is sufficiently small, a root of Hd(X) mod p can be found efficiently using a probabilistic algorithm. The Hilbert class polynomial also enjoys strong Galois-theoretic properties: in particular, its splitting field is an abelian extension of Q(√−d). Moreover, this splitting field has degree at most 2 deg( Hd) over Q. It follows from [Rón92, Cor. 5.6] that, under GRH, a root of Hd(X) mod p can be found deterministically in time polynomial in log p, deg( Hd) and in the bit-size of the coefficients of Hd(X). Unfortunately, the CM method is likely not sufficient to construct elliptic curves E with suffi-ciently small Ξ( E). Indeed, the requirement that d be small is quite restrictive, and it is therefore unlikely that, for every p, there exists an elliptic curve such that both d and Ξ( E) are polynomial in log( p).

# 8 Perspectives and open questions 

8.1 Improving the complexity bound 

There are several possible directions for improving the complexity bound in Theorem 5.8. The first one concerns the exponent ω. Inspecting the proofs of Propositions 5.3 and 5.5, only two steps 37 cost deg( f )ω, both in Algorithm 3: the implicit interpolation of Remark 5.1, and the characteristic polynomial of Remark 5.2. Both are currently solved through an explicit deg( f ) × deg( f ) matrix, whose construction alone costs ˜O(deg( f )2) operations. We expect that both steps can be performed without this matrix, using power projec-tions [Sho94, BSS03]. The interpolation problem of Remark 5.1 is an instance of inverse modular composition . When the quotient ring is a field, Shoup [Sho94, Thm. 3.5] solves this problem deterministically within the cost of Brent and Kung’s modular composition algorithm: he com-putes power projections by transposition of that algorithm, and then solves a Hankel system; see also [NSSV24, Sec. 1.4.2]. In our setting, u(X) is split, so that Fq[X]/(u(X)) is not a field, but we may use the trace form instead of Shoup’s linear form. The characteristic polynomial of Remark 5.2 could be handled similarly: Shoup also computes minimal polynomials from power projections [Sho94, Thm. 3.4]. If this approach is confirmed, the exponent ω in Theorem 5.8 would be replaced by ( ω + 1) /2. Going further, Neiger, Salvy, Schost and Villard [NSSV24] recently obtained the first algorithm for modular composition with an exponent below 3 /2, namely O(n1.43 ) operations, and their tech-niques also apply to the computation of minimal and characteristic polynomials of elements of quotient rings such as Fq[X]/(u(X)) [NSSV24, Sec. 10.1]. Their algorithms are randomized, and adapting them to our deterministic setting is an open question. 

8.2 Using more general curves 

Our approach can be extended to hyperelliptic curves of genus g > 1. One could start by choosing a squarefree polynomial h(X) of odd degree, defining a hyperelliptic curve H with equation Y 2 =

h(X). Algorithms 1 and 2 do not impose restrictions on the degree of h, and can therefore be used to construct split divisors on H and on its quadratic twist that encode the roots of the input polynomial f (X). The next step would be to use rational points on the Jacobian of H and its quadratic twist. In this setting, division polynomials in Algorithm 4 can be replaced by Cantor’s analogues for hyperelliptic curves [Can94]. The potential advantage of this approach is the increased flexibility in constructing groups over Fq with smooth order. However, a drawback is that the Jacobian of a genus-g hyperelliptic curve has approximately qg points, making smoothness less likely. Nevertheless, CM methods could potentially be adapted to enforce smooth group orders in this setting, for instance using techniques from [BILV16]. 

8.3 Using other algebraic groups 

In this paper, we focused on elliptic curves. As discussed in Section 8.2, the approach may extend to more general curves. More broadly, it may be possible to use other commutative algebraic groups defined over Fq.One potential direction is to consider algebraic tori [Vos98, RS03], whose group orders are given by evaluations of cyclotomic polynomials at q. While deterministic polynomial-time algorithms are known when such values are smooth [BvzGL01], these results are mainly of theoretical interest. Adapting the Mignotte–Schnorr framework in this setting could lead to more explicit and practical complexity bounds. 38 8.4 Polynomial-time deterministic precomputation 

We close by returning to Problem 1.7, an effective version of Conjecture 1.5. For prime q, it amounts under GRH to finding the curve (Section 6). By Proposition 1.4, it suffices to find a Frobenius trace U such that ( q + 1 − U )( q + 1 + U ) is (log q)O(1) -smooth, together with a curve realizing it. Among the roughly 4 √q admissible traces, candidates are cheap to reject and, by Proposition 6.4, cheap to certify; what is missing is a deterministic way of finding one. The CM method (Section 6.1) only reaches traces with small discriminant, exhaustive scanning of j-invariants (as in our implementation) is not polynomial, and Conjecture 1.5 asserts existence without saying anything about the density of suitable traces. The problem remains open even with randomization: drawing random curves, as in the elliptic curve method [Len87], heuristically yields a datum with Ξ( D) subexponential in log q in subexponential expected time, and a probabilistic polynomial-time construction with Ξ( D) ≤ (log q)O(1) would also be a significant result.
