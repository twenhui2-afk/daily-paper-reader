ROOT ISOLATION FOR ANALYTIC FUNCTIONS
USING CUBIC HERMITE INTERPOLATION
CHRISTOPHE RAFFALLI
Abstract. We present a root isolation algorithm for an analytic function. The method com-
bines interval subdivision with a heuristic test based on local cubic Hermite interpolation.
Whenever the function is sufficiently close to its cubic Hermite interpolant at a few sample
points, we assume that the critical points of the cubic interpolant give a valid root isolation for
the original function.
Such an algorithm cannot, in general, provide a rigorous certification of the computed inter-
vals. Nevertheless, extensive numerical experiments show that it is highly efficient and almost
always produces the correct results. We hope that a probabilistic or exact algorithm can be
derived from this work.
1. Introduction
In this paper, we propose a root isolation algorithm based on a local interpolation criterion.
The key idea is to replace symbolic root-counting tests by a numerical comparison between the
function and a local Hermite interpolant.
The isolation of the real roots of a polynomial, or more generally of an analytic function,
is a fundamental problem in computer algebra and computational geometry. Given a function
f : R →R, the objective is to compute a finite collection of pairwise disjoint intervals, each
containing exactly one real root of f and such that every real root belongs to one of these
intervals.
Several methods have been developed for isolating the real roots of a univariate polynomial.
Among the most classical ones are:
• Continued fraction methods [7]: this method transforms the polynomial using Möbius
substitutions and recursively computes continued fraction expansions to isolate roots,
without explicitly subdividing the real line.
• Descartes’ method [5, 4, 6]: based on Descartes’ rule of signs, it uses the number of sign
variations of transformed polynomials to bound the number of roots in an interval. It
recursively subdivides intervals until each interval contains at most one root.
• Sturm’s method: it uses Sturm sequences to count exactly the number of distinct real
roots in any interval. Combined with recursive subdivision, it provides a certified root
isolation algorithm.
There is less work on root isolation for general (non-polynomial) functions. We may cite
the work of John P. Boyd [1] and the paper of Cleonice F. Bracciali and Michael Carley [2].
Similarly to our approach, they use a local interpolation: Chebyshev for the former, Taylor
expansion for the latter. The main difference with our work is that we use a fixed degree 3
Hermite interpolation.
The decision tests used during root isolation generally have a quadratic complexity with
respect to the degree, typically O(d2). This complexity comes either from the computation of
Taylor shifts, as in Descartes-based methods, or from the basic construction and evaluation of
Sturm sequences. In the case of Descartes’ rule, we can abort the computation as soon as we
detect too many sign changes to mitigate the cost. Although asymptotically faster algorithms
based on FFT techniques can reduce this complexity for very large degrees, they are usually not
advantageous in practice for the moderate degrees encountered in most applications.

Our interpolation-based test has a complexity proportional to the cost of a few evaluation
of the function and its derivative. For a polynomial of degree d, a direct evaluation generally
costs O(d) operations, while for special families such as Chebyshev or Mignotte polynomials,
this cost can be reduced to O(1). This leads to a root isolation algorithm that appears linear in
our experiments for those classes of polynomials.
Another important advantage of our approach is that it does not require to expand the
polynomial and the test can be applied without modification to analytic functions.
Finally, unlike certified symbolic approaches, our method is designed as a numerical algorithm
relying on standard floating-point arithmetic, using either 64-bit double-precision or even 32-bit
single-precision floating point arithmetic (the latter is standard on GPUs). Despite this limited
precision, the method exhibits very good numerical stability in practice. This numerical stability
is also improved by the fact that we do not need to expand the polynomials.
For instance, at the end of this paper, we report an experiment to visualize surfaces using
GPUs with only 32-bit floating point arithmetic. It is very important to be able to evaluate the
polynomial and its derivative restricted to a line, by computing the polynomial and its gradient
in R3.
We are able to prove that our algorithm indeed terminates and produces intervals that contain
at least one root of the provided analytic function. The algorithm is parameterized by two
constants ε ∈]0, 1[ and n ∈N∗. The integer n is the number of point where we test the quality
of the Hermite interpolation, by checking that a quantity Q(x) < ε. Our experiments indicate
that very reasonable values of these constants, n = 3 or 6 for instance, are sufficient for many
families of polynomials or analytic functions.
We hope that an exact or a probabilistic algorithm may be within reach by developing the
idea in this work.

Algorithm 9 (root isolation algorithm). Our algorithm is a recursive function that considers
an interval [a, b]. It takes as fixed parameters a way to compute the value of a function f and
its derivative, ε ∈]0, 1[ and n ∈N∗. It returns a list of sub-intervals of [a, b] and processes as
follows:
• If the interval [a, b] under consideration is not (ε, n) −good we subdivide the interval at
a tested point that maximizes Q.
• Otherwise we use the sign changes of h (we computed the roots of h′ to test for goodness)
to collect at most 3 intervals with exactly one root of h and at least one root of f. The
lower bound on the number of roots of f follows from the fact that h and f have the
same sign in a, b and the roots of h′ (see the remark in the first item of definition 8).
Remark: We have not tested approaches using Newton step to improve performance in the
case of clustered roots like for Mignotte polynomials as in [4].
Proposition 10. Our root isolation algorithm terminates for an analytic function f on an open
set Ω⊂R, with no multiple roots and starting from any interval I = [A, B] ⊂Ω. It gives a list
of intervals in I that contain at least one root of f each. This is true for any value of ε ∈]0, 1[
and n ∈N∗.
Proof. Termination is a consequence of proposition 6 and 7. The former ensures that small
enough interval are (ε, n)−good. The latter ensure that intervals decrease to a 0 width. Indeed,
if the subdivision point is one of the regularly spaced test points, this is immediate. If the
subdivision point x is a root of h′, the derivative of the interpolant of f on the considered
interval [a, b], the latter lemma ensures that x−a > ηε and b−x > ηε Hence x−a < (b−a)−ηε
and b −x < (b −a) −ηε.
The fact that each interval contains at least one root of f is explained in the description of
the algorithm.
□
Conjecture 11. For any analytic function with no multiple roots, we can find ε ∈]0, 1[ and
n ∈N∗such that the above algorithm misses no roots (never or with very low probability, we
do not know if we can get an exact algorithm). Moreover, our experiments seem to show that ε
and n may be relatively small and independent of the degree and the size of interval for many
families of polynomials.

3. Experimental Results
3.1. Software availability. All the code used in this work is publicly available. The pure root
isolation algorithm is implemented in Julia and can be used independently for reproducing the
numerical experiments. The implicit surface visualization experiments rely on the CamlSurf
framework, available at https://github.com/craff/CamlSurf. In CamlSurf, the root finding
algorithm has been rewritten in the GLSL language.

The proposed interpolation criterion is also used in an implicit surface visualization frame-
work. The algorithm is integrated into a GPU-based renderer using single precision floating-point
arithmetic (IEEE 754 float32).
In most of our examples, we give the most complex here, the rendering rate remains above
30 FPS (frames per second), allowing interactive exploration and animation of the surfaces.
One exception is the heptic surface from O. Labs, whose very large algebraic expression leads
to a substantial increase in evaluation cost at only 20 FPS. We also show two non polynomial
functions. Their high number of roots and the use of transcendental functions also require more
time.
Despite the use of single precision arithmetic and the absence of exact certification, no visible
numerical artifact or incorrect root classification was observed in our experiments. These results
suggest that the local interpolation test remains highly reliable even in a demanding real-time
geometric setting.
