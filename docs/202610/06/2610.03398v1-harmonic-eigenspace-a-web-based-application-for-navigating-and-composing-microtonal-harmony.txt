ABSTRACT
This paper presents a web-based application for navigating and composing micro-
tonal harmony, built on the Harmonic Eigenspace, a four-dimensional psychoacous-
tically grounded space in which tetrad chord types are located by their spectral
dissonance profiles, computed with Sethares’s roughness/dissonance model. The co-
ordinate system is transposition-invariant: a coordinate triple (α, β, γ) locates the
three upper notes in relation to the root, so a chord quality corresponds to a direc-
tion in the space, the invariant ray along which transposition acts, while the root
frequency sets the scale. The dissonance field over these coordinates can be com-
puted at any register; the locations of its local minima are register-invariant, as they
arise from partial-coincidence ratio conditions. The dissonance volume contains 100
local minima that align with just-intonation intervals and act as landmarks, organ-
ising the space into basins around the most consonant tetrads. We embed tetrads
from three tonal equal temperaments as discrete lattices within this continuous vol-
ume. The application presents this space through two components: the Harmonic
Eigenspace as a navigable 3D visualisation of the dissonance volume in which all
nodes are playable, and a Modal Studio that extends modal interchange logic to
the ten-gradation interval vocabulary of 53-TET. The application also functions
as a MIDI controller with MIDI Polyphonic Expression support, which can be in-
tegrated into any digital audio workstation that supports this format. A listening
study with 31 participants used both scenes of the application: listeners were first
exposed to isolated 53-TET chords, recording how these new qualities are perceived
alongside chords familiar from Western practice, such as the maj7 and the m7; they
then rated chord progressions composed in the Modal Studio, measuring their ac-
ceptance/rejection of microtonal chord progressions encountered for the first time.
KEYWORDS
Microtonality; Tonal equal temperament; Modal Interchange; Harmony.
1. Introduction
Approached from the Western perspective, microtonality opens new creative possi-
bilities with a richer harmonic vocabulary that expands the standard 12-tone equal
temperament (12-TET), which offers two third-quality gradations, minor and major.
In 31-TET we find five: sub-minor, minor, neutral, major, and super-major; and in
53-TET, ten: sub-minor, down-minor, minor, up-minor, minor-neutral, major-neutral,
down-major, major, up-major, and super-major. These creative possibilities open ques-

starting point, we systematically explore the vocabulary and propose an instrument

Thus, this paper presents a web application that offers two scenes for exploring
microtonal harmonies from two complementary perspectives. The first scene is a four-
dimensional space formed by visualising and sonifying a cloud of dissonant/consonant
points. Those points are located in a proposed space we call the Harmonic Eigenspace,
formed by the degree of sensory dissonance, computed from the roughness between
partials, from a chosen root (for example, A3 = 220 Hz) across the ratio span of
one octave above it. The second scene is a grid we call the Modal Studio, where a
chord progression can be formed using modes derived from the 31- and 53-tone equal
temperaments, and modal interchange substitutions are generated and presented in the
application. The method’s finer scale resolution lets the 12-TET modes be deformed
into new formations, introducing new intervallic relations and chord qualities. We
conduct a listening test to expose participants to both scenes and observe how listeners
perceive basic examples in terms of novelty or musical tolerance/acceptance of the
proposed chords and harmonies.
The Harmonic Eigenspace principle rests on a psychoacoustic basis for the percep-
tion of consonance and dissonance, arising from the pattern of roughness produced
by the simultaneous partials of the sounding tones. This perceptive dissonance rela-
tionship was measured and formalised by Plomp and Levelt (1965) and extended to
complex timbres by Sethares (2005). Hence, we construct a space in which each tetrad
chord type occupies a position determined by its psychoacoustic dissonance profile: a
root and three upper voices span four frequency dimensions, and the three interval ra-
tios above the root form the coordinates, so chord types populate a three-dimensional
volume within a four-dimensional pitch space. In closed position within one octave,
this structure is the seventh chord, in which the qualities of the third, the fifth, and
the seventh act simultaneously. Therefore, a chord quality corresponds to an invariant
direction of pitch space under transposition; the local minima of the dissonance field
serve as landmarks that define distances to the chord types.
From here, we locate chords in 12-, 31- and 53-TET. 53-TET’s close approxima-
tion to both the just perfect fifth and the major third makes it consistent with both
Pythagorean and five-limit just intonation (Fokker, 1963; Tanaka, 1890). The Har-
monic Eigenspace (hereafter, the Eigenspace) provides a psychoacoustically rather
than music-theoretically grounded coordinate system for them: chord types are placed
as discrete objects without borrowing principles from a different tuning system.
However, it is important to clarify that a chord quality is a perceptual object. When
a listener hears a major seventh chord as stable or a diminished chord as tense, that
perception is shaped by cultural context, as well as by the timbral characteristics of
the musical instrument. The perceived dissonance varies across musical traditions and
practices. For instance, in Colombian Cumbia, dominant seventh chords can function
as a stable tonic rather than a tension requiring a resolution; the rhythm plays that
role of tension and release (Ochoa, 2016; Valencia et al., 2019). In the blues, major-
minor harmonic dualities do not conform to the harmonic principles of Western tonal
practice, yet listeners perceive their chord progressions as coherent and expressive
(Martin, 2023; Mulholland and Hojnacki, 2013). Many musical traditions worldwide
do not organise pitch simultaneously into chord qualities at all, relying instead on
melodic, rhythmic, or timbral dimensions of consonance and tension. This is why we
also propose a second scene where user can explore and listen to 31- and 53-TET chord
progressions in a more idiomatic way called the Modal Studio.

In the Modal Studio, we approach microtonality from the perspective of Western
modal-interchange principles applied in 12-TET. The harmonic navigation is proposed
by forming new modes and therefore new tetrads: the different gradations of intervals
from 31- and 53-TET can be heard and tested using a Scale Editor interface that
forms new dispositions of scales, mirroring the twelve notes used in Western modal
practice while offering a controlled surface on which to experiment and navigate new
harmonic trajectories. The standard modes can also be replicated, but there is a rich
collection of tetrad types that deviate from 12-TET, the 152 catalogued in Section 4.1;
many of those chords occupy regions of the Eigenspace where no 12-TET chord exists.
These generated progressions open new paths for musicians exploring microtonality.
The research questions are:
a) Can chord quality be defined as a position in a psychoacoustically grounded
space, such that the tetrad vocabularies of 12-, 31-, and 53-TET, and the con-
tinuum beyond them, can be located, compared, and audibly navigated in one
coordinate system?
b) When modal interchange logic is extended to the ten-gradation interval vocabu-
lary of 53-TET, do the resulting chord progressions sustain perceived harmonic
coherence and musicality, and how do these perceptual ratings vary with the
tonic chord’s proximity to familiar 12-TET qualities?
The contribution is twofold. The technical contribution is the instrument: a web
application that renders the dissonance volume as a navigable, sounding visualisation,
extends modal interchange to the 31- and 53-TET vocabulary in its Modal Studio,
and integrates with standard production environments through MIDI/MPE. The the-
oretical contribution is the foundation the instrument is built on, the Eigenspace: a
transposition-invariant coordinate space of tetrad types, grounded in the dissonance
function of Sethares (2005), in which 12-, 31-, and 53-TET coexist as discrete lat-
tices and can be compared without presupposing any scale theory. The listening study
documents how listeners, with their own cultural formation, respond to chords and
progressions drawn from the space.
The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 surveys related work on geometric
models of harmony, interactive harmonic exploration systems, and computational ap-
proaches to microtonality. Section 3 develops the mathematical foundations of the
Eigenspace, from the pairwise dissonance model for sinusoids through to the four-
dimensional dissonance surface for tetrads. Section 4 presents the harmonic principle
proposed. The design and implementation of the interactive application are described
in Section 5. Section 6 presents the listening test we performed. Section 7 discusses
implications and outlines directions for future work.

2. Literature Review
We first survey work on the computational modelling of spatial representations of
harmony, then interactive systems for exploring harmony, and finally, microtonality
applied to musicking as a cultural practice.

2.1. Geometric and Spatial Models of Harmony in 12-TET
Balzano (1980) framed harmonic structures using cyclic group theory, showing that
properties of the diatonic scale and the system of major and minor keys can be derived
from the algebraic structure of 12-fold pitch-class space, and argued that the same rea-
soning extends to other equal temperaments. Chew (2007) proposed the Spiral Array
model, placing pitches, chords, and keys on a helical surface in three-dimensional
space, with higher-level structures represented as convex combinations of their con-
stituent lower-level elements. This continuous representation reduces key-finding and
chord identification to nearest-neighbour searches in the spiral geometry, and has been
validated against empirical data on tonal pitch perception (Lerdahl, 2001).
A closely related tradition represents harmonic relationships through the Tonnetz, a
two-dimensional lattice in which pitch classes are nodes, triads occupy triangular cells,
and Neo-Riemannian operations correspond to reflections across shared edges. Cohn
(1997) formalised this structure and showed that voice leading between triads maps
directly onto geometric proximity within it. Tymoczko (2012) extended the frame-
work to a broader class of chords and tuning systems, while Milne and Holland (2016)
tested several models of perceived triadic distance against listener ratings, finding
that Tonnetz distance and spectral pitch-class distance were near-equivalent predictors
and nearly perfectly correlated with each other, while voice-leading models performed
poorly, suggesting that the Tonnetz derives its predictive power from psychoacoustic
rather than contrapuntal structure. The present work differs in its object: rather than
testing models of the perceived distance between pairs of 12-TET triads, it constructs
a coordinate space of tetrad types in which 12-, 31-, and 53-TET can be located and
compared. Topological approaches have extended spatial thinking to chord progres-
sions. Bigo and Andreatta (2015) represented progressions as paths through simplicial
complexes, where vertices correspond to pitch classes and higher-dimensional simplices
correspond to chords, making voice-leading structure geometrically explicit. These spa-
tial models share an important limitation for the present purpose: their geometry is
constructed from music-theoretic axioms (fifth-relatedness, scale membership, voice-
leading parsimony) rather than from a perceptual measurement.
Recent empirical work supports grounding harmonic geometry in perceptual mea-
surement: Frank and Harrison (2026) show that spectral interference is the primary
axis along which listeners vary in their pleasantness judgements of chords, account-
ing for the largest proportion of systematic between-listener variation, an effect that
strengthens with musical expertise.
2.2. Interactive Systems for Harmonic Exploration
Holland (1994) introduced Harmony Space, an early interactive system that uses spa-
tial metaphors derived from Balzano (1980) and Longuet-Higgins et al. (1962) the-
ories to make harmonic relationships navigable. Chords and keys are arranged on
a two-dimensional grid, and users explore tonal structure through direct manipula-
tion, making the system effective for both novices and experienced musicians. More
recent systems have incorporated data-driven generation. Huang et al. (2016) built
ChordRipple, which recommends chord continuations by learning statistical patterns
from a corpus, extending a novice composer’s harmonic vocabulary beyond familiar
progressions. Transformer-based approaches (Choi et al., 2016; Wu and Yang, 2020)
have since pushed sequential chord generation further, though these systems remain
bound to 12-TET and provide no perceptual account of why particular chords sound

2.3. Cultural Practices of Microtonal Music
Microtonal practice has a long and continuous history in both Western and non-
Western music. Harry Partch spent decades building instruments capable of realising
his 43-tone scale, grounding an entire compositional language in just intonation ratios
that standard Western tuning cannot express (Gilmore, 2003). In Europe, Manfred
Stahnke and his Hamburg school have pursued microtonal composition since the 1980s,
working with just intonation and 31-tone frameworks. Outside the Western tradition,
53 equal divisions of the octave serve as the reference unit of modern Turkish makam
theory, embracing its historical Pythagorean tone systems with less than a cent of
error (Yarman, 2007).
Contemporary microtonal practice is growing and increasingly visible. Jacob Collier
has brought microtonal harmony into mainstream popular music, introducing interme-
diate steps within standard chord progressions and adjusting voicing to align consonant
sonorities outside the 12-TET ratio. Online communities have accelerated this dissem-
ination; for example, the educators and composers of Hear Between The Lines1 offer
tutorials and compositions using 31-TET custom instruments. Zheanna Erose2 uses
microtonal keyboards and explores non-Western modes. Mike Battaglia3 blends West-
ern harmonic concepts and complex voicing in blues, jazz, and his own compositions.
The composer, instrumentalist, and singer Maddie Ashman4 has grown in popularity,
using a custom-tuned keyboard, a guitar with a slider, and a cello. The Quebec-based
microtonal rock band Angine de Poitrine5, using guitar, bass, electronics (pedals) in
24-TET, and drums; has reached 3.5M views on the YouTube channel KEXP6. The
xenharmonic movement7, with contributions from artists including Elaine Walker,
Birdhouse, and Stephen James Taylor8, using microtonal exploration for electronic
music.
Instrument development has followed: microtunable synthesisers date back at least
to the Yamaha DX7-II (1986), whose user-tuning tables made non-12-TET scales
widely available; the Lumatone9 isomorphic keyboard offers a hardware interface ca-
pable of mapping 31-TET and 53-TET tuning systems, though its cost remains a
barrier to wider adoption. The Roli10 keyboard introduced the possibility of bending
notes on a 12-TET-based keyboard by enabling an interactive surface to express in-
termediate steps between keys. The same company introduced the MPE11 formatting.
The piano keyboard called Osmose12 allows pitch bend expressive control on keys
using the MPE format.

2.4. Microtonality in Computation and Practice
The theoretical grounding for 53-TET goes back to Holder (1731) and was formalised
by Fokker (1963, 1955), who developed the antan´airesis approach to identifying equal
temperaments that closely approximate just intonation intervals. Muzzulini (2020)
documents Newton’s investigation of equal divisions of the octave from 15 to 612
parts, with particular attention to the 53-division for its accuracy in approximat-
ing the perfect fifth and the major third. 53-TET tempers out both the schisma
(32805:32768 ≈2.0 cents, the residue by which eight just fifths and a just major third
exceed five octaves) and the kleisma (15625:15552 ≈8.1 cents, the residue between
six just minor thirds and an octave plus a fifth), making it simultaneously consistent
with Pythagorean and five-limit just intonation (Tanaka, 1890).
In computational music, Anders and Miranda (2010) developed a constraint-based
system for microtonal composition supporting multiple temperaments, though it was
discontinued with its host platform. Hirai (2023) provides a dataset of microtonal MIDI
and audio recordings to support machine learning work in this domain. The present
application addresses a gap none of these fill: a real-time interactive tool grounded
in psychoacoustic dissonance in which the perceptual landscape itself determines the
structure of the navigable space, applied specifically to the harmonic possibilities of
53-TET.
3. Mathematical Foundations
This section develops the mathematical basis of the application’s harmonic map, the
Harmonic Eigenspace, from the ground up. We begin applying the Sethares formula
foundation, explained in sections 3.1 and 3.2: two pure sinusoids sounding together, and
the perceptual roughness that arises between them. From there, we apply the model
to a third simultaneous frequency, forming a 3D topology with consonance valleys and
dissonance mountains (section 3.3). Then, we add the fourth note, forming a four-
dimensional dissonance surface that forms the coordinate system of the Harmonic
Eigenspace (section 3.4). The underlying principle throughout is that dissonance is
the sum of pairwise roughness across all interacting partials.
3.1. Sensory dissonance for pairs of sinusoids

become individually resolvable by the cochlea. Sethares (2005) fitted a difference of

of the two tones coincide or fall far apart, producing little beating. When the ratio
deviates from these values, nearby partials fall within each other’s critical bands and
generate roughness. Minima can only arise at ratios where partials of the two tones
coincide: with six partials there is no coincidence at the minor sixth (8 : 5) or the
septimal minor seventh (7:4), which would require the eighth and the seventh partial,
respectively. For the same reason, the number of minima is finite: Sethares bounds it
by 2n2 for a spectrum of n partials. Partials of zero or negligible loudness contribute
nothing, since each pairwise term in Equation 4 is weighted by the loudness of the
quieter partial.
This result establishes a direct link between the spectral content of a sound and
the intervals that will be perceived as consonant. For harmonic timbres, the minima
recover the familiar intervals of Western tonal music. For inharmonic timbres, the
minima shift to entirely different locations, implying that consonance is not a fixed
property of interval ratios but a function of the relationship between tuning and timbre.
The construction is therefore not assumption-free: it inherits the roughness model of
Sethares (2005), its fitted psychoacoustic parameters, and the choice of a harmonic
timbre, and the alignment of the minima with just intonation throughout this paper
is a consequence of these choices; Section 7 discusses the inharmonic case.

The name Eigenspace refers to the following structure. Transposition acts on pitch
space as the uniform scaling Tλ = λI: moving a chord from root f to root λf replaces
v with λv and leaves every interval ratio unchanged. All transpositions of a chord
therefore lie on one ray through the origin, and each ray corresponds to one chord
quality: for instance, all maj7 (C maj7, D maj7, F♯maj7, etc.) lie on the same ray.
Since Tλ is a scalar operator, every vector is trivially an eigenvector of it; the name
therefore does not designate an eigenspace in the strict linear-algebraic sense but the
space of invariant directions (eigendirections). Chord quality is a direction, the scale
factor λ records the transposition interval, and (α, β, γ), the representative of a ray
with root 1, is a global coordinate chart on the space of qualities.
Note that even though the dissonance value attached to a point does depend on the
register, the locations of the local minima do not inherit this dependence: a minimum
sits where partials of the sounding tones coincide, and coincidence is a condition on
frequency ratios alone, so the coordinates of a minimum are the same at every register
Each point in the Eigenspace corresponds to the closed-position of a chord type,
with all upper voices within one octave of the root.
Specific tuning systems appear as discrete lattices within this volume. The 53-
TET grid samples the Eigenspace at intervals of 2k/53, closely approximating many
just intonation ratios. The 12-TET grid is a coarser subset, sampling at intervals of
12


2k/12. A dominant seventh chord in 12-TET, a septimal dominant seventh in just
intonation (with the 7:4 minor seventh), and their nearest 53-TET approximations
occupy distinct but neighbouring positions, and their dissonance values account for
the perceptual differences between these chords. Qualities from any tuning system
coexist in the same space and can be compared on the same psychoacoustic basis;
Figures 6 and 7 show the lattices inside the volume.
4. Harmonic Extension Principle
The Eigenspace locates chord types by their psychoacoustic properties; this section ad-
dresses how a composer moves between them. Modal harmony organises a progression
around the characteristic colour of each scale degree rather than around dominant–
tonic syntax. In 12-TET, modal interchange is a standard reharmonisation technique:
chords are borrowed from parallel or relative modes to introduce new harmonic colour
into a progression. The interval vocabulary of 31-, 53-TET, with its five and ten gra-
dations of third and seventh quality, extends this principle into territory that 12-TET
cannot reach. Modes built from non-standard Holdrian comma (HC) distributions gen-
erate chord types absent from the diatonic vocabulary, and interchange between such
modes produces substitution possibilities that grow combinatorially with the number
of available interval qualities.
4.1. Chord Quality Extensions
The chord vocabulary embedded in the Eigenspace is constructed systematically for
each temperament. In 12-TET, the standard set of 12 tetrad types covers the familiar
qualities: maj7, min7, dom7, ø7, o7, minmaj7, 7sus2, 7sus4, maj6, min6, power, and
sus2, the last two realised as four-note dispositions (power as root, fourth, fifth, and
octave-doubled root; sus2 sounding all four of root, second, fourth, and fifth); and
from there it is possible to add extensions to the chord. In 31-TET, the five gradations
of third quality (sub-minor, minor, neutral, major, super-major) are combined with
the same five gradations of seventh quality to form a 5×5 grid of 25 chords (Table 1),
with three additional chords (ø7, o7, M+7) for a total of 28. In 53-TET, the ten third
gradations paired with ten seventh gradations produce a 10 × 10 main grid of 100
chords (Table 2), with the fifth fixed at step 31 (the closest approximation to the
just perfect fifth). Separate grids cover the half-diminished chords with a flattened
fifth at step 26 (Table 3, 40 chords), augmented chords with a raised fifth at step 35
(Table 4, 10 chords), and two suspended chords, for a total of 152 (take into account,
dim, augmented, and sus chord can also be extended much further). The grand total
across all three temperaments is 12 + 28 + 152 = 192 chords. Each chord is placed
in the Eigenspace at the (α, β, γ) coordinates computed from its interval ratios, and
coloured according to its dissonance value.

4.2. Modal Interchange in 53-TET
Modal interchange introduces chords from outside the current mode into a progression.
Two types of interchange are used (see Figure 5).
In a parallel interchange, chords are borrowed from a mode that shares the same
root. Starting in C Ionian, a parallel Aeolian interchange replaces diatonic chords with
their Aeolian counterparts: the flattened 3rd, 6th, and 7th degrees alter the available
chord qualities while the tonal centre remains fixed. A progression I vi ii V I in C
major yields, under parallel Aeolian interchange, A♭maj7, Dm7♭5, and Gm7 for the
middle three chords.
In a relative interchange, the current root is reinterpreted as a different scale
degree of another key, and chords are borrowed from that key. Starting again in C
Ionian, an Aeolian relative interchange places C as the 6th degree of E♭major. The
middle chords of the same progression become Cm7, Fm7, and B♭7.
In 12-TET, each mode produces a fixed set of seven chord qualities per interchange.
In 53-TET, the ten gradations of third quality and ten of seventh quality multiply the
number of available substitutions: a single parallel or relative interchange can yield
chord types that have no 12-TET equivalent. In Section 5, we explain how the Modal
Studio implements both interchange types across all seven diatonic modes, populating
the Grid with the full set of substitution options for any user-defined scale.
The modal interchange mechanism takes the seven diatonic modes as its start-
ing point. In 53-TET, the standard Ionian mode corresponds to the HC distribution
[9, 9, 4, 9, 9, 9, 4], which lands on the same pitch classes as 12-TET (see Figure 8). Any
modification to these step sizes produces a mode with no 12-TET equivalent: for in-
stance, the distribution [8, 7, 7, 9, 5, 10, 7] introduces neutral intervals that generate
chord qualities absent from the standard diatonic vocabulary. Each such mode defines
its own set of consonance and dissonance relations, and modal interchange between
these non-standard modes opens a combinatorial space of substitutions unavailable in
12-TET.

5. The Application
Based on the two principles explained, the computer application provides two areas of
exploration: a) The Harmonic Eigenspace and b) the Modal Studio. The two areas di-
vide the work. The Eigenspace is a sonification of the dissonance volume for positional
comparison of the 12-, 31-, and 53-TET vocabularies: a place to survey and audition
chord qualities. Composition proceeds in the Modal Studio, where scales, progressions,
and voicing are developed explicitly.
5.1. The Harmonic Eigenspace
We used the Plotly JavaScript open-source graphing library13 to form an interac-
tive visualisation of the Eigenspace. The application is available at https://dazzid.
github.io/ANIMA_Harmonic_Eigenspace/. The visualisation is composed of three
types of nodes:
a) The zone-nodes, derived from the 4D dissonance calculation, where each node is
clickable and reproduces the sound of the four-note groups. Those groups can be
chords or clusters; the space is not limited to any particular temperament. Each
node is coloured according to the dissonance mapping, and the visualisation can
be navigated as a full cluster of nodes or filtered by degree of dissonance using
the ‘Switch to Layered View’ button. All dissonance values reported in this paper
(the volume of Figure 4 and the values of Table 6) are computed for tones of six
equal-loudness partials, with the root taken at A3 (220 Hz), the sounding root of
the application; the root is a starting point for the computation, and any other
choice changes only the depths of the field, not its coordinates or the locations
of its minima.
b) Local-minima (LM) nodes mark the less dissonant points in a region, as shown
in Figures 3, 7. These nodes serve as spatial landmarks, providing clustering
distances and locality for each node. For instance, local-minimum 1 corresponds
to a just-intonation major seventh, and local-minimum 3 is located close to the
ˆmˆm7. A tempered chord appearing nearby in the space can be understood
as a tempered variant of that same harmonic region. Because LMs are derived
from the continuous dissonance landscape, they are not tempered and align with
just-intonation intervals. LMs were identified by evaluating D(α, β, γ) on a 5003
grid (each axis spanning the interval [1, 2] in steps of ≈0.002) and selecting
points whose dissonance value was lower than all neighbours within an 113 voxel
footprint. Candidates near the domain boundary (within a 50-step margin), with
near-degenerate intervals (α ≈β or β ≈γ within two grid steps), or with
prominence below 0.001 were discarded. The remaining candidates were ranked
by a curvature score (prominence weighted by local gradient magnitude), and
the top 100 were retained. Each was then refined by 100 iterations of stochastic
perturbation to converge on the true continuous minimum. The number of nodes
used is a parameter set to 78 in the web-app from those 100 LM.
c) Chord positions for 192 chords across three temperaments (12-TET, 31-TET,
and 53-TET) are displayed, with each node coloured according to its dissonance
value (see Section 4.1 for the full chord vocabulary). As an example, consider
the 12-TET major seventh chord (maj7). Its three frequency ratios relative to

5.2. Modal Studio
The Modal Studio is an interactive compositional environment for exploring microtonal
harmony in 53-TET through the lens of modal interchange. At its core, the system
comprises three interconnected editors. The Scale Editor (Figure 8) presents a circular
visualisation of the 53-TET octave with twelve draggable nodes on its outer ring,
each representing a scale degree. By repositioning these nodes, the user reshapes the
underlying interval pattern, and the system automatically derives all seven diatonic
modes (Ionian through Locrian) along with their associated chord qualities.

Once a scale is defined, chords built on each degree can be dragged from a side
panel into the first row of the Grid (Figure 9), an 8×8 matrix that serves as a
chord-progression sequencer. The first row holds the user’s primary progression; the
remaining seven rows are populated automatically with modal substitutions, includ-

root, same intervallic structure), enabling the composer to audition modal interchange
options at a glance. This is explained further in section 4.2.

can be dragged to craft specific voicings by placing them on a concentric spiral display

6. Listening Test
A listening study was conducted to address two questions: how listeners rate the dis-
sonance of 53-TET chords drawn from the application’s Eigenspace, and how tolerant
listeners are to microtonal chord progressions composed using the Modal Interchange
mode of the web-app. In total, 31 participants (22 male, 5 female, 2 prefer not to
say, 2 non-binary) completed a listening study using jsPsych (De Leeuw et al., 2023).
Their mean age was 36.0 years (SD = 10.4, range 22–58). The average completion time
was 17.7 minutes (SD = 23.1). The study consisted of two phases. Following informed
consent (age, gender, microtonal experience), participants completed a preliminary
harmonic identification task requiring them to identify chord progressions from audio
examples. In Part 1, participants rated the dissonance of 19 isolated microtonal chords
(representing six categories: Consonant, Intermediate, Neutral, Less Consonant, Dom-
inant, and Diminished) on a scale from 1 (very consonant) to 10 (very dissonant),
with white noise presented between trials. In Part 2, participants rated 10 chord pro-
gressions on four dimensions-harmony coherence, musicality, dissonance/consonance,
and novelty, using identical 1–10 scales. Each chord progression was presented with
its underlying harmonic notation. Participants were required to listen to complete
audio stimuli before responding. All trials were presented in randomised order, with
white noise palate cleansers separating progression trials. The experiment included
an exit survey soliciting comments and reports of difficulties. Dependent variables in-
cluded dissonance ratings for individual chords and ratings of harmony, musicality,
dissonance, and novelty for progressions.
The dataset, audio stimuli, and experiment code are publicly available.14

7. Discussion and Conclusion
We address the discussion and conclusion in two sections: the first discusses the ap-
plication and its Eigenspace, and the second the findings of the listening test.
7.1. The Harmonic Eigenspace
The geometric and topological models of harmony reviewed in Section 2 share a com-
mon dependency: their spatial structure is derived from music-theoretic axioms (fifth-
relatedness in the Tonnetz, voice-leading parsimony in Tymoczko’s chord spaces, scale
membership in Balzano’s group-theoretic model) that encode the properties of 12-
TET as foundational assumptions. The Harmonic Eigenspace takes a different starting
point. Its geometry is constructed from the psychoacoustic dissonance function alone,
with no prior commitment to a tuning system, a scale, or a definition of consonance
borrowed from tonal theory. The consequence is that 12-TET, 31-TET, and 53-TET
chord types coexist in the same continuous volume and can be compared on the same
perceptual basis.
This matters because the question of what a chord quality is receives a specific
answer in the Eigenspace formulation. The coordinate triple (α, β, γ) encodes quality
as an invariant direction under uniform frequency scaling (transposition); the scale
factor records the transposition interval. Two chords that share interval ratios but
differ in root are the same point; two chords with different ratios are different points,
regardless of what tuning system produced them. This is not a classification imposed
from outside. It is a coordinate that falls out of the dissonance function, and it resolves
a problem that existing spatial models do not address: how to locate and compare
chord types across tuning systems that share no common scale structure.
The 100 local minima serve as the landmark system of the Eigenspace, partitioning
it into basins that group chord types by their nearest just-intonation sonority. Within
each basin, tempered chords from different tuning systems cluster around the same
just-intonation minimum but at measurably different distances from it. The 12-TET
dominant seventh and its nearest 53-TET variants, for example, occupy distinct po-
24


sitions within the same basin, and their dissonance values quantify a difference that
Western theory acknowledges informally (the dominant seventh is “not quite in tune”)
but cannot measure. The Eigenspace makes these distinctions navigable and audible.
The harmonic exploration of new dissonant/consonant chord relations is open, as
well as the definition of functional rules of chords in 53-TET. The dominant chord in
12-TET used by different cultural musical practices could be interpreted with different
roles; in blues, the blue note implicitly bends towards a neutral quality (Cutting,
2018), which causes the effect of making the chord solid as a sonority, stronger, or
less dissonant; it could be perceived as more emphatic. If we find the closest stable
zone, we find a just-intonation version that is very well-structured in harmonic terms,
losing the 12-TET unstable quality. This observation is mostly perceptual; the only
proposed test is to play with them in the web-app to explore the differences.
Recent work by Frank and Harrison (2026) supports this reading. Applying variance
decomposition to pleasantness ratings of 68 chords in 12-TET, they find that spec-
tral interference is the primary axis of systematic between-listener variation in chord
preferences, and that this effect strengthens with musical expertise. Their structural
equation modelling also shows that preference variance increases with familiarity and
decreases with interference: listeners agree on rough chords (they dislike them) but di-
verge on smooth, familiar ones. This aligns with our observation that neutral chords,
which have no 12-TET counterpart, received dissonance ratings above their Eigenspace
values, while consonant chords near 12-TET equivalents tracked theirs closely. Their
finding that major-scale compatibility subsumes harmonicity as a predictor of pleas-
antness suggests that listeners respond to interval combinations they frequently en-
counter in music, rather than solely to spectral properties. The Eigenspace captures
the spectral component without presupposing a scale system; the cultural component
may account for the deviations.
7.2. Listening Test
7.2.1. Part 1 - Chord Dissonance
The category-level ordering largely held: Consonant chords were perceived as least
dissonant (M = 3.37), followed by Intermediate (M = 4.89). However, two discrepancies
are notable. Neutral chords were perceived as the most dissonant category (M = 6.40),
substantially above their Eigenspace value (scaled = 5.28), while Diminished chords
(M = 5.61) fell well below theirs (scaled = 7.21). Dominant chords showed a milder
mismatch: perceived dissonance (M = 5.06) exceeded the Eigenspace value (scaled =
4.66). These deviations suggest that familiarity plays a role; listeners may penalise
chord qualities that lack any approximation in 12-TET (such as neutral intervals)
more than sensory roughness alone accounts for, while qualities closer to familiar tonal
functions (such as dominant-type chords) may carry cultural expectations that inflate
or deflate dissonance judgements independently of spectral roughness. For example,
the m7 and maj7 are perceived with lower dissonance than their Eigenspace values.
Inter-rater agreement was highest for chords closest to 12-TET equivalents (m7, SD
= 1.57; vMvM7, SD = 1.74) and lowest for vMˆm7 (Dominant; SD = 2.79) and ø7
(Diminished; SD = 2.69). This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that listeners
share perceptual anchors for chord qualities they can map onto prior tonal experi-
ence, while chords occupying ambiguous territory between familiar categories produce
greater individual variation.
Neutral qualities are in both Part 1 and Part 2 of the test perceived as dissonant,

with the particularity that smsm7 is the chord with the highest dissonance scores.
The Eigenspace shows that smsm7 is a higher-dissonance node compared to the coun-
terpart ˆmˆm7, which is the closest node to local-minimum 3. From the listening
perception report, listeners perceive locality in both chords ˆmˆm7 →local-minimum
3 and vMvM7 →local-minimum 1. As shown in Table 6 and Figure 7, both consonant
chords sit virtually on top of their closest LM: ˆmˆm7 →LM-3 (dist = 0.0015) and
vMvM7 →LM-1 (dist = 0.0016). Listeners perceive locality here: these are the two
most consonant chords. In contrast, smsm7 →LM-10 (dist = 0.0530) is much farther
from its closest minimum than ˆmˆm7 is from LM-3, and LM-10 has a higher disso-
nance (D = 12.95) compared to LM-3 (D = 11.60) or LM-1 (D = 11.73). Overall,
consonant chords sit at a distance < 0.002 from their closest LM, while neutral, less
consonant, and diminished chords lie 0.03–0.07 away, on the slopes, not at the bottom
of the valleys.
Per-chord mean perceived dissonance ratings were compared against the dissonance
values computed in the Eigenspace. Both correlation estimates are positive and their
intervals exclude zero (Spearman ρ = .481, p = .037, approximate 95% CI [.01, .78];
Pearson r = .569, p = .011, 95% CI [.15, .81]; n = 19 chords), but the intervals
are wide: nineteen chords support the direction of the association while leaving its
magnitude undetermined, and we read the result as preliminary evidence. These were
single planned comparisons, so no correction for multiple testing was applied. The
Eigenspace category-level ordering (Consonant = 2.15, Intermediate = 4.54, Dominant
= 4.66, Neutral = 5.28, Less Consonant = 5.52, Diminished = 7.21) broadly aligned
with participants’ perceived dissonance ordering, though Dominant chords showed a
notable discrepancy, perceived as more dissonant than their Eigenspace values indicate.
These deviations have more than one possible source: cultural familiarity, the timbre
of the synthesised stimuli (sine and triangle waveforms), and the order of presentation.
The study is correlational and cannot tell these sources apart.
7.2.2. Part 2 - Chord Progressions
In Part 2, the overall mean ratings across the four dimensions were: coherence (M =
6.86), musicality (M = 6.43), novelty (M = 6.12), and dissonance (M = 5.60). These
values indicate that listeners generally perceived the 53-TET progressions as coherent
and musical, while also recognising them as novel.
Musicality as a concept encompasses creativity, cultural practice, rhythm, aesthet-
ics, and performance, among other dimensions. The musicality ratings reported here do
not attempt to capture the scope of musicality; they specifically measure the listener’s
perceptual tolerance for microtonal chord progressions. Musicality ratings varied sub-
stantially across progressions, ranging from M = 4.23 (Progression 4, Neutral tonic)
to M = 8.00 (Progression 5, Dominant tonic). The three Major-tonic progressions (1,
3, 10) and the three Dominant-tonic progressions (5, 7, 9) received the highest musi-
cality ratings, while the two Neutral-tonic progressions (2, 4) received the lowest. This
ordering suggests that progressions built on tonic chords with closer approximations
to 12-TET qualities (major and dominant) were perceived as more musical than those
built on neutral chord types, which have no 12-TET counterpart.
The inter-dimension correlation analysis revealed consistent patterns across all four
chord types. Coherence and musicality were positively correlated in every group (Ma-
jor: ρ = .784; Dominant: ρ = .691; Minor: ρ = .652; Neutral: ρ = .467), indicating that
listeners who found a progression coherent also tended to rate it as musical. Dissonance
was negatively correlated with both coherence and musicality across all types, con-
26


sistent with lower perceived musical quality at higher perceived dissonance. Novelty
and musicality correlated positively for Major (ρ = .395) and Dominant (ρ = .477)
types, and negligibly for Minor (ρ = .140), suggesting that for the more familiar-
sounding tonic qualities, novelty was experienced as a positive musical attribute. The
Neutral type was the exception: novelty and musicality were negatively (though not
significantly) correlated (ρ = −.247), and novelty was positively correlated with dis-
sonance (ρ = .410), suggesting that for progressions built on the most unfamiliar
tonic quality, novelty and dissonance became perceptually entangled. The ratings are
descriptive measures of acceptance/tolerance of first-encounter microtonal chord pro-
gressions, with no applicable reference, shaped by the listeners’ cultural background,
and we read them as exploratory. That listeners nonetheless differentiated degrees of
novelty among uniformly unheard materials is itself part of the result.
7.3. Limitations and Future Work
The listening study has several limitations. The sample was small and uneven in gen-
der composition; prior experience with microtonal music was recorded in the entry
questionnaire but was not entered as a covariate, so the familiarity-based interpre-
tations above remain tentative. The dissonance scale was anchored to two reference
chords (maj6 as 1 and M+sm7 as 10); this practical choice constrains the ratings,
which should not be read as independent of the chosen anchors. Finally, the reported
correlations carry wide confidence intervals over 19 chords, and Part 2 is a reference-
free, first-encounter design, so the perceptual results are exploratory and descriptive
throughout.
The Eigenspace is computed here for harmonic timbres, where overtone frequencies
are integer multiples of the fundamental. This is the overtone structure of strings and
pipes, and the reason the LM align with just-intonation intervals. The dissonance func-
tion, however, is not restricted to this case. Instruments with inharmonic spectra (bells,
metallophones, bars) produce entirely different dissonance topologies, and musical tra-
ditions built around such instruments use scales and intervals that correspond to those
different surfaces. Computing the Eigenspace for inharmonic timbres would produce
a different set of LM, different basins of reference, and a different chord vocabulary.
The mathematical structure of the Eigenspace applies unchanged to any spectrum:
the coordinates, the dissonance field, and the extraction of minima take the partials
as input, and the harmonic content revealed is determined entirely by the timbre. This
observation points toward a broader programme: a family of Eigenspaces, each gen-
erated by a different spectral profile, mapping the perceptual harmonic possibilities
specific to each timbral world. Recent work by Etxebarria Ecenarro (2025) demon-
strate this concretely for the one-dimensional case: dissonance curves computed from
the spectra of ancient idiophones and aerophones recover the scales of Thai classical
music and Basque traditional practice, confirming that the relationship between tim-
bre and tuning holds across culturally independent musical traditions. Extending this
analysis to the four-dimensional tetrad case would produce a family of Eigenspaces,
each mapping the harmonic possibilities specific to a given timbral world.
Such a programme would ground comparative musicology in a common ana-
lytical framework, connecting tuning systems that appear unrelated when viewed
through the lens of Western tonal theory but that may share structural features when
viewed through the lens of their respective dissonance topologies. Within the present
Eigenspace, the chord vocabulary itself will grow: applying the ten-fold quality gra-
27


dation to the fifth, as it already applies to the third and the seventh, completes the
combinatorial family and is planned for the application. The coordinate system also
lends itself to a spatial grounding of chord quality for machine-learning models of
microtonal harmony; a companion study (anonymous) pursues this direction.
