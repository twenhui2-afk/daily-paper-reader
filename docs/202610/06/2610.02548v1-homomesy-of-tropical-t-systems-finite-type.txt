HOMOMESY OF TROPICAL T-SYSTEMS: FINITE TYPE
ARIANA CHIN AND PAVLO PYLYAVSKYY
Abstract. In 2007, Fomin and Zelevinsky introduced the bipartite belt, a sequence of
bipartite cluster mutations whose exchange relations form a discrete dynamical system. For
each Dynkin diagram, this system is periodic, as a special case of Zamolodchikov periodicity.
In the associated tropical dynamics, every mutation along the belt can be naturally colored
red or blue, according to which term attains the maximum in the tropical exchange relation.
This gives a red-blue statistic on tropical orbits. We prove that this statistic is homomesic,
i.e.
that the average numbers of red and blue mutations are independent of the orbit.
Moreover, the number of red mutations is given by the number of roots in the associated
root system. This resolves a conjecture of the first author. In the process we introduce the
Zamolodchikov fan, which is closely related to the positive tropical Grassmannian, and may
be of independent interest.

1.3. Homomesy and the main theorem. Homomesy is a phenomenon introduced by
Propp and Roby in [PR15]. Let S be a set, and consider a bijective map τ : S →S such
that every τ-orbit is finite. Let f : S →R be a statistic. We say that f is homomesic under
τ if there exists a constant c ∈R such that for every τ-orbit O ⊂S,

In other words, although the values of f may vary along an orbit, their average is the same
for every orbit. Homomesy has been studied in promotion and rowmotion, see [PR15, EP21].
For a quiver Q, consider the set
S = R|V (Q)|
of possible states of the tropical T-system, and define
τ = µ◦µ• : S →S.
Thus one application of τ consists of mutating first at all black vertices and then at all white
vertices. Although S is infinite, periodicity of the tropical T-system implies that τ has finite
order, and hence every τ-orbit is finite.
Now suppose that Q is the tensor product of two Dynkin quivers Γ and ∆. Each tropical
mutation can be assigned one of two colors. We call the mutation red if the maximum in the
tropical exchange relation is attained by the term coming from the Γ-direction, and blue if
it is attained by the term coming from the ∆-direction. For s ∈S, let f(s) be the number
of red mutations that occur during the next application of τ.
The following conjecture was observed in [Chi25], and is equivalent to the statement that
f is homomesic under τ.
Conjecture 1.2 (Chin). Let (Γ, ∆) be a Zamolodchikov periodic cluster algebra of rank n,
and let hΓ be the associated Coxeter number to Γ. In one period of the corresponding tropical
T-system, the number of red mutations is hΓ · n.
In this paper we prove this conjecture when one of the two Dynkin quivers consists of a
single vertex. Equivalently, we prove the following.
Theorem 1.3. Let Λ be a Dynkin diagram of rank n. In one period of the corresponding
tropical T-system, the number of red mutations is the number of roots in the associated root
system of Λ.

1.4. Zamolodchikov fan. In the process of proving Theorem 1.3, we introduce the notion
of a Zamolodchikov fan, which we believe to be of independent interest. Recall that tropical
mutation gives piecewise-linear maps on R|V (Q)|.
Restricting to the mutation sequences
appearing in the bipartite belt, we consider the common refinement of their domains of
linearity. We call the resulting fan Z the Zamolodchikov fan.
Equivalently, its cones are the maximal regions of initial conditions for which every tropical
mutation along the bipartite belt makes the same choice in each max. Thus, when Q = Γ⊗∆,
the red/blue mutation pattern is constant on each cone of the Zamolodchikov fan.
Tensor products of type A quivers appear naturally in the cluster algebra structure on
Grassmannians. In particular, the quiver
Ak−1 ⊗An−k−1
appears as a rectangular seed for Gr(k, n). This connection goes back, in a different lan-
guage, to Volkov’s proof of Zamolodchikov periodicity for pairs of type A Dynkin diagrams
[Vol07]. His argument uses projective geometry rather than cluster algebras, but the un-
derlying Grassmannian picture is essentially the same.
The cluster algebra structure on
Grassmannians was developed systematically by Scott [Sco06], relying on Postnikov’s work
on the totally nonnegative Grassmannian for its combinatorial framework [Pos06].
The positive tropical Grassmannian Trop+ G(k, n) was introduced by Speyer and Williams
[SW05]. For k = 2 it is parametrized by planar trees with n labeled leaves, whose edges
have nonnegative lengths and whose planar embedding is compatible with the cyclic order
1, 2, . . . , n. Dually, its cones are indexed by collections of pairwise noncrossing diagonals of
an n-gon. In particular, Trop+ G(2, n) is combinatorially the normal fan of the type An−3
associahedron.
More generally, one can associate a mutation fan to any collection of cluster mutations
by taking the common refinement of their domains of linearity.
For G(k, n), taking all
mutations between Pl¨ucker clusters gives a natural Pl¨ucker mutation fan.
By the weak
separation description of Pl¨ucker clusters due to Oh, Postnikov, and Speyer [OPS15], every
short Pl¨ucker relation occurs as such a mutation. Hence the Pl¨ucker mutation fan is precisely
the positive Dressian, which, by a theorem of Speyer and Williams [SW21], coincides with
Trop+ G(k, n).
For G(2, n), the smaller collection of mutations appearing on the bipartite belt already
recovers the whole fan.
Theorem 1.4. The Zamolodchikov fan associated with G(2, n) coincides with the positive
tropical Grassmannian Trop+ G(2, n).
Already for G(3, 6) the two fans are different however, see Remark 4.5.

2.4. Cluster algebras of type Dn: tagged triangulations of a punctured n-gon.
Tagged triangulations were first defined on marked surfaces in [FST08]. The following defi-
nition uses tagged triangulations only in the context of once-punctured polygons. For more
details, we refer the reader to [FWZ17, Section 5.4].
A once-punctured n-gon contains n + 1 marked points: n vertices on the boundary, and
one puncture p. An arc is a non-selfintersecting curve γij whose endpoints i, j are distinct
marked points, and which does not cut out an unpunctured bigon. An arc incident to the
puncture is called a radius. A tagged arc is either a regular non-radius arc, or a radius that
has been either tagged or left untagged.
Two arcs γ, γ′ are compatible if they do not cross, with the following exception: two radii
with distinct endpoints are incompatible if they are tagged differently. A tagged triangulation
is a maximal (by inclusion) collection of pairwise compatible tagged arcs.
A cluster algebra of type Dn can be geometrically realized by tagged triangulations of a
punctured n-gon (see Figure 1). The clusters are in bijection with tagged triangulations of

2.5. Laminations. Lamination spaces were first introduced by William Thurston in his
work on the geometry of 3-manifolds [Thu02], and were later shown to have connections
to Teichm¨uller theory by Fock and Goncharov [FG07]. Laminations on marked surfaces in
the context of cluster algebras were introduced by Fomin and Dylan Thurston [FT18]. A
lamination L is a finite collection of pairwise nonintersecting curves on the surface, considered
up to isotopy, where two radii that share a puncture are considered to be intersecting. Two
curves are compatible if they can appear in the same lamination L (i.e. are not intersecting).
Curves either connect to unmarked points on the boundary of the surface or spiral into a
puncture. We depict a curve that spirals clockwise into a puncture as an untagged radius,
and a curve that spirals counterclockwise as a tagged radius.

3. The Zamolodchikov fan
Given a B-matrix B with an initial labeling λ ∈Rm of the associated tropical T-system,
one period of mutations will generically produce some color sequence of red and blue mu-
tations. We can naturally visualize the space of initial labelings λ ∈Rm as a complete fan
whose cones correspond to the sequence of colored mutations produced by λ. This Zamolod-
chikov fan ZB (or just Z) is equivalent to the common refinement of the domains of linearity
of the tropical mutation maps appearing along the bipartite belt of B. The cones are com-
mon domains of linearity, and are separated by codimension 1 walls of non-linearity. Any
point on a wall of Z represents a nongeneric initial tropical labeling (i.e. contains a mutation
that is neither blue nor red), whereas a point inside a chamber of Z represents a generic
tropical labeling. In the Grassmannian case B = Ak−1 ⊗An−k−1, we write this fan as Zk,n.
An example of Z2,n is depicted in Figure 2.
One may define a slight generalization of the Zamolodchikov fan by allowing all tropical
mutation sequences. The resulting tropical mutation fan is the complete fan whose cones
are common domains of linearity of λ ∈Rm over all tropical mutation sequences.
A related fan introduced in [Rea14] is the mutation fan, defined as follows. Let B be
the (m + 1) × n matrix obtained from the B-matrix associated to the cluster algebra by
appending an additional row⃗a to the bottom of it. Each mutation sequence µk defines a
piecewise linear function defined as
fk : Rn →Rn
(a1, . . . , an) 7→(µk(B)m+1,1, . . . , µk(B)m+1,n) .
The mutation fan is the complete fan whose cones consist of the common domains of linearity
of fk across all mutation sequences k. This is equivalent to tracking the common domains
of linearity in the associated Y -system across all mutation sequences k. When restricted to
only mutations in the bipartite belt, we call this the Zamolodchikov mutation fan.

Proposition 4.4. Let B be the extended exchange matrix associated to the bipartite orien-
tation of An with triangulation coefficients. In one period, there are n(n + 1) red mutations
and 2n blue mutations.
Proof. By [Chi26], the behavior of the first half-period is equivalent to the behavior of the

each cluster variable in finite type appears exactly once in each half-period. Every mutation

Corollary 5.6. Let B be the full rank exchange matrix associated to the bipartite orientation
of Bn with tagged triangulation coefficients. In one period, there are 2n2 red mutations and
2n blue mutations.
Proof. As mentioned in the above proof of Proposition 5.5, the type Dn+1 laminations are
already foldable into type Bn, and the number of maximal laminations matches the correct
number of type Bn seeds.
We need only check that each of these maximal laminations
produces the correct color count for type Bn.
Identify the two cluster variable radii together, as they become folded together in type
Bn. We can now view mutating the radii together as one combined mutation, as they will
always be the same color. Now count the number of blue mutations. A maximal lamination
consists of 2 radii and n−1 full curves. Every non-radius lamination curve is blue for exactly
one quadrilateral, which contributes one blue mutation. And the two lamination radii are
blue for the same bigon which encompasses both cluster variable radii, contributing one blue
mutation. Thus, the total number of blue mutations in one half period is n.
□
6. Types E6, E7, E8, F4, and G2
The exceptional types E6, E7, E8, F4, and G2 have been checked via a computer search as
follows:
(1) Add coefficients to make the B-matrix full rank.
(2) By Proposition 3.4, the number of cones in Z is cΛ, the number of seeds in the cluster
algebra of type Λ.
(3) Randomly sample λ, an initial tropical labeling from the rational numbers, and ex-
ecute one period of mutations in the bipartite belt, coloring each mutation red or
blue.
(4) If the labeling is generic (every mutation is colored), verify the number of blue mu-
tations is 2n, then store the color sequence in a dictionary.
(5) Repeat from step (3) until cΛ different color sequences are discovered.
See Figure 14 for the number of cones for each exceptional type.

Remark 6.1. The original conjecture 1.2 was stated in the hopes of a bijective proof between
roots and red mutations. For types An, Bn, Cn, and Dn, the red mutations can be understood
from the root system perspective as follows. Each mutation quadrilateral appears exactly
once in each half-period, and contains a unique nontrivial lamination curve. A maximal
lamination L is a (tagged) triangulation, which can be viewed as a cluster in the associated

cluster algebra. Then all of the nontrivial lamination curves in L are cluster variables in
the initial seed, which also correspond to the blue mutation quadrilaterals. Every nontrivial
lamination curve not in L corresponds to a red mutation quadrilateral, and a cluster variable
not in the initial seed. Due to [FZ03b], there is a natural bijection between cluster variables
outside of the initial seed and the positive roots in the root system, given by the denominator
vectors. Thus once a maximal lamination has been fixed, there is a natural bijection between
positive roots and red mutation quadrilaterals in the first half-period. The second half-period
can then be interpreted to represent the corresponding negative roots. While the conjecture
has also been proved computationally for the exceptional types (E6, E7, E8, F4, G2), we were
unable to find an analogous bijection.
