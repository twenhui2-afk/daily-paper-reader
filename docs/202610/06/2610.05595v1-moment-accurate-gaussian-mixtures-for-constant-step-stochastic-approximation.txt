# ABSTRACT 

Local Gaussian models of constant-step learning predict output variability and ex-pected losses, but weak convergence alone does not justify these moment predic-tions. We establish moment-accurate Gaussian mixtures by matching stationary energy with local Ornstein–Uhlenbeck limits, ruling out quadratic tail mass invis-ible to weak convergence. For step size a, the second-order Wasserstein error is 

o(√a), uniformly over invariant laws, using each law’s actual root weights. The assumptions combine confinement, descent, finitely many hyperbolic equilibria and root continuity with finite-variance innovations. The result yields observable covariances, expected objective gaps and first-order mean shifts, while allowing singular covariances, compatible saddles and weights without a limit. For additive noise given by a fixed invertible transform of independent standardized Student t3

coordinates, symmetry gives an order-sharp √a smooth-test bound. Numerical transport calculations demonstrate the value of root-specific covariances; con-trolled SGD studies assess observable predictions across step sizes, batch sizes and model geometries. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

With a fixed learning rate a, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) keeps fluctuating near a trained solution. Local Gaussian models turn curvature and gradient-noise covariance into predictions of output variability and expected objective gaps (Mandt et al., 2017; Yaida, 2019; Liu et al., 2021). These predictions concern moments, so their validity requires more than a Gaussian description of typical fluctuations. When does local Gaussian theory accurately predict these quantities in a landscape with several equilibria? The first obstacle is that weak convergence can hide the very quantities these predictions require. As 

R → ∞ , (1 − R−2)N(0 , 1) + R−2δR converges weakly to N(0 , 1) , while its second moment tends to 2. A vanishing fraction of observations can therefore carry a nonvanishing share of quadratic energy. Single-center moment results address this difficulty under their respective stability and regularity conditions (Dieuleveut et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2025). Multiple equilibria introduce a second obstacle: their local covariances differ, while their occupation weights depend on global dynamics and the selected invariant law. Relation to existing theory. Classical constant-gain analyses establish stationary asymptotics (Kushner & Huang, 1981; Pflug, 1986; Kushner & Shwartz, 1984; Kushner & Yin, 2003). The finite-variance single-center results of Chen et al. (2022) identify scaled limits through an integral equation and Gaussian uniqueness conditions. Further single-center analyses establish moment ex-pansions, Gaussian approximation and stationary concentration (Dieuleveut et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Merad & Gaïffas, 2025); Table 1 compares their assumptions and conclu-sions. Our additional step is uniform quadratic-tail control across all roots using second innovation moments and root continuity. Finite-time W1 bounds with recursively defined Gaussian covariances address a complementary time scale (Haque et al., 2026). Nonconvex SA theory characterizes invariant support, saddle exclusion and nonunique invariant laws (Fort & Pagès, 1999; Benaïm, 1996; 1999; Bianchi et al., 2022; Shirokoff & Zaleski, 2025). We resolve moments within the root-labeled components; temporal-average normality addresses a different random object (Yu et al., 2021). The covariance, quarter-trace loss and bias formulas have single-center antecedents (Yaida, 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Dieuleveut et al., 2020; Allmeier & Gast, 2024); energy matching justifies their rootwise use with low-moment remainders. Occupation weights instead use global communication information, as in light- and heavy-tail metastability theory (Freidlin & Wentzell, 2012; Azizian et al., 2024; Wang & Rhee, 2023). Diffusion and modified equations describe finite-step dynamics (Li et al., 2017; 2021); stationary approximation requires control through the infinite-time limit, as provided by Poisson methods un-der their assumptions (Mattingly et al., 2010). Our energy argument works directly with discrete invariant laws. Multiplicative noise links innovation and iterate tails (Gürbüzbalaban et al., 2021; Hodgkinson & Mahoney, 2021), while equilibrium manifolds add tangential dynamics (Li et al., 2022). Corollary 5.1 (Additive-noise accuracy) . Under Assumption 2.1, centered additive innovations with a finite (2 + δ) moment give Ea,j = O(aδ/ 2) at every stable root. For a fixed invertible linear transform of d independent standardized t3 coordinates, Ea,j = O(√a) on H2,1.

Despite infinite third absolute moments, the t3 bound follows from symmetry and OU smoothing; invertibility ensures Cj ≻ 0 (Corollary H.3). In the scalar linear example, the same cosine test has error O(a) for Gaussian or Rademacher innovations and order √a for t3 noise (Proposition H.4). For state-dependent noise, a uniformly bounded local (2 + δ) moment and a locally β-Hölder covariance give Ea,j = O(aδ/ 2 + aβ/ 2 + √a) at stable roots, for 0 < δ, β ≤ 1 (Theorem F.1). # 7 DISCUSSION 

Stationary energy matching turns local Gaussian limits into reliable moment asymptotics across multiple equilibria. It gives covariance, loss and mean-shift formulas a common foundation under second innovation moments, separating local coefficients from global occupation weights. The heterogeneous-root comparison links this distinction directly to transport: even the best com-mon covariance retains a positive limiting same-label cost. In neural SGD, matched OU paths calibrate finite-trajectory variability, while step-size and batch-size comparisons identify departures from the local moment predictions. The present theory concerns stationary laws near finite hyperbolic roots. Extending energy match-ing to equilibrium manifolds would connect normal moment control with tangential dynamics (Li et al., 2022). Quantitative W2 bounds and equilibration estimates would connect these stationary predictions to prescribed training horizons. 9Preprint

# V RELATION TO DIFFUSION , HEAVY -TAIL AND MANIFOLD THEORIES 

The random object and order of limits. The object here is an arbitrary invariant law of the dis-crete recursion at step a, followed by a ↓ 0. The additive finite-variance analysis of Chen et al. (2022) addresses this order of limits around a single center. Its strongly convex, linear and contrac-tive settings lead to stationary scaling limits and Gaussian identification under the stated uniqueness conditions. Our assumptions instead permit multiple hyperbolic roots and state-dependent innova-tions. Energy matching establishes the quadratic uniform integrability needed for moment accuracy across all components and invariant-law selections. With a uniform scaled third moment, a Gaussian weak limit already implies qualitative W2 convergence; energy matching supplies the required tail control using second innovation moments instead. Classical stochastic approximation distinguishes 52 Preprint this constant-gain problem from diminishing-gain convergence (Kushner & Yin, 2003). Stochas-tic modified equations approximate the weak dynamics of SGD over specified time intervals and support algorithmic interpretations (Li et al., 2017). An approximation on a fixed interval does not by itself identify a stationary limit: passing to infinite time requires additional uniform control. Mattingly et al. (2010) develop Poisson-equation methods for time averages and invariant-measure errors of numerical SDE approximations under smoothness and ergodicity conditions. Our proof instead uses the discrete stationary energy balance to obtain quadratic uniform integrability under second innovation moments, with potentially several invariant probabilities. The Poisson equation enters later, for the separate smooth-test rate. The finite-step study of Li et al. (2021) further mo-tivates checking the accuracy of diffusion descriptions at the actual learning rate. Our matched OU trajectories calibrate the variability of local moment measurements; the nonlinear trajectories independently measure their departures from the limiting prediction. 

Heavy-tailed innovations and heavy-tailed iterates. Gürbüzbalaban et al. (2021) show how multiplicative SGD noise can produce heavy-tailed stationary iterates even with light-tailed data; Hodgkinson & Mahoney (2021) develop related mechanisms beyond linear models. Innovation moments and stationary-iterate moments are therefore distinct assumptions. We assume finite in-novation variance with covariance growth controlled by the drift, together with dissipativity and descent. For sufficiently small steps these conditions imply finite stationary second moments, and energy matching controls the second moments after rootwise rescaling. They allow the standard-ized t3 examples, whose third absolute moment is infinite. The symmetric smooth-test argument exploits this particular innovation structure; it does not assert a universal √a Wasserstein rate or cover stationary infinite-variance regimes. 

Local geometry and global communication. At isolated hyperbolic roots, the local covariance follows a Lyapunov equation and the mean shift follows a second-order drift balance. The new guar-antee is that these local formulas remain moment-accurate across root-labeled components, with ac-tual weights, singular covariances and second innovation moments. Single-center stationary expan-sions and harmonic loss identities provide the corresponding antecedents (Dieuleveut et al., 2020; Yaida, 2019; Allmeier & Gast, 2024). Equilibrium manifolds have additional tangential dynamics: Li et al. (2022) study how SGD moves along a zero-loss manifold under general noise covariance. Such motion is absent from a finite set of hyperbolic roots, so a manifold extension would require an additional limit along the equilibrium set. Separately, Freidlin–Wentzell theory concerns global rare transitions in small-noise diffusions (Freidlin & Wentzell, 2012), while discrete learning dynam-ics and heavy-tail communication require their own transition analysis (Azizian et al., 2024; Wang & Rhee, 2023). The invariant-rectangle example retains arbitrary weights, and the scalar commu-nicating example uses a heavy-tail transition proxy. Both use the same principle: local moment coefficients and global occupation probabilities carry different information. 53
