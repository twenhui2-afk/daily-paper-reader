Abstract 

Market makers are fundamental actors in today’s economy, as they provide liquidity by accelerating the matching between demand and supply. Accordingly, sequential market making 

models the problem of an agent who repeatedly interacts with a population of buyers and sellers. In each round, the market maker simultaneously posts a bid price and an ask price to a buyer-seller pair. A trade occurs whenever the posted prices meet the agents’ valuations: the buyer buys if their valuation is at least the ask price, and the seller sells if their valuation is at most the bid price. The two trades occur independently, so the market maker may sell without buying (short selling), buy without selling, or observe no trade at all. Crucially, the market maker must satisfy inventory constraints : over the entire horizon, the total number of bought and sold items should balance out, while during the process the inventory must remain sufficiently small, thus avoiding excessive accumulation of items and risky short-selling exposure. We study this problem through the lens of regret minimization , when buyers’ and sellers’ valuations are sampled from unknown distributions. In this setting, the market maker (that is, the learner) sequentially posts bids and asks so as to maximize its cumulative revenue over T ∈ N>0

interactions while satisfying the inventory constraints. Specifically, the goal is to minimize the difference between the learner’s performance and that of the optimal inventory-aware bid-ask pair that knows the valuation distributions ( i.e. , the cumulative regret ). We first study the hardest scenario, where the learner only observes whether each trade (buy and/or sell) occurs. In this case, we provide a computationally efficient algorithm attaining eO(T 2/3) regret, thus matching the well-known lower bound for the easier dynamic pricing setting of order Ω( T 2/3) [KL03], while satisfying the global inventory constraints and keeping the inventory at every round below 

eO(T 2/3) items. We then study the more interesting, and arguably more realistic, setting where the market maker has access to a Limit Order Book (LOB). Accordingly, the learner observes the realized valuation whenever the associated trade does not occur; otherwise, it only observes that the trade has occurred. In this case, we provide a computationally efficient algorithm that attains eO(√T ) regret, satisfies the global inventory constraints, and keeps the inventory below 

eO(√T ) items at every round. We complement these results by providing instance-dependent bounds that improve according to the c.d.f. value associated with the discretized optimum. We conclude by establishing a lower bound showing that any algorithm that guarantees per-round inventory of at most O(T β ) must incur regret of at least Ω( T 1−β ) for every β < 1/2, even in the significantly easier setting where the agent knows the valuation distributions.

21 Introduction 

Market making is a foundational part of modern financial markets [GM85, AS08]. At a basic level, market makers sustain trading by continuously offering to buy and sell, thereby providing the immediacy and liquidity that make markets usable in the first place. In the absence of such intermediation, even modest imbalances between buyers and sellers could translate into large delays, wide spreads, and severe price dislocations. This role is not minor and in today’s electronic markets, liquidity provision is carried out at a massive scale by highly sophisticated firms, whose business is precisely to keep markets tradable under a broad range of conditions. Market making is therefore not merely a financial activity, but a central problem in market design , algorithms , and online decision-making [MMR24, CBCC +25]: a market maker must repeatedly quote prices in an uncertain environment, balancing the short-term opportunity to earn spread revenue with the longer-term need to remain resilient as order flow evolves. What makes this problem especially subtle is that liquidity provision is inseparable from risk-taking [GLFT13]. A market maker does not simply post prices; it absorbs temporary supply–demand imbalances by taking them on directly. For this reason, 

inventory is not a secondary accounting quantity, but one of the key forces governing the entire process: without controlling inventory, apparent revenue gains may reflect unsustainable exposure rather than economic performance. More broadly, market making is closely connected to other well-studied problems in revenue maximization , such as dynamic pricing [KL03] and bilateral trade [CLM26]. As in dynamic pricing, the seller repeatedly sets prices in the presence of uncertain and evolving demand, with the goal of learning how prices affect trade and revenue over time. At the same time, market making also has a two-sided nature that brings it closer to bilateral trade settings, since the intermediary interacts with both buyers and sellers and must jointly manage the terms at which it is willing to trade on each side of the market. There is, however, an important difference. In standard bilateral trade models, trade is often viewed as a more localized interaction between the two sides. In market making, by contrast, trades may occur in only one direction for extended periods, as long as the intermediary is able to absorb the resulting imbalance. This makes inventory central to the problem: the market maker can keep trading on one side only as long as its inventory remains within acceptable bounds. For this reason, market making is not simply a two-sided pricing problem, but a sequential revenue maximization problem in which pricing, trade, and inventory are tightly linked across time. Thus, market making shares important features with classical revenue maximization problems, but differs in that current pricing decisions directly shape future feasible actions through their effect on inventory. These connections also point naturally to the framework of regret minimization and online learn-ing [CBL06, Ora19, Sli19]. In market making, as in other sequential revenue maximization problems, the distributions governing buyers and sellers are typically unknown to the maker. The market maker must therefore learn how prices affect trade while continuing to make sequential decisions, without knowing in advance which pricing policy maximizes revenue. Regret minimization cap-tures this objective by comparing the cumulative revenue of the algorithm to that of the best benchmark policy computed knowing the valuation distributions of both buyers and sellers (we refer to this difference as the cumulative regret ). This viewpoint is standard in related problems such as dynamic pricing and bilateral trade , where the central challenge is to balance exploration and revenue over time (or in welfare-maximization settings, to balance exploration and social welfare) [BZ09, BDKS15, BCBCC24, GDFS25]. It is thus a natural framework for studying market making in uncertain environments. 31.1 The Model

1.2 Related Work 

In this section, we discuss the work most closely related to ours. 

Market making Market making has also been studied from a mechanism-design perspective ( e.g., 

see [MMR24]), where the mechanism must provide an optimal allocation and an incentive-compatible payment rule, assuming access to both buyers’ and sellers’ valuation distributions. Our work is mainly related to sequential market making [AK13, XDX25], which studies the performance of a regret-minimization algorithm against a finite class of constant-spread dynamic strategies. Our results are not directly comparable to theirs, since we operate in a continuous bid–ask decision space and do not assume full feedback. The work most closely related to ours is [CBCC +25]. In this work, the authors study an online market-making setting with a single per-round taker in which the reward attained by the market maker (the agent) is given by the difference between the bid (or ask) and the unknown market value of the item. They provide optimal regret bounds under various assumptions on the nature of the environment, assuming that the market maker observes the market value and a one-bit feedback signal on the trade at the end of each round. While [CBCC +25] provides a foundational and technically very strong regret-minimization treatment of market making, our setting captures two features that are central in practice and that substantially change the nature of the problem. First, unlike [CBCC +25], which assumes the existence of a market value relating the rewards associated with sold items to those associated with purchased items, we explicitly model inventory constraints. As a result, in our setting, actions taken in early rounds affect the feasibility and profitability of the decisions in later rounds, posing additional and unique technical challenges. Second, we study different feedback models. We consider two-bit feedback—which is the natural minimal model in our setting, since buyers and sellers are treated separately, as in real-world market making—and we also study a more realistic partial-information model motivated by the limit order book . In this sense, we believe that our framework is arguably closer to the informational and operational constraints faced by actual market makers. The limit order book 

feedback was originally introduced in [MR26] and then further studied by [VSM26], who consider the market-value-based setting of [CBCC +25] while allowing full feedback when no trade occurs. Despite the shared motivation for the feedback model, the underlying setting differs substantially from ours since our explicit inventory constraints make the feasibility and profitability of future 5decisions depend on past trades. 

Online learning in economic settings and bilateral trade Regret minimization and online learning methods have become a standard toolkit for studying digital markets and economic de-sign under uncertainty, replacing strong prior assumptions with robust online guarantees. These techniques have been applied to one-sided and dynamic pricing [KL03, BDKS15, FKL +16], re-peated auctions [CGM15, CCC +24, MR15, WPR16, LST16, DS22, NCKP22, BG19], contract design [HSV16, DGSW23, ZBY +23], brokerage [BCC24b], and Bayesian persuasion [CCMG20, CCMG23, ZIX21]. A particularly relevant related line of work is repeated bilateral trade, where a learner acts as a broker and posts prices to agents with unknown private valuations, with the objective of maximizing the social welfare. Foundational results established optimal regret guarantees under i.i.d. assumptions, smoothness conditions, and budget-balance constraints [CCC +21, CBCC +24]. Subsequent work extended these guarantees to adversarial environments, that is, beyond i.i.d. assumptions, weaker budget-balance notions, and more general dependence structures between buyer and seller valuations [AFF22, CCC +23, BCCF24]. Recently, [GDFS25] studied the problem of revenue maximization in bilateral trade. Our work naturally fits within the online learning in the economic category; nonetheless, we remark the following key distinctive features between our setting and the aforementioned ones. On the one hand, our setting involves a two-sided interaction with both a buyer and a seller, which distinguishes it from standard dynamic pricing or auction settings. On the other hand, in market making, trades may occur in only one direction for extended periods, until some inventory constraints are satisfied, while in bilateral trade, a trade may happen in both directions only. 

Online learning with general and budget constraints A strongly related research direction is online learning under unknown constraints, where the objective is to design online learning algorithms that attain sublinear regret together with sublinear cumulative violation of some un-known constraints [MTY09, MYJ13, JHA16, YNW17]. A particularly influential framework in this area is that of bandits with knapsacks (BwK), introduced by [BKS18]. In BwK, the learner observes bandit feedback and faces stochastic rewards and resource consumptions, with the goal of maximizing cumulative utility while keeping the total consumption of each of the m resources within a budget B. For the stochastic setting, improved and optimal algorithms were later developed by [AD19]. The adversarial BwK version, that is, allowing the rewards and consumption to change arbitrarily during the learning dynamic, was first investigated by [ISSS22], who adopt as benchmark the best fixed distribution over arms in hindsight. Under this benchmark, standard no-regret guarantees are in general unattainable, and one must instead resort to weaker notions such as 

α-regret. If ρ denotes the learner’s per-round budget, the best currently known competitive-ratio guarantees are 1 /ρ when B = Ω( T ) [CCK22], and O(log m log T ) in the general case [KS20]. More recently, [KK22, BCC +24a] generalized the BwK model to allow non-monotone resource dynamics, so that resources may also be replenished over time. Related in spirit, but closer to reinforcement learning [SB18] formulations, is the literature on online learning in constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs). In CMDPs [Alt99], the learner must optimize cumulative reward while enforc-ing trajectory-level constraints in an environment with unknown state dynamics. A foundational contribution in this direction is due to [EMP20], who studied the exploration–exploitation trade-off in unknown CMDPs and provided sublinear guarantees for both regret and constraint violation. More recent works extended the model to settings with adversarial losses [QWY +20] and adversarial constraints [SGG +24], while others investigated stronger safety notions that do not allow past constraint satisfaction to compensate for future violations [MAC +24, SCMG25b]. Our setting shares 6similarities with the online learning with general constraints (or even online CMDPs) framework and the replenishable budget ones. Nonetheless, the aforementioned techniques cannot be transferred directly to our problem, due to the presence of a continuous and structurally richer action space, as well as a much weaker feedback model than the one usually assumed in bandit settings. Additionally, we remark that our setting does not present any Slater-like condition for the offline optimum. 

1.3 Technical Challenges 

In this section, we discuss the main technical challenges of our setting. In particular, online market making under inventory constraints presents several unique difficulties that make the learning problem substantially challenging. 

On the action space and the reward function The action space of bid-ask pairs available to the agent during learning is continuous and two-dimensional, namely {(b, a ) ∈ [0 , 1] 2 : b < a }. This already rules out standard multi-armed bandit techniques [LS20], which are inherently tailored to discrete action spaces. At the same time, existing methods for continuum-armed bandits, such as the Zooming algorithm [KSU08, KSU19], do not apply directly to our setting for several reasons. First, in two-dimensional problems, these methods typically yield regret bounds of order T 3/4,which we show to be suboptimal in our setting. Second, our reward function is neither Lipschitz nor smooth, since we impose no regularity assumptions on the c.d.f.’s of buyers and sellers. As a result, standard discretization arguments or smoothing-based reductions cannot be applied in any straightforward way to obtain sublinear regret. We acknowledge that related, but substantially different, challenges also arise in no-regret algorithms for bilateral trade [CBCC +21]. In such settings, however, these challenges are typically overcome either by assuming regularity of the buyers’ and sellers’ c.d.f.’s [CBCC +21, CCC +23], or by leveraging the special structure of the bilateral trade objective [BCCF24]. Intuitively, that structure makes discretization considerably less challenging since trades can occur only when both sides simultaneously accept the proposed prices. 

On the inventory constraints The inventory constraints play a crucial role in our setting. Indeed, they clearly relate the bid and ask sides of the problem, preventing us from optimizing them independently. More precisely, the bid-ask pair selected in earlier rounds directly affects the inventory level carried into later ones, and therefore affects both the feasibility and the profitability of future bid-ask pairs. More generally, while the literature on online learning under unknown budget and general long-term constraints is huge [BKS18, CCM +22], our inventory constraints have a structure that does not allow for any direct application of existing techniques. First of all, the overall structure of the problem ( e.g., action and rewards space) is already substantially more involved than in standard constrained online learning models, thus preventing simple adaptation of existing techniques from the literature. Second, our constraints do not fit the canonical templates studied in the literature. On the one hand, the overall inventory requirement is closely related to replenishable-budget models [KK22, BCC +24a], but with some additional challenges: indeed, i)

both the initial budget and the final one must be 0 and ii ) more importantly, no Slater’s condition is available —that is, there is not a bid-ask pair which strictly satisfies the offline expected constraint, since it is an equality constraint, leaving no slack around the optimum. On the other hand, the 

per-round inventory constraints can be seen as any-time general constraint [CCM +22], where α is the violation rate and Slater’s condition is not available . Overall, these features make our setting significantly different from the ones considered in the existing literature, and show that handling the inventory constraints in our problem requires substantially different ideas. 7On the benchmark It is standard in the literature on online learning under unknown constraints to compare the performance of the algorithm against the expected reward attained by the optimal action, or strategy of actions, satisfying the constraints in expectation—which we denote by OPT. This is the benchmark typically considered in the existing literature, and the one for which most of the available techniques are designed [EMP20, LZK +21, CCK22, BCC +24a, CCM +22, SCMG25a]. On the feedback The feedback structure is another major source of difficulty in our problem. Under two-bit feedback, after posting a bid-ask pair, the learner only observes whether the seller accepts the bid and whether the buyer accepts the ask, while the agents’ valuations remain unobserved. Equivalently, on each side of the market, the learner only learns whether the corresponding valuation lies above or below a posted threshold, rather than observing its actual value. This provides extremely coarse information relative to the complexity of the decision space. The LOB model is also non-trivial: whenever trade occurs on one side of the market, the learner only receives a one-bit signal for that specific direction of trade. Thus, although this hybrid observation model is more informative than purely two-bit feedback, it still reveals only partial information. Notably, despite this limitation, we show that in the LOB setting, one can still attain the same optimal bounds as in the full-feedback case. We also note that even the full-feedback setting, which is not the focus of this work, is far from trivial and has been studied in several related settings [CBCC +21, CBCC +24]. We believe that this makes our results for the LOB setting particularly interesting, as it shows that the same optimal bounds can be achieved under weaker feedback.

The common difficulty is to learn profitable bid–ask pairs while keeping purchases and sales balanced. Indeed, exploration itself generates trades, and a pair with a large spread may lead to many more purchases than sales, or vice versa. 3.2 The Inventory Constraints 

Market making differs from standard buy–sell interactions between agents. In particular, market makers can sell an asset even if they have not bought it yet (this is the so-called short selling ). However, market makers usually try to keep their inventory small, meaning they try to avoid building a large imbalance between how many units they have sold and how many they have bought. The reason is simple: 

• if the maker ends up holding too much of the asset (positive inventory), they are exposed to the risk that the market price goes down; 

• if the maker ends up too short (negative inventory), they are exposed to the risk that the market price goes up. In both cases, the market maker becomes more sensitive to price movements, because part of their profit (or loss) starts depending on the market valuation of the asset, not only on the trading activity 15 itself. This is typically against the idea of market making, which (in the simplest view) aims to earn mainly from the bid–ask spread , while keeping directional price risk as low as possible. We encode this aspect by imposing the following two constraints on the learning dynamics. On the one hand, we require a global inventory balance over the whole horizon, namely:

5.1.2 Computational Complexity 

In this section, we provide the computational complexity of Algorithm 1. In the following, we refer as γt to the per-round complexity of choosing Bt, A t at each time-step t.Thus, our goal reduces to bound: Γ := max

While another common objective is the average total running time T −1 PTt=1 γt, we prefer to quantify the performance with respect to Γ for the following reasons: 

• In real-life market making, the agent must react quickly to market changes at every time step, not only on average. In an environment where every microsecond matters, even a single spike in computation time may cause the agent to miss the best opportunities. 

• From a more technical point of view, the average time complexity can be artificially reduced by using the well-known doubling trick [BK18]. This method consists in repeating the same actions over batches of length 2 n. Γ is indeed a stronger metric since it does not allow to employ this rather artificial trick. This is done by means of the following theorem. 

Theorem 5.2. The (per-round) maximum computational complexity of Algorithm 1, including the calls to Algorithms 2–3, is bounded as: 

Γ ≤ O (|B ◦||A ◦|) .

In particular, under the discretization |B ◦| = |A ◦| = ⌊T 1/3⌋, it holds Γ ≤ O (T 2/3).

Proof. We inspect the pseudocode of Algorithms 1–3. First, we observe that the initialization of Algorithm 1 requires setting B0 = B◦, A0 = A◦, and initializing the confidence bounds F up 0 (b), F low 0 (b), S up 0 (a), S low 0 (a) for all b ∈ B ◦ and a ∈ A ◦. 7.1 Algorithm 

In Algorithm 6, we provide a black-box metaprocedure that converts any algorithm with a sublinear per-round imbalance guarantee into one that additionally satisfies the global inventory constraint. Specifically, given any algorithm A whose per-round inventory imbalance is bounded by IA ,6 we execute A for the first T − IA rounds and reserve the final IA rounds for a balancing phase. By the guarantee on A , the inventory at time T − IA has magnitude at most IA , and can therefore be fully balanced over the remaining horizon. During this phase, the algorithm repeatedly selects the most inventory-increasing bid-ask pair whenever the agent is short, and the most inventory-decreasing bid-ask pair whenever the agent is long, until the total imbalance reaches zero. After that, it selects the no-trade bid-ask pair (0 , 1) for all remaining rounds. In this way, the resulting algorithm satisfies the global inventory constraint. The careful reader will notice that, in this section, we allow our algorithm to play the extreme bid-ask pairs (1 , 1) and (0 , 0), which do not satisfy the strict inequality Bt < A t. Nonetheless, this is only a notational convenience. In standard market-making models, one typically has access to prices that effectively implement the decision of not selling or not buying. Thus, these extreme actions should be interpreted as shorthand for reserve prices that deterministically prevent trade on one side while forcing it on the other.

# 8 Final Discussion 

In this paper, we study the sequential market making problem through the lens of regret minimization .Specifically, we formulate sequential market making as an online learning problem under peculiar inventory constraints. In this setting, the goal of the agent (the market maker) is two-fold. On the one hand, the agent iteratively posts bid and ask prices so as to maximize the revenue collected during the learning process. On the other hand, the agent must handle the inventory by i) guaranteeing that the number of sold items matches the number of purchased ones at the end of the learning process, and ii ) keeping the inventory as small as possible throughout learning. We study the setting in which buyers’ and sellers’ valuations are sampled at each round from fixed distributions, without making any additional assumption on the shape of the corresponding c.d.f.’s. Moreover, we consider two feedback models, which determine what the market maker observes at the end of each round. Our results can be shortly summarized as follows: 

• We first study the well-known two-bit feedback model, in which the market maker only observes whether a trade with the seller and/or with the buyer occurred. In this setting, we develop an algorithm attaining eO(T 2/3) regret and eO(T 2/3) per-round inventory bounds, while satisfying the overall inventory constraint. 

• We then turn to the LOB feedback model, where the agent has access to the limit order book ,and therefore observes the realized valuation of the buyer or seller whenever the corresponding trade does not occur. This feedback model is particularly relevant for several reasons. First, it is realistic in practice, since larger market players often have access to limit order book information. Second, as we show in this work, it allows one to achieve eO(√T ) regret and per-round inventory bounds—which we prove to be tight up to logarithmic factors—thus yielding significantly improved guarantees while still relying on a realistic feedback structure. Finally, our results show that in sequential market making, observing the complete per-round valuations at the end of the round is not necessary to get eO(√T ) guarantees. Overall, our results show that sequential market making is a technically challenging online learning problem, where limited feedback and inventory constraints interact in a non-trivial way and require substantially new ideas. 56 8.1 Open Problems
