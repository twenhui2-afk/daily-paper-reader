Abstract: In sample selection models, identifying a regression slope does not determine how accurately it can be estimated or distinguished from zero. We establish sharp uniform limits for outcome slopes in a fixed-dimensional Gaussian/probit selection model with unknown scale and an unknown covariate density bounded above and away from zero on a cube. We prove a uniform inverse bound between slope differences and observed-law Hellinger distance, allowing every nuisance parameter to differ between the two models. A matching path lies entirely within positive-correlation nonignorable selection. On parameter classes containing a feasible weak-selection neighborhood, the minimax squared slope risk is of order 𝑛 −2/3. For each prespecified minimum-signal sequence 𝑏 𝑛 , uniform exact support recovery is possible if and only if 𝑛 1/3𝑏 𝑛 → ∞ , with rules allowed to depend on that sequence. The same estimator attains uniform root-𝑛 slope accuracy on ∥𝛾 𝑠 ∥ ≤ 𝑀𝑛 −1/3

for each fixed finite 𝑀 ≥ 0, including the exact zero-selection-slope layer. A testing-based construction establishes statistical attainability without estimating the covariate density; computational efficiency is not asserted.

1. Introduction 

When an outcome is observed only for a nonrandom subset of a population, identifying its regression coefficients is only the first step toward reliable inference. A coefficient may be uniquely determined by the observed distribution and still be difficult to distinguish from zero. In Gaussian sample selection, changes in the outcome mean, noise scale, and selection mechanism can compensate one another. We ask how small an outcome slope can be estimated uniformly, and how large a nonzero slope must be for its presence to be recovered reliably. The distinction between an exact parameter layer and its neighborhood is central. At zero marginal selection slope, selection shifts the respondent intercept but leaves its outcome slopes unchanged. Our full-model estimator has uniform root-𝑛 slope accuracy on that layer, including zero correlation, and on ∥𝛾 𝑠 ∥ ≤ 𝑀𝑛 −1/3 for every fixed finite 𝑀 ≥ 0 (Corollary 4.4). Yet joint movement of selection slopes and correlation can conceal different outcome supports, giving worst-case squared slope risk of order 𝑛 −2/3 over a weak neighborhood. A related distinction between pointwise and local minimax rates appears in finite-mixture estimation (Heinrich and Kahn, 2018); here both outcome and nuisance parameters may move. We study a fixed-dimensional Gaussian/probit experiment in which the noise scale is free and bounded, and the covariate density is unknown but bounded above and away from zero on a cube. Related work. Singular sample-selection likelihoods and higher-order rates have a long history (Lee and Chesher, 1986, Sections 2.1, 5.1, and 6); see also Rotnitzky et al. (2000, Sections 4.3–4.5). Jin and Lee (2018, Section 3.1 and Appendix A) use 𝑟 = 𝜌 3 for singular likelihood estimation and group selection. At their fixed singular endpoint, non-intercept outcome slopes are root-𝑛 , the 𝑛 −1/3 rate concerns the variance parameter 𝜎 2, and the penalized group consists of selection slopes and correlation. Our support lower bound instead compares two moving endpoints with different outcome supports. Cubic and centered skew-normal coordinates also precede this analysis (Hallin and Ley, 2014, Section 3.2 and Section 6.1). Ho and Nguyen (2019) study singularity structures and coordinate-dependent rates in finite mixtures, including skew-normal mixtures. These provide geometric context for the complete selection experiment studied here. Hellinger geometry and testing-based estimation provide the methodological basis. Beckert and McFadden (2010, Propositions 1–2) relate moduli to uniform rates, with additional LAQ and estimator conditions for attainability. Our construction follows Birgé’s 𝑇 -estimator architecture (Birgé, 2006, Sections 4.2 and 5) and local metric-dimension ideas (Baraud, Birgé and Sart, 2017, Proposition 15 and Corollary 16). Do, Do and Nguyen (2025) derive conditional-law inverse bounds for regression mixtures, a different observed experiment. General conditional-distribution estimation with unknown design is treated by Baraud and Birgé (2018); conditional-integral corrections in candidate comparisons appear in Sart (2017). Here uniform density-coordinate and local packing bounds across the singular region make these testing ideas yield 𝐶 /𝑛 squared Hellinger risk, which combines with the slope geometry and matching path. Related selection procedures include adaptive Lasso under positive definite information (Ogundimu, 2022, Section 4.3, C4 and Theorems 1–2) and Bayesian spike-and-slab selection (Iqbal, Ogundimu and Rubio, 2026). Leeb and Pötscher (2008) emphasize that pointwise oracle properties need not control uniform risk; our support boundary quantifies this distinction over shrinking signals. Uniform inference near singularity is studied by Bottai (2003, Sections 3–4 and 6) for scalar models, including informative nonresponse, and by Ekvall and Bottai (2022, Theorem 2.4) under their coverage conditions. Han and McCloskey (2019) analyze extremum/Wald inference across identification strengths; their sample-selection example uses control-function moments. Ma et al. (2026a) study normal regression under a mixture of MAR and unrestricted MNAR response mechanisms. The regression result in the arXiv version (Ma et al., 2026b, version 2, Section 5, Theorem 17) uses a specified noise scale and gives a high-probability squared Euclidean error bound of order 1/log 𝑛 when the remaining parameters are fixed; its conditions also allow dimension growth. The positive MAR component imposes a lower bound on response probabilities conditional on the outcome and covariates. Our targets are uniform squared slope risk and a shrinking-separation support boundary with free scale. Section 2 specifies the experiment. Sections 3–5 develop the geometry, slope rates, and support boundary; Section 6 summarizes the restricted regimes. Section 7 illustrates the separation mechanism. 8. Discussion 

The worst-case boundary persists with known design, alongside the root-𝑛 slope accuracy on shrinking neighborhoods in Corollary 4.4. These slope bounds do not assert root-𝑛 accuracy for the full parameter vector near the singularity. Sharp rates over all joint drifting sequences remain open. The fixed-dimensional, full-cube design bounds and exact Gaussian/probit structure supply the projections, cumulants, and density envelopes used in the proofs. The distinction from semiparametric selection is substantive: Honoré and Hu (2020) obtain partial-identification results without exclusion restrictions when the error distribution is unspecified. Here the exact model identifies the parameters, and the question is the uniform accuracy remaining within it. The finite net and conditional integrals establish statistical attainability. A computational extension would need to control approximation and testing errors before transferring the risk bounds to a practical procedure; they do not automatically hold for maximum likelihood or adaptive Lasso. Other open problems are the sharp known-scale boundary (Remark G.5) and adaptation to an unspecified support-separation sequence.

Code and data availability 

The proofs and numerical protocols are included in Appendices A–I of this preprint. Pro-grams and data for its numerical exhibits are included as ancillary files, under anc/ reproducibility/ in the source archive. They contain the fixed uniform and two nonuniform designs, all 120000 saved likelihood-ratio statistics, the quadrature results, and the corresponding numerical checks. The guide REPRODUCIBILITY.txt lists files, depen-dencies, and reproduction commands; NUMERICAL_PROTOCOL_DETAILS.txt records random streams, runtime versions, exact nonuniform sampling, and quadrature diagnostics. Running run_reproduction.py in stored mode recomputes summaries, quadrature, figures, and tables from the saved statistics; fresh mode regenerates the Monte Carlo samples from the fixed random streams. The scope of these experiments is stated in Section 7.
