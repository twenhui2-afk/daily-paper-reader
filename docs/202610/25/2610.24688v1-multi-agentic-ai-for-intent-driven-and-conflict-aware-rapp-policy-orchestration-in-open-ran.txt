# Multi-Agentic AI for Intent-Driven and Conflict-Aware rApp Policy Orchestration in Open RAN 

Haiyuan Li, Yulei Wu, Luyu Qi, Dimitra Simeonidou 

Abstract —Open radio access network (O-RAN) enables a multi-vendor ecosystem that accelerates xApp development for near-real-time (RT) network management. However, this openness shifts a combinatorial coordination burden to the Non-RT layer, where rApp policies compose xApps from a growing catalog to realize operator intents. Coexisting intents further compound this challenge, as conflicts may arise among xApp actions, rApp policies, and their cross-layer interactions. To maximize intent admission while maintaining key performance indicator (KPI) compliance across incoming and incumbent intents, we propose a large language model-based Multi-Agentic AI framework. A Reasoning Agent screens candidate xApps against KPI contracts and produces a joint intent admission and xApp assignment decision. On post-deployment KPI violations, a Diagnosis Agent identifies the root causes of failure, labels the conflict mechanism, and guides the Reasoning Agent in subsequent attempts. Experi-mental results show that the framework based on GPT-OSS 120B achieves 28.6% more KPI-compliant cases and admits over 15 ×

as many incoming intents as state-of-the-art baselines. Under the Qwen 72B variant, Diagnosis increases compliant cases by 50.0%, quadruples admitted intents, and reduces deployment attempts by 32.6%. A live deployment conforming to O-RAN specifications further validates the feasibility of the proposed solution for conflict-aware rApp policy orchestration in real networks. 

Index Terms —Open RAN, Agentic AI, rApp policy, intent-based networking, conflict mitigation 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Mobile networks carry heterogeneous services with diverse performance and operational objectives. To address these de-mands, Open radio access network (O-RAN) exposes RAN control to third-party applications, expanding the ecosystem beyond vendor-specific functions [1]–[3]. In O-RAN, the non-real-time (Non-RT) RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) within the Service Management and Orchestration (SMO) orches-trates rApps that translate operator intents into policies, while the near-real-time (Near-RT) RIC hosts xApps that carry out near-RT control such as resource scheduling, mobility optimization, and traffic steering [4]–[7]. Although this layered open architecture enables flexible multi-vendor RAN control, it also introduces orchestration challenges. Compared with xApp development in the Near-RT RIC, automation at the Non-RT orchestration layer remains less mature. To serve diverse network management intents, the SMO must orchestrate rApps that select and compose xApps for each intent from a shared catalog without predefined xApp assignments. As the catalog

Coexisting intents may conflict through overlapping xApp actions, implicit runtime coupling across xApps, or incompatible rApp policies that cannot be jointly satisfied. Therefore, automated Non-RT RAN management requires intent-driven and conflict-aware rApp policy orchestration over a shared xApp ecosystem. Existing studies mainly address this challenge from two per-spectives. The first is intent-based RAN management, which maps operator intents to executable policies, configurations, descriptors, or control loops through intent fulfillment and intent-driven control [8]–[16]. The second focuses on conflict management, which detects and mitigates interference that arises when multiple rApp policies and their constituent xApps operate concurrently over shared RAN resources. Conflict management studies can be further organized by intervention time into pre-deployment and post-deployment methods. Pre-deployment methods screen or avoid conflicts ahead of ex-ecution through pre-execution screening, sandbox profiling, conflict-graph and causal learning, and optimization-based co-ordination [17]–[21]. In comparison, post-deployment methods use runtime control messages, key performance indicator (KPI) observations, or network context to detect conflicts and coor-dinate xApp actions during operation [22]–[27]. Despite these advances, several limitations remain in Non-RT orchestration:  

> •

Lack of joint orchestration. Intent-based RAN management reduces the burden of translating operator objectives into policies or configurations. However, most solutions consider single-intent settings and fixed intent categories without offering joint reasoning over intent admission, xApp assign-ment, and coordination between incumbent and incoming intents.  

> •

Catalog-dependent profiling. Existing orchestration and conflict-detection methods often rely on offline profiles, learned conflict graphs, sandbox measurements, or optimiza-tion models over predefined rApp policies. As the xApp catalog expands, exhaustive profiling of candidate compo-sitions and manual maintenance of such policies become impractical.  

> •

Overlooked cross-layer conflicts. Current research predomi-nantly targets Near-RT xApp interactions, especially over-lapping RAN-parameter control. However, conflicts that originate at the rApp policy layer and manifest through xApp execution are rarely modeled.  

> •

Disconnected pre- and post-deployment orchestration. Pre-deployment methods focus on conflict avoidance but may miss issues that emerge only at runtime. Post-deployment arXiv:2610.24688v1 [eess.SY] 17 Oct 2026 methods focus on conflict mitigation but lack causal analysis to prevent recurrence. The two stages are not well integrated, leaving no closed-loop path from runtime KPI evidence back to orchestration decisions. To address these limitations, we introduce a Multi-Agentic AI framework for intent-driven, conflict-aware rApp policy or-chestration in O-RAN. The main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

Multi-intent rApp policy orchestration formulation. To jointly orchestrate coexisting intents over a combinatorially expanding xApp composition space while preventing cross-intent conflicts, we formulate rApp policy orchestration as a joint intent admission and xApp assignment problem with the objective of maximizing admitted incoming intents subject to post-deployment KPI compliance.  

> •

Cross-layer conflict taxonomy. We introduce a two-layer, five-class conflict taxonomy. The first layer inherits the Near-RT xApp conflict classes defined by O-RAN Alliance WG3 [28], covering direct attribute contention, indirect KPI coupling, and implicit runtime interference. The second layer extends to cross-layer conflicts between Non-RT rApp policies and Near-RT xApp execution, including shared-instance policy contention and inter-intent objective incom-patibility.  

> •

Multi-agentic orchestration framework. Building on the for-mulation, we propose a Multi-Agentic AI framework with guided chain-of-thought (CoT) designs for pre-deployment reasoning and post-deployment diagnosis. Following the taxonomy, the Reasoning Agent screens candidate xApps against KPI contracts and produces a joint intent admis-sion and xApp assignment decision. On KPI violations after deployment, the Diagnosis Agent identifies the failed intent-KPI pairs, analyzes suspect xApps, classifies conflict mechanisms, and summarizes cross-attempt evidence to guide subsequent attempts, closing the loop between pre-deployment reasoning and post-deployment diagnosis.  

> •

Benchmark and over-the-air testbed evaluation. The frame-work is evaluated on a multi-intent O-RAN benchmark with 17 developed xApps and 19 operator intents across 10 incumbent/incoming-intent cases, with five runs per case for the full solution, ablations, and local large language model (LLM) variants. The proposed solution based on GPT-OSS 120B achieves 28.6% more KPI-compliant cases and admits over 15 × as many incoming intents as state-of-the-art baselines. The ablations and model comparison further show that guided CoT and LLM reasoning capability affect orchestration performance. Under the Qwen 72B variant, Diagnosis increases compliant cases by 50.0%, quadruples admitted incoming intents, and reduces mean deployment attempts by 32.6%. A live over-the-air proof of concept conforming to O-RAN specifications further validates the feasibility of the proposed Agentic AI solution for conflict-aware rApp policy orchestration in real networks. Section IV discusses the technical details of the multi-agent framework. Section V reports the benchmark evaluation, Section VI presents the live-testbed proof of concept, and Section VII concludes the paper. II. L ITERATURE REVIEW 

The expanding third-party xApp ecosystem introduces a heterogeneous set of candidate functions for realizing oper-ator intents at the Non-RT layer. Moreover, conflicts among multiple intents may arise from incompatible KPI contracts, inconsistent rApp policies, or interfering xApp actions across the shared RAN infrastructure. Existing studies address these challenges mainly through intent-based RAN management and conflict management. The former translates high-level objectives into executable control, while the latter detects or mitigates conflicting interactions among deployed xApps and rApps. This section reviews both directions and identifies the need for intent-driven and conflict-aware rApp policy orchestration. 

A. Intent-Based RAN Management 

Intent-based RAN management converts operator objectives into executable policies, configurations, descriptors, or control actions. Intent-fulfillment studies focus on the representation and translation stage. Banerjee et al. introduce an intent specification platform and an Intent Fulfillment System that translates formal intents into Cognitive Autonomous Network (CAN)-based RAN actions [8]. Garcês et al. use a chatbot interface to translate natural-language commands into network policies enforced by an ONOS software-defined networking (SDN) controller [9]. Mekrache and Ksentini use an LLM to generate network service descriptors for network function virtualization (NFV) deployment [10]. Beyond translation, intent-driven control optimizes the re-sulting network behavior through learned or agentic control loops. Radio scheduling, xApp orchestration, and slice re-source allocation have been optimized with reinforcement learning under fixed intent objectives and action spaces [13]– [15]. More recent studies extend intent-driven management with LLMs and agentic mechanisms, including distributed agents across rApp, xApp, and dApp timescales [29], RAN configuration generation from structured prompts and network feedback [11], SMO-level semantic translation connected with Non-RT rApp and Near-RT xApp enforcement [12], and planning, tool-use, memory, and reflection for O-RAN manage-ment tasks [16]. These studies reduce the manual cost of intent translation and control, but coexistence among intents, rApp policies, and independently developed xApps remains outside their main decision scope. A related conflict-oriented direction detects inconsistent control-parameter objectives or semantic conflicts among intents and policies [30], [31]. These conflict-oriented studies focus on semantic contradictions, while run-time network state, xApp catalog metadata, deployed rApp policies, and post-deployment KPI evidence remain largely absent. 

B. Conflict Management in O-RAN 

O-RAN conflict management is commonly grouped by intervention time. Pre-deployment methods detect or avoid conflicts before xApps act on the RAN. One group predicts configuration risk, as in Armstrong et al., who estimate KPI degradation from rApp-initiated configuration changes using performance-management, configuration, and KPI data [17]. A second group profiles or learns conflict structure. Del Prever et al. introduce PACIFISTA, which profiles O-RAN applica-tions in sandbox settings and builds application–attribute–key performance measurement (KPM) graphs to estimate conflict severity [18]. Zolghadr et al., Al Shami et al., and Sharma et al. use GraphSAGE, graph convolutional networks, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) with causal inference to construct or explain xApp–attribute–KPI relationships [19], [20], [32]. Adjacent orchestration work optimizes how xApps are selected and shared for service realization. Mungari et al. propose OREO, which models RAN services as configura-tions of functions implemented by xApps and selects service configurations, xApp complexity levels, sharing decisions, and resource allocations under quality, latency, and resource constraints [21]. Zhang et al. and Erdol et al. reduce conflicts earlier during the development stage through team learning or xApp distillation [33], [34]. These methods support risk screen-ing, conflict-knowledge construction, resource-aware xApp placement, or conflict-aware xApp design, but their fidelity depends on offline profiles, learned graphs, predefined service configurations, resource estimates, or trainable xApps. Post-deployment methods use runtime control messages, KPI observations, or network context after xApps have been deployed. At the control-message level, Adamczyk and Kliks propose a Near-RT RIC mitigation framework that intercepts xApp control messages to identify direct and indirect conflicts, and Sultana et al. demonstrate a control-message ordering mechanism on a software-defined radio O-RAN platform [22], [35]. Parameter-level work then moves from arbitration to mitigation. Wadud et al. develop cooperative-bargaining and quality-of-service (QoS)-aware mechanisms for selecting fea-sible settings of contended parameters [23], [24]. Beyond its pre-deployment use, explainable learning also appears in runtime diagnosis. Varshney et al. and Wadud et al. introduce models based on SHAP, graph neural networks (GNNs), and bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) for explain-ing or classifying runtime conflicts [25], [26]. Cinemre et al. and Giannopoulos et al. broaden runtime handling beyond single-parameter mitigation. Cinemre et al. propose a context-aware scheduler for active xApps without retraining them [27], while Giannopoulos et al. introduce a workflow that combines proactive and reactive conflict handling with emulated-network validation of candidate xApp actions before live execution [36]. These methods provide runtime observability, control-message arbitration, parameter-level mitigation, or xApp-level schedul-ing. However, the upstream Non-RT decision over intent admission and xApp assignment remains outside their decision scope. III. S YSTEM MODEL AND PROBLEM FORMULATION

ORCHESTRATION 

The orchestration problem (8) couples incoming-intent ad-mission and xApp assignment for rApp policy orchestration. In response, we propose a closed-loop Agentic AI framework as illustrated in Fig. 2. It iterates pre-deployment reasoning, deployment, KPI supervision, and post-deployment diagnosis. Given incoming intents In and incumbent intents Ia, the Reasoning Agent proposes a candidate rApp policy from the xApp catalog, incumbent deployment state, and retrieved O-RAN context. We introduce a policy deployment module that applies the candidate to the RAN and a supervision module that evaluates post-deployment KPI compliance against (8e). On KPI violations, the Diagnosis Agent converts the failure into structured evidence, and the framework launches another deployment attempt. We refer to one complete iteration of this loop as an orchestration cycle . Attempts within a cycle are indexed by k (k = 0 for the initial attempt, k ≥ 1 for retries). The retry budget Kr limits the retries after the initial attempt, so a cycle contains at most Kr + 1 deployment attempts. Both agents use guided CoT prompts that decompose each LLM call into ordered, labeled stages and record structured intermediate outputs before the final policy or attribution. 

A. Conflict Taxonomy

Each mechanism describes an interaction structure that can cause an admitted intent to violate a KPI contract. The Reasoning Agent screens the mechanisms as hypotheses before deployment, and the Diagnosis Agent uses them as labels after supervision reports a violation. (iii) Ineffective perturbation denotes a class of assign-ment changes that failed to shrink the affected set across attempts. D4 records the change as ineffective, allowing the next Reasoning attempt to avoid repeating it and instead select a different xApp assignment or reject the incoming intent. E. Non-LLM Modules 

Beyond the two agents, the closed-loop framework includes two deterministic modules that handle policy deployment and KPI supervision in the Non-RT RIC. The policy deployment module receives the candidate policy P(k) = (y(k), b (k))

and derives the active xApp set X(k)act = Xact (y(k), b (k)). It then compares the candidate policy with the deployed rApp policies R to determine newly required xApps, updated xApp assignments, and unchanged rApp policies. Newly required xApps are instantiated through the O1 and O2 interfaces, and xApp assignments are dispatched through the A1 interface. For an incumbent intent, the module updates only the assignment entries bi,x of its deployed rApp policy, since the admission variable is fixed by (8b). Retained xApps and unchanged rApp policies continue without redeployment. The supervision mod-ule evaluates KPI compliance after each deployment. It col-lects O1 performance-management telemetry and E2SM-KPM indications, aggregates the measurements over the evaluation window θi, and computes the KPI compliance verdict ηi,m 

in (7). The resulting observation record is emitted as O(k) . If 

ηi,m = 0 for any target KPI m ∈ Mi of an admitted intent, the module marks the deployment as non-compliant and invokes the Diagnosis Agent. Otherwise, the deployment is deemed KPI-compliant and terminates the current orchestration cycle. The framework above establishes a closed-loop methodol-ogy for rApp policy orchestration. The optimization problem in (8) specifies joint incoming-intent admission and xApp assignment under fixed KPI contracts. In operational de-ployments, the network operator may iteratively refine KPI contracts and specify admission priorities through interaction with the agentic interface, such as by designating mandatory intents subject to feasibility and incumbent-intent compliance. These operator inputs can be incorporated into the optimization formulation and guided prompts without retraining the LLM, while interactive requirement negotiation and priority-aware orchestration remain outside the present scope.

V. E XPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed closed-loop Multi-Agentic AI framework for rApp policy orchestration, we measure incoming-intent admission while requiring post-deployment KPI compliance for incumbent and admitted incoming intents. In a heterogeneous O-RAN environment, all methods use the same RAN realization, xApp catalog, evaluated intent set, and supervision module. For comparison, we adapt three baselines from the literature: OREO [21], PACIFISTA [18], and Causal-SHAP [20]. We also evaluate two internal ablations and an alternative local LLM backend.

1) OREO: We implement an OREO-based baseline [21] that preserves priority-weighted service admission and tradeoffs involving xApp sharing and resource cost under the same KPI validation procedure. Because our setting is defined by KPI contracts and xApp catalog metadata rather than O-Cloud CPU/RAM/disk placement or explicit service-function graphs, we adapt OREO’s service-configuration selection by mapping each target KPI to candidate xApps from the xApp catalog. The baseline filters configurations that violate KPI contracts and greedily selects the feasible configuration with the highest adapted score. This score combines intent priority, the cost of newly instantiated xApps, reuse of already active xApps, and KPI margin. 

2) PACIFISTA: A PACIFISTA-based baseline [18] is con-structed from profiles collected in the O-RAN environment. Each xApp is executed alone to collect observations for empir-ical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) over network attributes and KPI measurements. These profiles, together with xApp catalog metadata and latent-resource links, form the augmented application–attribute–KPM conflict graph. The KPM severity term σK is computed from ECDF integral-area distances over the measurements used to evaluate the corresponding KPI contracts. For each incoming intent’s fixed candidate xApp set, the admission score is the maximum σK

between any candidate xApp and the currently deployed xApp set. Incoming intents are processed greedily by priority and admitted only when this score is at most δTOL . Following the tolerance examples in the reference, we report δTOL = 0 .25 

and δTOL = 0 .50 .

3) Causal-SHAP Graph Gate: The Causal-SHAP graph gate follows the XGBoost-SHAP/DoWhy causal-DAG path of Sharma et al. [20] on the same xApp profile data. SHAP importance over per-KPI XGBoost surrogates retains attribute– KPI edges, and DoWhy backdoor regression estimates av-erage treatment effects and produces refutation outputs for those retained edges. We then convert the learned attribute– KPI graph into a conservative xApp-set admission score. An incoming intent is greedily admitted only when the risk score of its candidate xApp set against the currently deployed xApps remains below the tolerance. 

4) Agentic ablations and local LLM variants: The agentic con-figurations use locally served LLMs so that operator intents, incumbent deployment state, and KPI telemetry remain within the local orchestration environment. The default backend is GPT-OSS 120B. GPT-OSS Full uses the complete Reasoning– Diagnosis loop described in Section IV. GPT-OSS No Diag-nosis keeps the same Reasoning Agent, supervision module, and retry budget, but removes Diagnosis feedback from retry attempts. GPT-OSS No Guided CoT keeps both agents and the same input/output contracts, but replaces the guided reasoning prompts with objective-only role prompts. Qwen 72B Full and Qwen 72B No Diagnosis keep the same orchestration solution and validation procedure and change only the local LLM backend. All agentic configurations allow at most 10 deployment attempts, consisting of one initial attempt and 

Kr = 9 retries. A budget-exhausted cycle reaches this limit without finding a KPI-compliant deployment. For consistent reporting, cycles ending in a timeout, interruption, or runtime error are assigned 10 deployment attempts and zero admitted incoming intents. 

D. Results 

To evaluate overall orchestration performance while ac-counting for LLM output variability, we ran each case five times for the full Agentic AI solution, its ablations, and the local LLM variants. Fig. 4 reports the per-case KPI-compliant run percentage and the mean and standard deviation of admitted incoming intents and deployment attempts for these Agentic AI experiments, together with the single-run baseline outcomes. A case outcome is KPI-compliant only when all incumbent intents and all admitted incoming intents satisfy their KPI contracts after deployment. As shown in the figure, GPT-OSS 120B Full is compliant across all five runs in 8 of the 10 cases and has a nonzero mean admitted-intent count in every case except the infeasible C-03. In the larger cases C-06, C-07, and C-09, it remains compliant in all five runs while admitting 3.0, 3.0, and 3.2 incoming intents on average. By comparison, Causal-SHAP and PACIFISTA with 

δTOL = 0 .25 reach 7 and 6 compliant cases but admit no incoming intents. OREO and PACIFISTA with δTOL = 0 .50 

each admit only 1 incoming intent and reach 2 and 1 compliant cases, respectively. This difference could be attributed to the one-shot decision structure of the baselines. Their decisions rely on pre-deployment evidence, so compliance is achieved mainly by rejecting incoming intents instead of revising xApp assignments after KPI supervision. The No Guided CoT ablation examines the contribution of the guided reasoning order while retaining both agents and the same input/output contracts. Table V shows that the ablation averages 7.2 compliant cases, 6.8 admitted incoming intents, and 5.90 attempts per case, compared with 9.0, 15.4, and 2.16 for GPT-OSS 120B Full. Comparing the corresponding columns in Fig. 4, the compliant-run percentage falls from 100% to 40% in both C-06 and C-07. A similar gap appears in the attempt counts for cases where both configurations are fully compliant. No Guided CoT requires 6.2 versus 1.6 attempts in C-04 and 3.2 versus 1.0 in C-09. These results show that the guided CoT stage order helps the agents convert conflict evidence and KPI feedback into effective screening and assignment revision across attempts. Table V also summarizes the performance of the two local LLMs under the same orchestration logic. Of the two, Qwen 72B Full achieves 7.8 compliant cases, 12.8 admitted incoming intents, and 3.88 attempts per case, whereas GPT-OSS 120B Full reaches 9.0, 15.4, and 2.16, respectively. Fig. 4 provides a case-level example of this gap. In C-07, GPT-OSS is compliant in all five runs, while Qwen is compliant in one. Together, the aggregate and case-level results highlight the importance of LLM reasoning capability in conflict-aware orchestration. Finally, the Full and No Diagnosis comparison isolates the contribution of post-deployment failure attribution under the same Reasoning Agent, supervision module, and deployment-attempt budget. Table V shows a smaller difference with GPT-OSS 120B. GPT-OSS 120B Full reaches 9.0 compliant cases and 15.4 admitted incoming intents, compared with 8.4 and 14.2 for No Diagnosis, while reducing attempts from 3.28 to 2.16. The difference becomes larger with Qwen 72B. Diagnosis increases the averages from 5.2 to 7.8 compliant cases and from 3.2 to 12.8 admitted incoming intents, while reducing attempts from 5.76 to 3.88. This widening gap shows that Diagnosis becomes more important as the LLM’s reasoning capability decreases. In this setting, the Reasoning Agent relies more strongly on structured failure attribution to identify an effective revision for the next assignment.

VI. P ROOF OF CONCEPT 

Complementing the evaluation in Section V, we deploy the proposed framework as a proof of concept (PoC) on a live over-the-air O-RAN testbed. The testbed connects the full orchestration loop to real radio hardware for multi-intent rApp policy orchestration on a single live cell serving commercial user equipment (UEs). 

A. Testbed Setup 

The PoC setup is shown in Fig. 5. The OpenAirInterface (OAI) distributed unit (DU), centralized unit (CU), and 5G core run in Docker containers on a DGX Spark, with an Ettus USRP B210 as the radio unit (RU). The band n78 cell operates at 3.6048 GHz with time-division duplexing (TDD), 30 kHz subcarrier spacing, and 24 physical resource blocks (PRBs). Two Quectel RM520N-GL modules attach over the air as UEs. FlexRIC connects to the E2 agent over E2AP/SCTP using the KPM, MAC, and SLICE service models (SMs). For monitoring, a MAC-SM monitor xApp streams per-UE throughput, modulation and coding scheme (MCS), block error rate (BLER), PRB usage, and signal-to-noise ratio through ZeroMQ into Prometheus. A DU-side scheduler extension exposes three per-UE network attributes: PRB quota, MCS cap, and proportional-fair scheduling weight. Python xApps built on the FlexRIC SDK subscribe to MAC-SM telemetry and apply these attributes through SLICE-SM control messages over the standard xApp → Near-RT RIC → E2AP → DU path. The Agentic AI layer and the Non-RT RIC function are implemented as separate components. The former reuses the Reasoning and Diagnosis Agents of Section IV, including their guided CoT prompts and conflict taxonomy, with GPT-OSS 120B running on the DGX Spark. The Non-RT RIC function maintains the xApp catalog and incumbent deployment state and launches the xApps selected by each candidate policy.

This paper presented a closed-loop Agentic AI framework for intent-driven and conflict-aware rApp policy orchestration in O-RAN. The framework jointly decides incoming-intent admission and xApp assignment, with each admitted intent realized by an rApp policy composed of assigned xApps. The framework combines a guided CoT Reasoning Agent for pre-deployment screening and assignment, a guided CoT Diagnosis Agent for post-deployment failure attribution, and structured non-LLM modules for policy deployment and KPI supervision. Across five runs per case, the proposed solution based on GPT-OSS 120B achieves 28.6% more KPI-compliant cases and admits over 15 × as many incoming intents as state-of-the-art baselines. The ablations show that guided CoT im-proves compliant admission and attempt efficiency, while the model comparison highlights the importance of LLM reason-ing capability in mixed-conflict cases. Diagnosis partly com-pensates for reduced reasoning capability, increasing Qwen 72B’s compliant cases by 50.0% and reducing mean deploy-ment attempts by 32.6%. A live over-the-air OAI/FlexRIC proof of concept further demonstrates the practical feasibility of the framework by preserving incumbent-intent compliance while orchestrating rApp policies in real networks.
