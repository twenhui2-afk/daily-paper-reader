## ABSTRACT 

Behavioral foundation models (BFMs) provide humanoids with reusable reper-toires of whole-body behaviors; in real-world humanoid control, the same be-havioral intent may require fundamentally different motions under different ter-rain constraints. Covering such variation with terrain-matched demonstrations requires enumerating a combinatorial space of behaviors and terrain geometries. We introduce P-BFM , a perceptive behavioral foundation model that factorizes humanoid control into terrain-invariant intent and terrain-conditioned realization. A forward–backward representation maps motion references, target states, and reward functions into a shared intent space without terrain information, while a perceptive policy learns how to realize each intent under the surrounding terrain geometry. We further identify a complementary limitation of purely latent control: behavioral latents can preserve local motion patterns while losing precise global progress. We therefore introduce Explicit Root Instructions (ERI), which sepa-rates global transport (e.g., heading and planar velocity) from behavioral intent, while leaving local whole-body adaptation to the perceptive policy. This decom-position leads to a characteristic capability absent from conventional BFMs: one fixed intent induces different, terrain-appropriate behaviors, including changes in step height, stride, posture, foothold placement, and contacts, without terrain-specific demonstrations. Across challenging terrains, P-BFM improves motion fidelity, root-control precision, and perturbation recovery over strong baselines. A single frozen policy further supports motion tracking, goal reaching, reward inference, teleoperation, and terrain-aware recovery, and transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1 using onboard depth sensing. 1arXiv:2610.15833v1 [cs.RO] 11 Oct 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Humanoid robots are designed to operate in human-centric environments, with versatile embodi-ments suited to diverse physical tasks. Realizing this potential requires a reusable skill space and a unified controller capable of executing a broad range of skills. Recent advances in Behavior Foun-dation Models (BFMs) have made substantial progress in this direction, enabling a single controller to execute diverse behaviors through a shared skill space learned from large-scale Mocap datasets. However, for humanoid robots operating in the real world, generality should also encompass perceiv-ing and adapting to their surroundings. Many tracking-based BFMs rely heavily on demonstrations, and extending them to perceptive control substantially expands the data space to combinations of terrain geometry and motion. Covering this space with paired demonstrations is difficult, limiting generalization to unseen combinations. Prior approaches Wang et al. (2026) address this bottleneck through terrain-aware motion augmentation, but the underlying optimization can yield suboptimal references, particularly for behaviors involving rich contact with the ground. To address this limitation, we propose a Perceptive Behavior Foundation Model (P-BFM ), which learns through autonomous interaction with complex terrains rather than relying solely on terrain-matched motion imitation. We build on forward–backward (FB) representations, an unsupervised RL (Reinforcement Learning) framework that jointly learns behavioral representations and a latent-conditioned policy, allowing task-level reward functions to be mapped to latent variables for policy execution. These rewards map robot states to scalar values and specify behavioral intents, such as raising an arm or lying down. This exploration-driven framework enables the robot to acquire diverse behaviors without requiring demonstrations for terrain–motion combination. Within this framework, the reward-specified intent should remain consistent across terrains, while its execution adapts to the terrain geometry. We therefore separate intent encoding from perception-driven execution. The backward representation encodes body-state features without terrain inputs, allowing the same reward specification to yield the same latent intent across terrains. The policy uses depth observations to realize intents specified by flat-ground rewards across diverse terrains. How-ever, weak root-motion supervision in unsupervised learning can lead to heading drift or stepping in place before obstacles. We therefore introduce ERI (Explicit Root Instructions), which adds target heading and base-velocity instructions to improve root tracking while preserving terrain adaptation. Experiments in simulation and the real world demonstrate that P-BFM outperforms all evaluated baselines in precision and robustness across terrains, supporting motion tracking, goal reaching, reward inference, fall recovery, and teleoperation within a shared perceptive policy. By realizing the same intent through terrain-adaptive behaviors without terrain-specific demonstrations, P-BFM 

enables humanoid robots to execute a shared repertoire of skills across different environments. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose P-BFM , which uses unsupervised RL and FB representations to unify motions, goal states, and rewards in a terrain-agnostic intent space, enabling perceptive control across terrains without terrain-specific demonstrations. • We introduce ERI, which complements unsupervised behavior learning with explicit heading and base-velocity supervision, enabling precise root control during terrain-adaptive execution. • We demonstrate superior precision and robustness over existing baselines through extensive ex-periments and ablations, and validate diverse terrain-adaptive behaviors on a physical humanoid. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 BEHAVIORAL FOUNDATION MODELS AND HUMANOID WHOLE -B ODY CONTROL 

Learning from human motion offers a practical foundation for expressive and diverse whole-body humanoid control. BeyondMimic Liao et al. (2025) combines robust motion tracking with guided diffusion to synthesize and compose motion primitives for downstream tasks. Similarly, NVIDIA’s SONIC Luo et al. (2026) scales motion tracking with large-scale human motion data to learn a unified whole-body controller for diverse humanoid behaviors. Complementarily, unsupervised RL explores behaviors without tracking specific motion references, aiming to learn reward-inferable 2representations through self-exploration. Recent methods based on forward–backward representa-tions Touati & Ollivier (2021), including Meta Motivo Tirinzoni et al. (2025), BFM-Zero Li et al. (2025), and UFO RoboParty Lab Team (2026), organize behaviors in a shared latent space where re-ward functions specify behavioral latents. They demonstrate diverse whole-body behaviors, pertur-bation recovery, and flexible task specification, but lack environmental perception for terrain-aware control. Their self-exploration capacity nevertheless offers a promising foundation for terrain adap-tation without terrain-specific demonstrations. Building on this, P-BFM integrates depth perception with latent-conditioned control and learns through interaction with diverse terrains, allowing the same intent to be realized through terrain-adaptive behaviors. 2.2 PERCEPTIVE LOCOMOTION AND TERRAIN ADAPTATION 

Perceptive humanoid control uses environmental observations to anticipate obstacles and adapt movement to terrain geometry Long et al. (2024); He et al. (2025); Zhu et al. (2026); Wu et al. (2026); Zhuang et al. (2026); Wang et al. (2026); Yu et al. (2026); Duan et al. (2024); Song et al. (2025); Sun et al. (2026a); Zhuang et al. (2024); Cui et al. (2026); Sun et al. (2026b); Wang et al. (2025). PIM Long et al. (2024), AME He et al. (2025), Hiking in the Wild Zhu et al. (2026) and SSR Yu et al. (2026) integrate terrain maps or depth observations for robust locomotion. Data-driven approaches, such as Perceptive Humanoid Parkour Wu et al. (2026) and Deep Whole-body Parkour Zhuang et al. (2026), enable dynamic whole-body skills but still depend on sufficient motion coverage and terrain-compatible demonstrations. PMT Wang et al. (2026) reduces this dependence through offline reference optimization and teacher–student training, but separating reference syn-thesis from policy learning can introduce discrepancies and complicate extension to contact-rich behaviors such as getting up. In contrast, P-BFM learns terrain adaptation through unsupervised RL and autonomous interaction, without terrain-specific demonstrations or reference synthesis. A shared policy uses latent intents to specify behavior and depth observations to adapt execution.

## 4 METHOD 

Our goal is to learn a single low-level humanoid controller that can realize diverse intents while adapting to complex terrain geometry. As shown in Fig. 2, P-BFM consists of a terrain-agnostic intent space and a terrain-aware perceptive actor: the intent space specifies what behavior to perform, and the actor determines how to execute it with perception. During pretraining, we encode depth observations into visual features for terrain perception (Sec. 4.1), learn the intent space with FB representation, and train the actor with discriminator and auxiliary critics (Sec. 4.3). The FB objective enables zero-shot inference, the discriminator improves motion quality, and auxiliary critics facilitate sim-to-real transfer. To address the weak global-motion supervision in unsupervised RL, we introduce ERI (Explicit Root Instructions), which combines root heading and base-velocity instructions with intent latents to preserve commanded progress during terrain-adaptive execution (Sec. 4.2). At inference, different task specifications are mapped to corresponding intent latents, including tracking intent, goal-reaching intent, and reward intent (Sec. 4.4). The same perceptive actor then generates terrain-adaptive actions conditioned on the inferred latent, without task-specific policy optimization. 4.1 PERCEPTION INTEGRATION 

To execute the same intent across terrains, the actor requires deployable terrain perception. Follow-ing the temporal aggregation and preprocessing design of Hiking in the Wild Zhu et al. (2026), we maintain a 37-frame depth buffer, from which we sample every 5 frames to obtain K = 8 obser-vations with ages (35 , 30 , 25 , 20 , 15 , 10 , 5, 0) , while applying a per-episode temporal offset of 0–1 frames to model sensing latency. Each 36 × 64 depth frame is cropped to 18 × 32 , filtered with a 3 × 3 Gaussian kernel (σ = 1) , clipped to [0 , 2.5] meters, normalized. The resulting sequence 4is encoded by a shared CNN and aggregated with a GRU into hvt , which is used by the policy and velocity estimator to adapt execution to surrounding geometry. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate whether P-BFM can translate different forms of behavioral intent into robust terrain-adaptive control. Following Sec. 4.4, we consider three intent types: goal-reaching intent , tracking intent , and reward intent . These intents are derived from target goal states, motion references, and reward functions, respectively. They correspond to three main evaluation settings: goal reaching, motion tracking, and reward inference. We further include teleoperation to test whether the learned policy can follow online user instructions while adapting to real-world terrain. Across these settings, our evaluation is organized around four questions: • Does P-BFM outperform existing general-purpose humanoid controllers in motion tracking on challenging terrains? • Can P-BFM generate terrain-adaptive behaviors from the same intent across diverse terrains? • How do explicit root instructions improve control precision and terrain traversal? • How does P-BFM perform on a real humanoid robot using onboard sensing?

UFO is an unsupervised RL framework based on FB representations, while PMT adapts generic reference motions to terrains through offline optimization and incorporates terrain perception into reference-motion tracking. Under matched robot models and terrain conditions, we evaluate tracking accuracy, motion fidelity, and recovery on challenging terrains. Since PMT’s full training dataset is not publicly available, its motion coverage cannot be verified. We therefore evaluate its released model on the only reference trajectory provided, while evaluat-ing P-BFM and UFO on a broader set of 40 LaFAN sequences. Reference-motion coverage and trial counts are detailed in Appendix A.7. As shown in Table 1, despite this broader motion cov-erage, P-BFM achieves lower joint-position and heading errors, less foot sliding, and substantially higher recovery rates than the baselines. Although the differing evaluation sets preclude a strictly matched comparison, these results suggest higher motion style reproduction accuracy and robust-ness for P-BFM . We attribute these gains to end-to-end learning through autonomous exploration, which enables motion adaptation through direct interaction with diverse terrains rather than offline reference-motion optimization. Under matched evaluation conditions on the unseen motion dataset, P-BFM consistently out-performs the baselines in motion fidelity, root-instruction tracking, and recovery, demonstrating stronger generalization beyond its training motions. The lower heading and velocity errors highlight the effectiveness of explicit root instructions, while the higher recovery rate suggests that unsuper-vised exploration helps the policy learn to recover from diverse off-nominal states. 5.2 TERRAIN -A DAPTIVE BEHAVIOR FROM A SINGLE INTENT 

We examine whether P-BFM can realize the same reward-inferred intent through terrain-dependent motion patterns. Using forward walking as an example, we define a reward function for flat-ground walking and infer the corresponding latent intent through reward inference. This single latent is then evaluated across multiple terrains. As shown in Fig. 3, the policy does not simply reproduce a fixed walking pattern. Instead, it autonomously changes step height, stride length, body posture, and foothold selection according to the perceived terrain geometry. It is worth noting that no terrain-specific motion demonstrations are provided during training. The adaptive behaviors emerge from autonomous exploration in complex environments rather than from terrain-matched reference mo-tions, demonstrating that P-BFM can discover distinct ways to realize the same reward-inferred intent under different terrain constraints. 5.3 ABLATION STUDY

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced P-BFM , a Perceptive Behavior Foundation Model that unifies motion references, target states, and rewards in a terrain-agnostic intent space. A shared perceptive policy realizes these intents across terrains, while ERI complements unsupervised behavior learning with explicit root-motion supervision. Simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate motion tracking, goal reaching, reward inference, teleoperation, and terrain-adaptive recovery without terrain-specific demonstrations. However, behavioral generalization remains limited by the diversity of motion priors and terrains explored during training. Errors are evaluated against the corresponding reference time without post-hoc temporal alignment. 

LaFAN. The LaFAN evaluation uses 40 source motion sequences. Episodes are equally weighted across segments or recordings, initial terrains, terrain rows, and eval-uation repeats. The reported standard deviation describes variation among episode-level means. For full-recording SOMA evaluation, each scheduled recording condition receives equal weight regardless of record-ing duration. Metric-specific validity counts are retained because undefined heading or FS values can change the effective sample size. Ordinary tracking failures do not trigger automatic resets when simulation can continue. Contin-uous metrics therefore include subsequent execution rather than only the successful portion of a rollout. In the SOMA comparison, 8 PMT episodes and 219 UFO episodes ended early at the global environment boundary. Their recorded-time means remain included. We additionally evaluate a category-balanced SOMA subset to examine recovery under a differ-ent motion composition. The subset contains 30 recordings from each of six categories: walk-ing, running, dancing, jumping, low-pose motions, and sports. The 180 recordings comprise 161 motion-name groups and yield 255 complete 10-second segments. Incomplete terminal windows are discarded. This evaluation uses the same perturbation and recovery criteria as Appendix A.9. For this supplement, outcomes are first averaged over conditions and segments within each record-ing. Recording-level rates are then averaged within each category, followed by an equal-weight average over the six categories. This differs from the episode-weighted aggregation of the 281-segment SOMA recovery set used in the main tables. The balanced results are reported separately and do not replace the original SOMA main-table val-ues. In particular, PMT’s high pre-push violation rate indicates that its recovery score also reflects tracking difficulty already present before perturbation. Differences in the aggregate rates therefore cannot be attributed solely to the applied forces. A.12 REAL -W ORLD DEPTH ACQUISITION AND PREPROCESSING
