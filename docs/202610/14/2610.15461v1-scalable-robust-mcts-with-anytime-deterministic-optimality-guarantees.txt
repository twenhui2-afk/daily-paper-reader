## ABSTRACT 

MDP planners often rely on nominal transition models that may differ from the true dynamics. Robust MDP planning accounts for this uncertainty through a set of admissible models, but doing so within the limited computation available on-line is challenging: the search tree grows exponentially with the horizon and each robust backup requires an additional optimization. Existing online robust methods remain limited in scale and offer at most probabilistic guarantees. We introduce, to the best of our knowledge, the first deterministic anytime guarantees for robust on-line MDP planning. We obtain these guarantees by restricting computation to the sampled search tree while deterministically bounding the contribution of unsam-pled trajectories. For any fixed policy, we derive lower and upper bounds on the gap between its projected and true robust values under both rectangular and non-rectangular uncertainty sets. Under rectangular uncertainty, we propagate these bounds through the robust Bellman operator to bound the optimal robust value. These certificates are computable from the current sampled tree, hold at every node, tighten as the sampled support expands, and converge to the robust values as full support is recovered. We develop CAR-MCTS, a bound-guided MCTS planner that uses these deterministic certificates to guide exploration, prune prov-ably suboptimal branches, and terminate once the optimal root action is certified. The fixed-policy bounds also yield an anytime policy-evaluation mechanism that certifies the robust value of any given policy, independent of how it was obtained. Experiments verify the deterministic certificates, their tightening under support expansion, substantial branch pruning, root-action certification, and convergence to the optimal robust policy. To our knowledge, this is the first scalable robust MCTS framework with anytime deterministic guarantees for both online planning and policy evaluation, without enumerating the full state space. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Markov decision processes (MDPs) provide a standard framework for sequential decision making under uncertainty, with the objective of finding a policy that maximizes the expected cumulative reward. As the state space or planning horizon grows, solving the MDP globally becomes compu-tationally intractable. Online planning instead focuses computation on the current decision through a lookahead from the current state. Common online methods, such as Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), address this lookahead through sampling, incrementally constructing and selectively ex-panding a partial search tree (Kocsis & Szepesv´ ari, 2006). By allocating simulations to a subset of future outcomes, MCTS reduces the cost of online planning while refining root action-value. Model-based online planning relies on a generative transition model that may differ from the true dynamics. Consequently, nominal value estimates may be inaccurate and nominally optimal policies may become suboptimal under model perturbations (Wiesemann et al., 2013). Robust Markov deci-sion processes (RMDPs) address this sensitivity by optimizing against the worst-case model within a set of admissible transition models (Iyengar, 2005; Nilim & El Ghaoui, 2005), but each robust backup then requires an additional optimization over transition models. Repeating these backups throughout the online search tree under a limited decision-time budget further restricts how much of the tree can be explored. 1arXiv:2610.15461v1 [cs.AI] 11 Oct 2026 Preprint To improve scalability, robust RL has explored approaches such as function approximation and pol-icy learning from sampled interactions under model uncertainty (Tamar et al., 2014; Wang & Zou, 2021; Panaganti & Kalathil, 2022). These methods generally aim to learn a robust policy over the state space, rather than allocating computation to the specific decision faced online. More recently, sample-based methods have targeted robust online planning directly, including Robust Sparse Sam-pling (RSS) (Shazman et al., 2026) and robust MCTS (Dam et al., 2026). However, RSS scales exponentially with the planning horizon, while Dam et al. (2026) robust MCTS relies on the full state space, hindering scalability in real world problems that often exhibit large state spaces. Beyond scalability, online planning acts on a partially explored tree, leaving the effect of unex-plored outcomes on the current decision unknown. This is particularly important in the robust setting, where such outcomes may alter the worst-case value and preferred action. For MCTS un-der a known model, Shah et al. (2022) provide finite-time probabilistic guarantees, which Dam et al. (2026) leverage to establish finite-sample guarantees for Robust-Power-UCT. Similarly, RSS provides probabilistic finite-sample guarantees for robust sample-based planning (Shazman et al., 2026). In a complementary line of work, Barenboim & Indelman (2023; 2026) establish determinis-tic anytime guarantees for sample-based anytime POMDP planning in the nominal setting. However, such deterministic anytime guarantees are not available for robust online planning, where the effect of unexplored outcomes must be assessed jointly with model uncertainty. To address these gaps, we formulate robust online planning directly over the sampled portion of the search tree while explicitly accounting for the probability mass associated with unexplored out-comes. Restricting computation to the sampled tree preserves the scalability required for online planning. The resulting bounds quantify how much unexplored outcomes may still affect the current solution. This remaining uncertainty guides further search, focusing the limited planning budget where it can most effectively tighten the bounds and ultimately certify the decision. 

Contributions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide deterministic anytime bounds for sample-based online planning in robust MDPs. Our main contributions are: • We derive deterministic anytime two-sided bounds on the robust value of a fixed policy under general and non-rectangular uncertainty sets. The bounds tighten monotonically as the sampled support expands and, in the non-robust setting, improve upon prior deterministic sample-based bounds by providing tighter lower and upper guarantees. • Under rectangular uncertainty, we extend the fixed-policy bounds to deterministic anytime lower and upper certificates on the optimal robust value through robust Bellman recursion. • We introduce CAR-MCTS (Certified Anytime Robust MCTS ), an anytime robust planner with deterministic optimality guarantees computed solely from the constructed partial search tree. By avoiding enumeration of the full state space, CAR-MCTS remains applicable to large state spaces while retaining online computation. It uses the upper certificate to guide exploration and prunes provably suboptimal actions to focus computation on unresolved branches. Once the bounds certify the optimal root action, the search can terminate immediately and return the certified optimal decision. • We empirically evaluate CAR-MCTS against existing robust online planners, demonstrating certificate tightening, pruning, root-action certification, and convergence toward the optimal robust solution. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Robust online planning has received comparatively limited attention. Sharma et al. (2019) intro-duced RAMCP, a Monte Carlo planner for risk-sensitive Bayes-adaptive planning, but its formu-lation relies on a prior over a parametric family of transition models. Kohankhaki et al. (2024) developed UA-MCTS, which incorporates transition uncertainty into MCTS exploration; its em-pirical evaluation considers deterministic environments, while its theoretical analysis is limited to the corrupted-bandit setting. More closely related to our setting, Shazman et al. (2026) introduced Robust Sparse Sampling (RSS), which extends Sparse Sampling to RMDPs using Sample Average Approximation and provides finite-sample performance guarantees. Its complexity is independent of the state-space cardinality but grows exponentially with the planning horizon. Its analysis is further based on non-negative rewards and assumes the existence of a zero-reward fail-state that is absorb-2Preprint ing under every model in the uncertainty set. Dam et al. (2026) developed a robust MCTS planner that combines robust power-mean backups with exploration bonuses and establishes finite-sample convergence throughout the search tree. While its tree search is selective, the robust transition op-timization retains an explicit dependence on the full state space, thereby limiting scalability as the state-space cardinality grows.

Monte Carlo Tree Search. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) incrementally constructs a finite lookahead tree rooted at the current history ht through repeated simulations (Kocsis & Szepesv´ ari, 3Preprint 2006). During each simulation, at a decision node hk, k ≥ t, an action is selected according to an exploration-strategy that balances exploration and exploitation, and a successor state is sampled from a generative transition model. If the resulting successor has not previously been encountered, the tree is expanded with the new history hk+1 = ( hk, a k, s k+1 ). The simulation then continues until the planning horizon is reached, after which the resulting values are backed up along the visited path. Repeating this process progressively expands the sampled portion of the planning tree while concentrating computation on branches favored by the search strategy. At decision time, the planner selects an action at the root based on the resulting action-value estimates. In our setting, this sampled tree defines the support of the simplified RMDP introduced in Section 4.

At each simulation, the planner descends the current search tree by selecting 7Preprint the action with the largest upper certificate URDB and samples the next state from the nominal transition model. Previously unseen successors are added to the sampled support, after which the projected robust values and certificates are backed up along the visited path, avoiding explicit enu-meration of the full state space and thereby preserving scalability to large state spaces. During the backup, actions whose upper certificate falls below a competing lower certificate are pruned at every visited node. At the root, planning terminates early whenever an action is certified optimal. If cer-tification is not reached within the sampling budget, CAR-MCTS returns the action with the largest projected robust action-value bQrob 0 together with deterministic optimality certificates. 

## 7 RESULTS 

Experimental setup. We evaluate CAR-MCTS in the Frozen Lake domain with varying grid sizes, terminal holes, and different start and goal cells. At each nonterminal cell, the agent chooses one of four actions: LEFT , DOWN , RIGHT , or UP . Under the nominal transition model, the agent moves toward the intended neighboring cell with probability 1 − pslip ; the remaining probability pslip 

is distributed over cells in concentric Chebyshev-distance rings around the current cell, up to a configurable radius k. This ring model allows the number of possible successor states to be varied across experiments. Transition uncertainty is modeled by rectangular total-variation sets around the nominal transition rows. The reward function and remaining experiment-specific parameters, including the grid size, horizon, pslip , ring radius k, and uncertainty radius, are provided in the Appendix. We first evaluate the fixed-policy guarantees of Theorem 1 by progressively expanding the sampled tree of a fixed policy. Figure 1a plots the projected robust value and its deterministic certificate against the fraction of the complete depth-H tree represented, with the exact robust value obtained from the full tree shown for reference. Throughout the search, the exact value remains within the certificate, while the interval contracts as the sampled support expands and the projected value con-verges to the exact value. The experiment is repeated over 50 independent nested tree expansions, illustrating both the anytime validity and the convergence of the certificate.

## 8 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we presented a methodology for scalable robust online MDP planning with determin-istic anytime guarantees. The core principle we propose is to restrict computation to the sampled search tree while explicitly accounting for the contribution of unexplored outcomes, thereby avoid-ing enumeration of the full state space. We first derived deterministic lower and upper bounds on the error between the simplified and full robust values of an arbitrary policy under general transition uncertainty, including non-rectangular sets, and showed that these certificates tighten as the sampled support expands and converge as the full support is recovered. Under rectangular uncertainty, we extended this construction to recursive action-value certificates that bound the optimal robust value, enabling provably suboptimal actions to be pruned, reducing unnecessary computation, and optimal root actions to be certified for early termination. Building on these results, we introduced CAR-MCTS, which integrates the certificates directly into MCTS to guide exploration, prune resolved branches, and terminate once the root decision is certified, while retaining deterministic action-value certificates when the sampling budget is exhausted. Experiments validate the derived guaran-tees, demonstrate robustness to model mismatch, and show that CAR-MCTS remains practical as the full state space grows while competing robust MCTS methods become increasingly costly. To our knowledge, this is the first robust online MCTS framework combining scalable sampled-support planning with deterministic anytime guarantees. 9Preprint

## B ROBUST BACKUP UNDER TV U NCERTAINTY 

For the TV uncertainty sets used in our experiments, the inner optimization in (19), (20), can be solved directly on the sampled successor support. Let 

˚pout = 1 − X 

> s′∈bS(h,a )

˚P T (s′ | s, a )

denote the nominal probability mass outside the currently sampled support. Since projection restricts the tran-sition row without renormalization, all unsampled successors can be represented by a single aggregate outside state. Consequently, the projected uncertainty set is equivalently represented by a TV ball over the | bS(h, a )|+1 

dimensional vector consisting of the sampled successor probabilities and the aggregate outside mass. The ag-gregate outside state represents the omitted probability mass, with its contribution determined by the corre-sponding projected backup. The resulting inner problem is a linear optimization over a TV ball. We solve it by the standard mass-transfer procedure: probability mass of at most ρ is shifted from successors with the largest continuation values toward those with the smallest values, subject to the simplex constraints. Sorting the continuation values gives a per-backup complexity of O(| bS(h, a )| log | bS(h, a )|) and O(| bS(h, a )|) memory. Thus, the robust backup depends on the currently sampled successor support rather than on the cardinality of the full state space. 

## C EXPERIMENTS 

C.1 FROZEN LAKE DOMAIN 

We evaluate the planners on a family of Frozen Lake domains represented by two-dimensional grids con-taining traversable frozen cells, terminal holes, a start cell, and a goal cell. Each grid cell corresponds to an MDP state, and we additionally introduce an absorbing terminal state sdone . The action space is 

A = {LEFT , DOWN , RIGHT , UP }, corresponding to the four cardinal movement directions. Reaching either a hole or the goal terminates the episode, after which the process transitions to sdone . The objective is to reach the goal while avoiding holes. Different experiments vary the grid size, obstacle layout, start and goal locations, and planning horizon; their specific configurations are summarized in Section C.6.

C.5 PLANNING PROTOCOL 

All online planning experiments use a receding-horizon protocol. At each environment step, the planner con-structs a finite search tree of depth H rooted at the current state and performs planning using its available computation budget. The selected root action is then executed in the environment, the resulting next state is observed, and a new depth-H planning problem is initialized from that state. Thus, H denotes the planner’s lookahead horizon rather than the episode length. For model-mismatch experiments, planning is performed using the nominal transition model ˚P T parameter-ized by pslip , whereas executed transitions are sampled from the environment model parameterized by ptrue .Episodes terminate upon reaching either the goal or a hole.

C.6.2 ABLATION 2: S CALABILITY WITH PLANNING HORIZON 

We next isolate the effect of the planning horizon on online computation. All environment and planner pa-rameters are held fixed while varying H ∈ { 1, . . . , 10 }. Experiments use a 100 × 100 Frozen Lake domain with hole density 0.15 , ring radius k = 8 , and β = 1 . The planner uses pslip = 0 .3, while the true execution dynamics use ptrue = 0 .1, giving ρ = 0 .197 . CAR-MCTS and Robust Power UCT use 20 MCTS simulations per decision, and results are averaged over 20 independent seeds. 

Figure 8: Decision time as a function of planning depth. CAR-MCTS and Robust Power UCT use 

20 simulations per decision, while RSS uses branching factor C = 2 . A 20 -second computation cap is imposed per decision; RSS reaches this limit at depth 6, and deeper configurations are therefore not evaluated. 

Figure 8 shows a clear difference in scalability with planning depth. CAR-MCTS remains within the tens-of-milliseconds regime throughout the entire sweep, with decision time increasing only gradually as the depth grows. Robust Power UCT similarly remains approximately constant over the tested range. In contrast, RSS is initially the fastest method at small depths, but its computational cost grows rapidly with each additional planning level, increasing from below 1 ms at depth 1 to more than 200 ms at depth 5. We impose a 20 -second computation cap per decision; at depth 6, RSS reaches this limit before completing the planning step. Consequently, deeper RSS configurations are not evaluated.

The difference stems from the underlying search structure. RSS recursively samples C successor states for every action at each level of the tree, so its computational cost grows rapidly with depth. In contrast, CAR-MCTS uses a fixed simulation budget: increasing the planning depth lengthens the sampled trajectories and their backups, but does not require recursively expanding all sampled successor combinations. Consequently, CAR-MCTS supports substantially deeper lookahead while retaining practical online decision times. 

C.6.3 ABLATION 3: T RUE OPTIMAL -A CTION SELECTION 

Finally, we evaluate the quality of the action selected by each planner directly, rather than through the stochastic outcome of an episode. For each planning problem, we compute the optimal action offline using the true transition model and measure the fraction of decisions in which each planner selects this action. Importantly, the true model is used only as an evaluation oracle and is not available to any of the planners. We use a 4 × 4 Frozen Lake domain with ring radius k = 1 , search depth 3, and 20 simulations for the MCTS-based methods. RSS uses C = 2 , and each configuration is evaluated over 100 independent seeds. As in Ablation 1 C.6.1, the columns vary the true environment slip probability ptrue ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, while the x-axis varies the planner-assumed pslip .

Figure 9: Fraction of decisions selecting the action that is optimal under the true transition model. Columns correspond to different values of ptrue , while the x-axis varies the planner-assumed pslip .The true model is used only for evaluation. 

Figure 9 shows that CAR-MCTS selects the true optimal action more reliably than the competing planners over nearly the entire sweep. When the true environment is only mildly slippery, all methods perform well when the planning model is accurate, but their behavior diverges as the assumed model moves away from the true dynamics. CAR-MCTS degrades more gradually and maintains a substantially higher optimal-action selection rate under increasing model mismatch. The difference becomes particularly pronounced as the true environment itself becomes more slippery. For 

ptrue = 0 .2 and 0.3, CAR-MCTS maintains a clear advantage over a broad range of pslip values, showing that it continues to identify the correct decision even when both the environment is more difficult and the planner model is inaccurate. Robust Power UCT generally improves over nominal UCT in parts of the sweep, but does not approach the consistency of CAR-MCTS. RSS with C = 2 is typically comparable to or below nominal UCT, indicating that its robust formulation does not translate into a consistent advantage in identifying the true optimal action under the tested planning budget. This experiment complements the episode-level results of Ablation 1 C.6.1. Higher success rates can in prin-ciple result partly from favorable stochastic transitions after an action is selected; here, the evaluation isolates the planning decision itself. The results therefore show that CAR-MCTS’s advantage is not limited to better realized outcomes: it more frequently selects the action that is optimal under the true environment dynam-ics. Together with the previous ablations, this demonstrates stronger decision quality under model mismatch, reduced sensitivity to broad transition support, and practical scalability with planning depth. 

C.6.4 ABLATION 4: C ERTIFICATION AND PRUNING IN PRACTICE 

Finally, we examine how frequently the deterministic bounds used by CAR-MCTS lead to root-action certifi-cation and branch pruning in practice. We evaluate three grid sizes, 4 × 4, 8 × 8, and 16 × 16 , while varying 

pslip and the planning budget over {100 , 200 , 500 , 1000 } simulations. We report the fraction of decisions for which the root action is certified and the fraction of branches pruned during search. 

32 Preprint 

Figure 10: Root-action certification rate (top) and branch-pruning rate (bottom) for different grid sizes, planning budgets, and values of pslip .

Figure 10 shows that root certification occurs frequently in practice and increases strongly with the available planning budget. Even with relatively small budgets of 100 –200 simulations, CAR-MCTS certifies a substantial fraction of root decisions, with certification rates often around 50% or higher depending on the configuration. As the budget increases, the deterministic intervals tighten further and the certification rate rises sharply. At 

500 and 1000 simulations, CAR-MCTS certifies the root decision in nearly all runs over a large portion of the tested configurations. This demonstrates that the certificates are not only theoretical guarantees, but can become sufficiently tight during online planning to provide an effective early-stopping criterion. The lower row shows that the same bounds also eliminate a meaningful fraction of suboptimal branches during search. Interestingly, the measured pruning rate tends to decrease when certification becomes frequent. This is consistent with the two mechanisms playing complementary roles: while the root decision remains unresolved, continued sampling contracts the action-value bounds and enables dominated branches to be pruned; once the root action is certified, planning terminates and no further pruning is needed. Overall, the ablation shows that CAR-MCTS uses its deterministic certificates in two complementary ways to reduce online computation: locally, by pruning branches that can no longer contain the optimal action, and globally, by terminating planning once the root action is certified. The high certification rates achieved with larger simulation budgets show that both mechanisms are actively useful in practice rather than serving only as theoretical guarantees.
