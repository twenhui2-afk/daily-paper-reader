## ABSTRACT 

Quasi-periodic oscillations are characterized by fitting Lorentzian functions to a nonparametric estimate of the power spectral density, a two-step procedure that separates estimation from modeling and does not accommodate measurement error. We develop a parametric alternative based on autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) processes observed with measurement error. We derive an exact decomposition of the ARMA spectral density into real-valued components, one for each class of autoregressive roots under complex conjugation, plus a polynomial term when the moving-average order is at least the autoregressive order. Each component is a Wrapped Cauchy kernel, and for complex roots it is accompanied by a conjugate-Poisson term that makes the peak asymmetric and displaces its maximum from the frequency given by the argument of the root. We obtain closed-form expressions for the location, height, width, and asymmetry of each peak, and a matching variance decomposition. Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood through the Kalman filter, and all descriptors follow by plug-in. Simulations show that the closed-form peak location is recovered without appreciable bias. An X-ray binary light curve illustrates the methodology. 

Keywords ARMA process · Spectral density · Measurement error

## 1 Introduction 

Time series analysis is a fundamental tool in many scientific disciplines, from astrophysics and geophysics to neuro-science and economics. A central objective is to characterize the oscillatory behavior of a process, often by studying its power spectral density (PSD). In particular, identifying and quantifying periodic or quasi-periodic oscillations (QPOs) embedded in noisy, broadband signals is a common challenge across fields. Traditionally, practitioners have relied on a two-step approach: first, compute a nonparametric estimator of the PSD, such as the periodogram or the Welch estimator [31]; second, fit a sum of phenomenological functions, typically Lorentzians, to the estimated spectrum to extract peak frequencies, widths, and amplitudes. While widely adopted, this methodology suffers from several statistical limitations: it separates estimation from modeling, leading to loss of efficiency; it lacks a coherent probabilistic framework for uncertainty quantification; and it does not naturally accommodate measurement error or complex dependence structures. An alternative and more principled approach is to represent the observed time series using a parametric model that directly captures its second-order structure. Among parametric models, autoregressive moving-average (ARMA) processes [7, 6] occupy a central role in time series analysis due to their flexibility and parsimony. An ARMA( p, q )process is defined by a finite set of autoregressive and moving-average parameters, and its spectral density has a arXiv:2610.14384v1 [stat.ME] 9 Oct 2026 DECOMPOSING THE ARMA SPECTRAL DENSITY A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 14, 2026 known rational form. When the process is stationary and causal, the shape of the PSD is governed by the roots of the autoregressive polynomial. This suggests that, rather than first estimating the spectrum nonparametrically and then fitting Lorentzians, one could estimate the ARMA parameters directly and derive the spectral peaks analytically from those roots. The idea of attributing spectral features to individual autoregressive roots is not new. In continuous time, the spectral density of a continuous-time autoregressive moving average (CARMA) process admits a decomposition into Lorentzian-type components, one per autoregressive root, a property exploited by [17] to analyze quasi-periodic oscillations in astrophysical light curves. In discrete time, [24] expressed the ARMA spectral density as a sum of root-associated terms. What is missing is a decomposition of the ARMA spectral density into real-valued components, valid for arbitrary orders p and q, together with closed-form descriptors of the peaks those components produce. The present paper fills this gap. Our first contribution is an exact decomposition of the spectral density of a causal ARMA( p, q ) process. We show that the PSD splits into p components, one for each root of ϕ(z−1) = 0 , together with a polynomial term fX, 0(ω) that appears only when q ≥ p and which we characterize in closed form. Individual components associated with complex roots are complex-valued, so we group the roots into equivalence classes under conjugation and show that each class contributes a real-valued function: a real root contributes a single rescaled Wrapped Cauchy kernel, equivalently a rescaled Poisson kernel, centered at frequency 0 or at the Nyquist frequency 1/(2∆) according to its sign, while a complex-conjugate pair contributes the sum of a Wrapped Cauchy kernel and a sine-modulated conjugate-Poisson term, together with their reflections about the origin. Writing nR and nC for the numbers of real and complex classes, the spectral density is thus a sum of nR + 2 nC real-valued terms, plus one further term when q ≥ p. The Wrapped Cauchy kernel carries the entire spectral mass of a class, with concentration governed by the modulus s of the root, while the conjugate-Poisson term integrates to zero and affects only the shape of the peak. Our second contribution concerns the peaks themselves. In the phenomenological approach, the oscillation frequency of a QPO is read off as the centroid ωj, 0/(2 π) of a Lorentzian fitted to the periodogram, and in the CARMA representation of [17] this centroid is given by the imaginary part of an autoregressive root. Our decomposition yields the discrete-time analogue: each complex class carries a Wrapped Cauchy kernel centred at α/ (2 π∆) , where α is the argument of the representative root, so that α/ (2 π∆) plays the role of the Lorentzian centroid. We show, however, that this centroid is not where the class component peaks: the conjugate-Poisson term is antisymmetric about α/ (2 π∆) and tilts the kernel, so the maximum is displaced. We derive the exact location and height of that maximum in closed form, together with the frequencies at which the component falls to a fixed fraction k of its peak value, the resulting width, which at k = 1 /2 is the full width at half maximum, and an asymmetry index measuring the imbalance between the two half-widths. All of these are explicit functions of the model parameters, requiring no numerical search over frequency. The displacement from α/ (2 π∆) is negligible for sharply concentrated roots but substantial for broad ones, so reading the peak frequency from the argument of the root, as the analogy with the Lorentzian centroid suggests, is justified only in the former regime. Our third contribution is a decomposition of the process variance that matches the spectral decomposition term by term: a real class contributes 2πK (r), a complex class contributes 4π Re( K(r)) , and measurement error contributes σ2

A related line of work represents the spectrum directly as a superposition of elementary resonators. In the Gaussian-process literature, the celerite model of [9] builds covariance functions as sums of stochastically-driven damped harmonic oscillators with peaked, Lorentzian-type spectra, chosen so that likelihood evaluation scales linearly in the sample size; and [11] model a spectrum as a Bayesian mixture of AR (2) components, each contributing a single peak of given location and bandwidth. These approaches posit resonators as modeling primitives to be fitted, whereas we obtain the resonator structure as an exact algebraic identity for the spectral density of an already-estimated ARMA (p, q ) process, with each component tied to a specific autoregressive root. Relative to [24], we generalize in four ways. First, we remove the restriction p > q , so that the decomposition covers all ARMA orders, with the additional component fX, 0(ω) characterized explicitly when q ≥ p. Second, we express the decomposition entirely through real-valued functions, each tied to an equivalence class of autoregressive roots. Third, we derive the peak location, width and asymmetry of each class in closed form, quantities that the earlier decomposition does not provide and that are precisely what a phenomenological Lorentzian fit is used to obtain. Fourth, we derive the accompanying variance decomposition and extend the whole framework to processes observed with measurement error. To make the methodology operational, we estimate ARMA models by maximum likelihood through a state-space representation and the Kalman filter [12], which accommodates measurement error naturally and evaluates the likelihood at a cost linear in the sample size [10]. Plug-in estimators of every spectral component and of every peak descriptor follow by substituting the estimated parameters into the analytical expressions. A simulation study covering an AR( 2)process with a single sharply concentrated class, an AR( 4) process combining a sharp and a broad class, and an ARMA( 3, 2) process with one complex and one real class shows that the closed-form peak location is recovered without appreciable bias whenever the underlying root is identified. It also shows that the two natural alternatives are inadequate outside the sharply concentrated regime: the nominal centroid α/ (2 π∆) carries a bias equal to the displacement our result predicts, which for a class of modulus 0.70 is more than three times the sampling standard deviation and does not diminish with the sample size; and a numerical maximization of the fitted spectral density, which locates the maximum of the total density rather than of the individual component, has a root mean squared error an order of magnitude larger for the same class. Finally, we illustrate the approach by analyzing an X-ray binary light curve from the source XTE 1550 −564, observed by the Rossi X-ray Timing Explorer (RXTE). Following [17], we apply a logarithmic transformation to approximately Gaussianize the data and incorporate a known measurement error variance estimated by the method of [30]. The fitted ARMA( 3, 2) model captures the dominant quasi-periodic oscillation at approximately 2.84 Hz through a single complex-conjugate class, and the variance decomposition attributes 55 .5% of the total variance to the broadband component associated with the real class, 26 .8% to the oscillatory class, and 17 .7% to measurement noise. Residual diagnostics support the adequacy of the fit. While our application is motivated by astrophysics, the framework is general and applicable wherever spectral peaks are of interest, including neuroscience, geophysics and engineering. By providing an analytically tractable and interpretable decomposition of the ARMA spectral density, together with closed-form descriptors of the peaks it contains, this work connects parametric time series modeling to the phenomenological peak fitting that is standard practice in those fields. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews ARMA processes with measurement error and their spectral density, and situates the Lorentzian description of QPOs. Section 3 develops the spectral decomposition, the closed forms for the peak location, width and asymmetry, and the variance decomposition. Section 4 describes estimation by maximum likelihood through the Kalman filter and the resulting plug-in estimators. Section 5 reports the simulation study, Section 6 the analysis of the RXTE light curve, and Section 7 concludes.

Because the same root-decomposition argument applies to any process whose spectral density is a ratio of trigonometric polynomials, discrete-time ARMA models are, in principle, equally valid candidates for this Lorentzian-based QPO decomposition. 

## 3 Decomposition of the spectral density 

CARMA processes are the continuous-time analogs of the well-known discrete-time ARMA models, extending the classical framework to irregularly sampled data, as often encountered in astronomical observations. A key feature of CARMA models is that their PSD admits a closed-form decomposition as a sum of Lorentzian functions, each associated with a root of the autoregressive polynomial. This property makes CARMA modeling particularly effective at capturing QPOs [17]. The following proposition establishes a discrete-time counterpart to this result: the PSD of a causal ARMA (p, q )

process can likewise be decomposed algebraically into a sum of root-associated spectral components, one per root of the autoregressive polynomial, together with a residual polynomial term that arises whenever q ≥ p. ## 6 Application

## 7 Conclusion 

This paper provides an exact decomposition of the spectral density of a discrete-time ARMA process with measurement error into components indexed by the equivalence classes of the autoregressive roots, together with closed-form expressions for the location, width and asymmetry of the peak of each complex class. The decomposition is the discrete-time counterpart of the Lorentzian representation of a CARMA spectral density, and it extends that representation to the ARMA processes that do not arise as sampled CARMA processes. Because the peak descriptors are explicit functions of the model parameters, they are obtained by substitution once the model is fitted, with no search over frequency and no separate fit to a non-parametric spectral estimate. The simulation study of Section 5 supports three conclusions. First, the closed form of Proposition 2 recovers the peak location without bias whenever the corresponding root is identified. Second, the nominal centroid α/ (2 π∆) , is an adequate approximation only when the component is sharply concentrated: for a class of modulus 0.70 it carries a bias of 0.0137 that does not diminish with the sample size, and across the whole design the empirical bias of the centroid differs from the displacement predicted by Proposition 2 by at most 0.0023 . 2.8 Hz through a single complex-conjugate root pair, and the variance decomposition of Section 3 attributes 55 .5% of the total variance to the broadband component associated with the real class, 26 .8% to the complex-conjugate class, and the remaining 17 .7% to measurement noise. Residual diagnostics support the adequacy of the fit. Several limitations remain, and they indicate the natural directions for further work. The decomposition assumes the autoregressive roots to be simple; repeated roots produce poles of higher order and components that are no longer weighted Poisson kernels, and the weights K(r) become numerically unstable as two roots approach one another. Order selection is the main practical obstacle: for the ARMA (3 , 2) model of the simulation, BIC recovered the true order in 76 .0% of replications without measurement error and in only 1.5% with it, the moving-average parameters being weakly identified relative to the penalty, though the complex class was still matched in 97 .6% of the latter. At 

n = 100 the descriptors are too imprecise to be reported, so the framework is suited to the long, regularly sampled series for which it was designed rather than to short records. Uncertainty in the peak descriptors was assessed here by the parametric bootstrap, which for the light curve gave a wide interval for the width; since the descriptors are smooth functions of the model parameters away from repeated roots, standard errors also follow by the delta method from the observed information, and calibrating either route is left for future work. Finally, the measurement error variance has been treated as known throughout, estimated externally in the application [30]; its joint estimation with the ARMA parameters, and the identifiability of that joint problem, deserve separate study, as does the behaviour of the method when the data-generating process is not exactly ARMA.
