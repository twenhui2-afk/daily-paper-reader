# When Does Trust Help? A Leakage-Free Evaluation of Representation- and Aggregation-Based Defenses Against Backdoors in Federated Intrusion Detection

Abstract —Federated learning (FL) lets distributed intrusion-detection systems (IDS) train on private traffic, but exposes the global model to poisoning and backdoor attacks under non-IID client data. A popular defense family scores or filters clients using a small trusted “root” dataset and the similarity of client behaviour to a trusted reference. I re-examine these trust-based 

defenses under a protocol designed to remove common evaluation artifacts: train-only feature standardization, a server root set 

disjoint from client data, multi-seed ( n=6 ) runs with paired significance tests, formal (ε, δ ) accounting for DP-SGD, and a defense-aware adaptive attacker. Across three NIDS datasets (CSE-CIC-IDS2018, NF-ToN-IoT, NF-UNSW-NB15-v2) I find: (1) a representation-similarity aggregator (RepGuard) significantly improves classification utility and seed-stability over FLTrust under heavy non-IID poisoning, and—under severe heterogeneity or many adversaries—also lowers backdoor ASR substantially (e.g. 0.48 vs FLTrust 0.86 at Dirichlet α=0 .1); (2) at strong 

trigger magnitudes no defense reduces backdoor attack success rate (ASR ≈0.95 ); (3) a controlled trigger-magnitude sweep shows defenses help only for small triggers (ASR 0.10 for RepGuard at magnitude 0.5, vs. 0.40 undefended) and collapse to ASR ≈1.0 by magnitude 2; (4) a triggered-canary check that assumes knowledge of the attacker’s trigger—the assumption behind much prior “success”—lowers ASR from 0.49 to 0.12 , i.e. trigger knowledge, not representation scoring, suppresses the backdoor; (5) correctly-accounted DP-SGD ( ε ∈ [0 .3, 16] ) costs little utility but does not mitigate the backdoor. I release the protocol so future FL-IDS defenses can be evaluated without the leakage and oracle assumptions that inflate reported robustness. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A. Background and Motivation 

Federated IDS keeps raw traffic on-device, but Byzantine clients can poison updates or implant backdoors. A large body of trust-based defenses [1]–[6] down-weights clients whose updates, gradients, or representations diverge from a trusted reference; the area is now mature enough to have dedicated surveys [7]. 

B. Problem Statement 

I ask when trust helps: under what attack strengths, het-erogeneity, and adversary fractions do trust-based aggregators actually reduce backdoor risk, and how much of their reported robustness is an artifact of the evaluation rather than the defense? 

C. Limitations of Existing Approaches and Research Gap 

I argue that the reported robustness of this family is frequently overstated by evaluation artifacts, and I measure it under a protocol that removes them. Prior pipelines (including an earlier version of this one) suffered from full-dataset standardization (test leakage), a root set drawn from the client pool, single-seed headline numbers, “DP” that clips batch-mean gradients with no reported ε, non-adaptive “adaptive” attackers, and—most importantly—backdoor filters given the attacker’s exact trigger. Removing each changes the conclusions. 

D. Research Objectives and Main Contributions 

(1) A leakage-free, statistically-tested evaluation protocol for FL-IDS poisoning defenses (disjoint trusted root, n=6 -seed runs with paired tests, RDP-based (ε, δ ) accounting, and a defense-aware adaptive attacker). (2) RepGuard, a representation-similarity aggregator that significantly improves utility and stability over FLTrust, which I do not claim as a backdoor cure. (3) A characterization of when trust-based defenses work—effective at moderate trigger magnitudes, defeated at strong ones—with the oracle-vs-trigger-agnostic gap quantified. (4) A privacy–utility–robustness study with formally accounted DP. 

E. Paper Organization 

Section II reviews related work; Section III formalizes the threat model and presents RepGuard and the evaluation proto-col; Section IV describes the experimental setup; Section V reports results and analysis; Section VI discusses implications, strengths, and limitations; Section VII concludes. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Conventional Approaches 

Byzantine-robust aggregators replace the FedAvg mean with a resilient estimator: Krum selects the update closest to its neighbours [8], Trimmed-Mean discards coordinate-wise extremes [9], FoolsGold penalizes Sybil-like similar updates [2], and FLTrust bootstraps trust from a small clean root via cosine alignment [1]. These target generic poisoning and, as shown in §V-C, do not stop strong targeted backdoors. arXiv:2610.14767v1 [cs.CR] 10 Oct 2026 B. Deep Learning-Based Approaches 

Recent FL-IDS work scores clients with server-side signals: WeiDetect fits a Weibull model over validation scores [3]; P4P combines a probe response, norm filtering and temporal suspicion [4]; other 2026 work uses gradient stabilization or synaptic-weight adjustment [5], [6]; and representation-attention defenses operate in feature space [10]. RepGuard belongs to this family; the area is now broad enough to warrant surveys [7]. 

C. Recent State-of-the-Art Methods 

Bagdasaryan et al. showed model-replacement backdoors and that participant-level DP curbs them only at a utility cost [11]; later work showed many FL backdoor defenses remain vulnerable [12], and 2026 attacks use dynamic or triggerless patterns [13]. I use the RDP accountant for the subsampled Gaussian mechanism [14]–[16]. 

D. Limitations of Existing Methods 

Robust aggregators assume generic, untargeted poisoning and degrade under strong targeted backdoors; trust-based FL-IDS defenses are typically evaluated with root sets overlapping client data, single seeds, unaccounted “DP,” and filters that know the attacker’s trigger. The trigger-magnitude sweep here is consistent with the known limits: static defenses help only for small triggers. 

E. Research Gap and Motivation 

Unlike these method papers, the contribution of this paper is an evaluation and characterization : a shared, leakage-free protocol that makes FL-IDS defense results honestly compa-rable, and a measurement of how much reported robustness survives once oracle assumptions and evaluation artifacts are removed. III. P ROPOSED METHOD 

A. Problem Formulation 

Cross-device FL with N =10 clients, non-IID data via a Dirichlet prior ( α=0 .3). Up to f =2 clients are Byzantine and perform sign-flip update poisoning and tabular backdoor poisoning (an additive trigger on the first k features flipping triggered inputs to Benign ). The server is honest, holds a trusted root disjoint from client data, and knows an upper bound on 

f (standard for Krum/FLTrust [1], [8]). It does not know the trigger; a separate oracle variant that does is reported only as an upper bound. I also evaluate a defense-aware adaptive attacker (§III-E). 

B. Overall Architecture 

Each round the server briefly trains a trusted root model and scores each client on clean root data; it keeps the top-scoring clients, anchors the aggregate toward the root update, and optionally applies trust memory, Weibull tail calibration, and a short distillation step. No component uses trigger knowledge. Algorithm steps per round: (1) train θ0 on Dr; (2) score all clients via Eq. (1); (3) Weibull/memory blend; (4) keep top-

(N −2f ); (5) root-anchored weighted aggregate; (6) optional distillation from top-trusted clients. 

C. Input Representation and Preprocessing 

Features are z-scored on the train split only (no test leakage); a ≥1024 -sample server root is held disjoint from client data. To avoid class-ordering bias I sample rows uniformly at random (not the file prefix) before partitioning.

E. Oracle Variant and Defense-Aware Attacker 

Oracle variant (upper bound). A triggered-canary check applies the attacker’s exact trigger to root samples and penalizes flips to the target. This requires oracle knowledge and is reported only as an upper bound. 

Defense-aware adaptive attacker. The attacker embeds the backdoor while penalizing deviation of its clean-data representations from the received global model, explicitly trying to look benign to a representation-based filter, with evasion strength λ.

F. Training Strategy 

Two clearly-distinguished regimes: harsh (trigger 5.0,

5 rounds, 100k rows/file, DP-SGD on) and moderate (trig-ger 1.0, 3 rounds, DP off). DP-SGD uses per-example clipping with RDP accounting [14]–[16]. 

G. Computational Complexity 

RepGuard’s added cost is one short root-model fit plus N

scoring passes per round—linear in clients and root size, no per-example trigger search. Table I confirms this empirically: the defense-specific overhead is ∼8–10 s of server time atop 

∼230 s of client training ( <4% ); P4P’s probe is negligible. All defenses are practical for periodic FL rounds. IV. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

E. Comparison with the State of the Art: A Cautionary Result 

I attempted a best-effort reimplementation of P4P [4] from its published description. In this multiclass, heavily non-IID setting it did not transfer: macro-F1 0.275 ±.057 on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and 0.074 ±.061 on NF-ToN-IoT—at or below undefended FedAvg—with ASR ≥0.99 . Because P4P’s authors report accuracy gains , I attribute this to under-specified hyperparameters and a probe/norm/suspicion pipeline that, with these settings, excludes legitimately skewed benign clients under strong non-IID, rather than to a deficiency of the original method. I therefore make no superiority claim over P4P. I instead read this as direct evidence for the central point: FL-IDS defense results are difficult to compare faithfully without released code and a shared protocol—exactly the gap this paper’s evaluation framework targets.

I. Error Analysis 

Failures concentrate where the data give the model no signal: on NF-UNSW-NB15-v2 the rarest classes (Worms: 5 samples) are effectively unlearnable for every method, and undefended FedAvg’s low ASR on NF-ToN-IoT reflects a weak classifier rather than robustness. Seed variance is high ( ±0.10 –0.31 ) on the stress sweeps and the α trend is non-monotonic, so single-seed readings of any cell in Table III would be unreliable.

A. Interpretation of Findings 

Trust-based defenses preserve utility under non-IID poison-ing and raise the cost of a backdoor (forcing larger, more detectable triggers) but do not prevent one without trigger knowledge. The effect is dataset-dependent: on NF-UNSW-NB15-v2 trust-based defenses cut a strong backdoor relative to no defense (FedAvg 0.999 vs 0.40 –0.59 , §V-D), with RepGuard+ lowest, whereas on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 the same trigger defeats all methods. 

B. Comparison with Previous Studies 

The findings are consistent with known FL-backdoor vulner-ability results [11], [12]: static defenses help only for small triggers, and DP curbs backdoors only at a utility cost. The failed P4P transfer (§V-E) further shows that published FL-IDS defense numbers are hard to reproduce without released code. 

C. Implications 

Evaluations of FL-IDS defenses should report trigger-magnitude sweeps, trigger-agnostic ASR, accounted (ε, δ ), and multi-seed paired tests as a minimum; the released protocol makes this the default rather than the exception. 

D. Strengths 

A leakage-free, oracle-free, statistically-tested protocol; a defense-aware adaptive attacker; three datasets spanning balanced and extreme-imbalance regimes; and transparent reporting of negative and non-significant results. 

E. Limitations 

A single MLP architecture and one (additive) trigger family; trust defenses assume a clean disjoint root and a known bound on f ; baselines span classic robust aggregation, and the best-effort reimplementation of the 2026 P4P defense [4] did not transfer (§V-E), so no comparison is drawn from it; a faithful comparison awaits the authors’ code. Other recent defenses [3], [5], [6] are discussed but not re-implemented; and the adaptive attacker, though defense-aware and λ-tuned, is weaker than recent dynamic/triggerless attacks [13]. F. Future Directions 

Extending the protocol to deeper architectures, additional trigger families, and dynamic/triggerless attacks; and re-running the comparison once P4P’s official code is available. VII. C ONCLUSION 

Under a leakage-free, oracle-free, statistically-tested protocol, trust-based FL-IDS defenses are best understood as utility-preserving aggregators that help against moderate backdoors and fail against strong ones. I quantify how much of prior reported robustness depended on trigger knowledge, and release the protocol for honest comparison. REFERENCES [1] X. Cao, M. Fang, J. Liu, and N. Z.
