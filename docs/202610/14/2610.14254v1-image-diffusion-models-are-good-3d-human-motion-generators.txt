## ABSTRACT 

Text-to-motion generation typically relies on dataset-specific skeletal representa-tions, with models trained separately for benchmarks with different joint layouts. We investigate whether image diffusion architectures can model 3D human mo-tion through a common image-compatible representation that accommodates het-erogeneous skeletal layouts while preserving motion-specific kinematic structure. To this end, we organize root-centered joint trajectories into topology-informed joint–time images and model global root dynamics through a complementary la-tent stream. A motion autoencoder conditions local-motion decoding on the root latent, while a text-conditioned Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT) jointly generates both latent streams, with supervision on decoded 3D joint positions further constraining generation in motion space. In addition to dataset-specific models, we train a single motion autoencoder on HumanML3D and KIT-ML, fol-lowed by a single text-conditioned SiT shared across both datasets, preserving their respective skeletal definitions without skeleton retargeting to a common joint layout. Experiments on HumanML3D and KIT-ML show that our dataset-specific models achieve state-of-the-art performance, while shared training yields further gains on both benchmarks, demonstrating the benefits of shared modeling across heterogeneous skeletal layouts. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Text-to-motion generation aims to synthesize realistic 3D human motion from natural-language de-scriptions (Guo et al., 2022; Petrovich et al., 2022). Recent methods have made substantial progress in motion quality and text–motion alignment (Tevet et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a; Guo et al., 2024), but commonly operate on representations tied to particular skeletal definitions. Widely used benchmarks such as HumanML3D (Guo et al., 2022) and KIT-ML (Plappert et al., 2016) use differ-ent skeletal definitions, including different joint counts and indexing conventions. In vector-based representations, these differences affect both the vector dimensionality and the correspondence be-tween vector entries and skeletal joints, requiring explicit handling when sharing model inputs and outputs across datasets. Recent work has explored motion generation across heterogeneous skele-tal structures under different conditioning settings, including skeleton-conditioned synthesis (Gat et al., 2025), style- and trajectory-controlled generation (Khani et al., 2025), and text- and skeleton-conditioned generation (Liu et al., 2025). Within this broader goal, we investigate whether an image-compatible representation can support shared motion autoencoding and text-conditioned generation while preserving each dataset’s native skeletal definition.

Root-centered joint trajectories naturally form a joint–time array, with the three spatial coordinates serving as separate channels. Mapping this array to a common image resolution makes the spa-tial dimensions presented to the model independent of the native joint count, providing a basis for shared modeling across datasets (Fig. 1). This image-compatible form also allows motion to be pro-cessed by image autoencoding and latent generative architectures (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024). However, a common image shape alone does not encode anatom-ical relationships or capture dependencies between global movement and local articulation. These considerations motivate a representation and modeling framework that explicitly incorporates both. To address these challenges, we adopt the compact motion representation used in MARDM (Meng et al., 2025), which demonstrated that root motion and root-centered local joint positions can sup-port strong motion generation. We investigate how these two motion components can be organized for image-based generative modeling. Specifically, we reorder local joints according to body-part organization and kinematic topology and arrange their trajectories into joint–time images, giving the joint axis a meaningful anatomical organization. Global root dynamics do not share this joint-wise organization and are therefore modeled through a complementary latent stream. To capture depen-dencies between global movement and local articulation, our motion autoencoder encodes skeleton images and root motion into separate latent streams and conditions image decoding on the root la-tent. A text-conditioned Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT) then jointly generates both latent streams from noise, modeling their interaction during generation. To further constrain generation in motion space, we supervise decoded 3D joint positions. Beyond dataset-specific training, we use the proposed representation to train a single motion autoen-coder across HumanML3D and KIT-ML, followed by a single text-conditioned SiT shared across both datasets. A dataset condition specifies the target dataset during latent generation. Decoded motions are recovered and supervised according to each dataset’s native skeletal definition, enabling parameter sharing without skeleton retargeting or joint padding. Experiments on HumanML3D and KIT-ML show that our dataset-specific models achieve state-of-the-art performance, while shared training further improves all six reported metrics on both datasets. These results demonstrate that the proposed representation supports both strong dataset-specific generation and shared modeling across heterogeneous skeletal layouts. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce an image-compatible motion representation that organizes root-centered local joint trajectories into topology-informed joint-time images while representing global root dynamics through a complementary stream. • We develop a coupled motion generation framework combining root-conditioned image decoding, joint text-conditioned generation of both latent streams with SiT, and supervision on decoded 3D joint positions. 2Preprint • We enable shared motion autoencoding and text-conditioned generation across Hu-manML3D and KIT-ML using a single motion autoencoder and a single SiT, while preserv-ing each dataset’s native skeletal definition without skeleton retargeting or joint padding. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Text-to-Motion Generation. Diffusion-based methods generate text-conditioned motion directly in motion space, as in MDM (Tevet et al., 2023) and MotionDiffuse (Zhang et al., 2024), or in a learned latent space, as in MLD (Chen et al., 2023). MARDM (Meng et al., 2025) combines diffusion with masked autoregressive modeling using a compact motion representation. Another line of work repre-sents motion using discrete tokens. T2M-GPT (Zhang et al., 2023a) uses autoregressive generation, MoMask (Guo et al., 2024) combines hierarchical motion quantization with masked prediction, and BAMM (Pinyoanuntapong et al., 2024a) integrates masked and autoregressive modeling for flexible-length generation and editing. Building on discrete motion tokens, MotionLLM (Wu et al., 2025) uses a pretrained language model for motion generation, editing, and understanding, while IRG-MotionLLM (Li et al., 2026) interleaves generation with assessment and refinement. Com-plementary to these advances in generative modeling, we investigate an image-compatible motion representation that supports image generative architectures while incorporating skeletal organization and enabling shared modeling across heterogeneous skeletal layouts. 

Motion Representations and Image-Compatible Generative Modeling. The standard text-to-motion representation (Guo et al., 2022) combines root motion, joint positions, rotations, veloci-ties, and foot-contact information. Following MARDM (Meng et al., 2025), which demonstrates strong generation performance using only root motion and root-centered joint positions, we adopt this compact representation for image-based generative modeling. Image-based skeleton representa-tions have also been explored for action recognition. Ke et al. (2017) transform skeleton trajectories into image-based clips encoding spatial relationships for convolutional networks, while SkeleMo-tion (Caetano et al., 2019) encodes joint-motion magnitudes and orientations as skeleton images. These approaches demonstrate that skeletal sequences can be organized into structured images for action recognition. Our work explores this general idea for text-conditioned generation: we en-code root-centered 3D joint trajectories in topology-informed joint–time images, model global root dynamics through a complementary stream, and recover generated outputs as 3D motion sequences. Diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Li & He, 2026; Wang et al., 2026) have been at the forefront of recent advances in image generation, while DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) and SiT (Ma et al., 2024) develop Transformer architectures for diffusion and interpolant-based generation. We build on these advances to jointly generate image and root-motion latents, using root-conditioned decoding and decoded 3D joint supervision to connect latent generation to motion geometry. 

Motion Modeling Across Skeletal Structures. Motion retargeting transfers motion from one skele-ton to another. Aberman et al. (2020) introduce skeleton-aware convolution, pooling, and unpooling operators to embed motions from different skeletal structures into a shared latent space for cross-structural retargeting. More recent work directly generates motion across heterogeneous skeletal structures. AnyTop (Gat et al., 2025) incorporates skeletal topology and textual joint descriptions into a diffusion model for skeleton-conditioned synthesis. UniMoGen (Khani et al., 2025) dynami-cally processes character joints within a skeleton-agnostic diffusion model for style- and trajectory-controlled generation. OmniSkel (Liu et al., 2025) generates motion conditioned on text and a target skeleton, using a skeleton-aware motion autoencoder. Our image-compatible approach maps local joint trajectories to a common image resolution, enabling shared motion autoencoding and generation across datasets with different native joint counts. We investigate this formulation on HumanML3D and KIT-ML with a single motion autoencoder and a single text-conditioned SiT, using dataset conditioning and dataset-specific recovery to preserve each dataset’s native skeletal definition without retargeting or joint padding.

The root latent is decoded independently to reconstruct the global root sequence as bR = Droot (zroot ). Al-though encoded separately, local articulation and global movement remain coupled in natural mo-tion. We therefore condition image decoding on the root latent, as shown in Fig. 3 (a). The image decoder first maps the image latent to decoder features. A learned conditioner transforms the root la-tent to match their channel dimension and temporal resolution, then broadcasts the resulting features along the joint axis. These conditioning features are added to the image decoding features before the first upsampling stage. We denote the complete root-conditioned image decoder by Dimg :

ˆImot = Dimg (zimg ; zroot ) . (4) The root latent thus supports both root-motion reconstruction and local-motion decoding, coupling the two components while retaining separate latent representations.

With the motion autoencoder frozen, we use a text-conditioned Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT) to jointly generate the image and root latents, as illustrated in Fig. 3 (b). 

Latent interpolation. For each stream s ∈ { img , root }, we use the encoder’s posterior mean as the clean latent zs and independently sample Gaussian noise ϵs ∼ N (0, I). Both streams share the same generative time t ∈ [0 , 1] , which is distinct from the motion-frame index. We use a linear interpolant with its corresponding velocity target: 

xs(t) = tzs + (1 − t)ϵs, us = zs − ϵs. (5) 

Text-conditioned joint latent modeling. Given a text description, a frozen text encoder provides both a global text condition ctext and token-level text features. The global condition is combined with the timestep embedding to modulate the SiT blocks through adaptive layer normalization, while the token-level features are projected into text prefix tokens htext for joint self-attention with the motion tokens. To construct the joint input sequence, we tokenize the current image and root states separately and project the resulting motion tokens to the Transformer’s hidden dimension. The text prefix tokens use the same hidden dimension, allowing all three token sequences to be concatenated: 

H(t) = [ htext ; Tok root (xroot (t)) ; Tok img (ximg (t))] . The common image representation enables shared modeling across HumanML3D and KIT-ML de-spite their different native joint counts and skeletal definitions. We first train a single motion au-toencoder on both datasets, sharing the image and root encoders, decoders, and root conditioner. Each motion is normalized using dataset-specific training statistics and mapped to a fixed-resolution skeleton image with a complementary root sequence. Decoded outputs are restored to the cor-responding dataset’s native joint–time resolution, joint order, and coordinate scale. This enables parameter sharing without skeleton retargeting or joint padding. We then freeze the shared autoencoder and train a single SiT on paired latents from both datasets. To specify the target dataset during generation, we augment the global condition with a learnable dataset embedding ed:

cd = τ (t) + ψ(ctext ) + ed, (8) where τ (t) is the timestep embedding, ψ projects the global text condition ctext to the SiT hidden dimension, and ed is the learnable embedding associated with target dataset d. The combined con-dition modulates the shared Transformer blocks through adaptive layer normalization, while text prefix conditioning remains unchanged. For classifier-free guidance, we jointly drop the text condition and replace the dataset ID with a null ID during training. At inference, the conditional branch uses the target dataset embedding, while the unconditional branch uses a learned null embedding e0. Generated latents are decoded by the shared autoencoder and recovered in the target dataset’s native skeletal representation. 3.5 TRAINING OBJECTIVES 

We first train the motion autoencoder with reconstruction losses and variational regularization, then freeze it and train the SiT with latent flow matching and decoded joint-position supervision.

For the image stream, reconstruction combines mean absolute and mean squared errors between the input and reconstructed skeleton images; for the root stream, it uses an L1 loss on root features. Evaluation Metrics. We follow the MARDM evaluation protocol (Meng et al., 2025), using its pretrained evaluators for the 67-dimensional HumanML3D and 64-dimensional KIT-ML represen-tations. The T2M-style evaluator (Guo et al., 2022) measures text–motion alignment through R-Precision (Top-1, Top-2, and Top-3; higher is better) and Matching Score (lower is better). We also report Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID; lower is better) between generated and real motion embeddings to assess distributional similarity. Finally, we use MARDM’s CLIP-based motion–text evaluator to report CLIP-score (higher is better) as an additional measure of text–motion alignment. We repeat evaluation 20 times and report mean results with 95% confidence intervals. 

Implementation Details. We train both dataset-specific models and a shared model on Hu-manML3D and KIT-ML using the two-stage procedure in Sec. 3.5. The motion autoencoder uses an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 image encoder (He et al., 2016; Deng et al., 2009) and a 1D convolutional root encoder. With the autoencoder frozen, we train SiT-L (Ma et al., 2024) to jointly generate both latent streams, using a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 text encoder (Radford et al., 2021) for conditioning. For shared training, we mix HumanML3D and KIT-ML samples at a 3:1 ratio, ap-proximately reflecting their relative dataset sizes, and use corresponding dataset weights of 0.75 and 0.25. At inference, we jointly sample both latent streams with a 50-step Euler solver and classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022), using guidance scales of 5.5 for HumanML3D and 4.5 for KIT-ML. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and run on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Further architecture and training details are provided in Appendix B. 4.2 COMPARISON WITH EXISTING METHODS

## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we show that image generative architectures can effectively model text-conditioned 3D human motion through a structured, image-compatible representation. Our framework com-bines topology-informed joint–time images with a complementary root-motion stream, coupling the two through root-conditioned autoencoding and joint SiT generation. Supervision on decoded 3D joint positions further constrains generation in motion space. This representation also enables a single motion autoencoder and a single SiT to model HumanML3D and KIT-ML jointly, pre-serving their native skeletal definitions without retargeting. Experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art dataset-specific results and further improvements through shared training on both benchmarks. These findings highlight the potential of image-compatible representations for motion generation across heterogeneous skeletal layouts. Future work will explore this formulation across a broader range of skeletal structures and motion datasets.

## B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

B.1 MOTION AUTOENCODER ARCHITECTURE 

Image encoder. Skeleton images have a resolution of 256 × 256 . The image encoder uses the stem and the first three residual stages of an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone. The resulting image latent has 16 channels and a spatial resolution of 16 × 16 .

Image decoder. The image decoder first maps the 16-channel image latent to 256-channel fea-tures using a 3 × 3 convolution with padding 1, followed by group normalization with 32 groups, a SiLU activation, and a residual block. Root conditioning is added to these features before the first upsampling stage. Four successive stages then increase the spatial resolution from 16 × 16 to 

256 × 256 , with channel dimensions 256 → 128 → 64 → 32 → 16 . Each stage consists of 2×

nearest-neighbor upsampling, a 3 × 3 convolution, group normalization, a SiLU activation, and a residual block. Group normalization uses 32 groups in the first three stages and 16 groups in the final stage. The output head applies a 3 × 3 convolution with 16 output channels, group normalization with 16 groups, a SiLU activation, and a final 3 × 3 convolution to reconstruct the three-channel skeleton image. Together with the root conditioner, this complete decoding process is denoted by 

Dimg (zimg ; zroot ) in Sec. 3.2. 

Root encoder. The four-channel root sequence is first linearly interpolated to 16 temporal positions, then processed by two length-preserving 1D convolutions with kernel size 3 and channel dimensions 

4 → 128 → 128 . Each convolution is followed by group normalization with 32 groups and a SiLU activation. Separate kernel-size-one convolutional heads predict the posterior mean and log variance, yielding a root latent of size 8 × 16 (channels × temporal positions). Temporal compression is performed by the initial interpolation, while the convolutional layers preserve the sequence length. 

Root decoder. The root decoder applies two length-preserving 1D convolutions with kernel size 3 and channel dimensions 8 → 128 → 128 . Each convolution is followed by group normalization 13 Preprint with 32 groups and a SiLU activation. A final kernel-size-one convolution maps the features to four root channels, which are then linearly interpolated to the original sequence length T .

Root conditioner. The root conditioner uses two kernel-size-one 1D convolutions with channel dimensions 8 → 256 → 256 . Group normalization with 32 groups and a SiLU activation are applied between the two convolutions. The resulting features are temporally aligned with the image decoding features by linear interpolation when needed, then broadcast along the joint axis. In our configuration, this produces a conditioning tensor with 256 channels and a spatial resolution of

Decoded geometry weight. We examine the effect of supervision on decoded 3D joint positions by varying λpos .
