1. Introduction 

Many authors studied which integral quadratic forms are universal , i.e. represent all natural numbers. The most famous example is the sum of four squares; its universality is precisely the Four-square theorem of Lagrange [La]. Since it is well known that no positive definite quadratic form in three variables over Z can be universal, the universal forms in four variables received particular attention. For example, Liouville [Li] proved that there are exactly seven universal forms of the type x2 + ay 2 + bz 2 + abw 2; from Pépin’s work [Pep] follows the universality of several other quaternary quadratic forms. Ramanujan [Ra] then completely classified which forms 

ax 2 +by 2 +cz 2 +dw 2 are universal, making the famous mistake of including the form with coefficients (1 , 2, 5, 5), which in fact represents all natural numbers except 15. This was pointed out by Dickson [Di], who also systematically studied universality of non-diagonal forms (i.e. with cross-terms) and introduced the word universal itself. Willerding’s work [Wi] contains a classification of quaternary universal quadratic forms that are classical , i.e. all their cross-terms are even. Much later, universal classical quadratic forms of any rank have been elegantly classified by the 15-Theorem of Conway– Schneeberger [Co] (a simpler proof has been provided by Bhargava [Bh]); this also allowed to check Willerding’s list and correct mistakes therein. The generalisation to non-classical quadratic forms is the 290-Theorem by Bhargava–Hanke, which has a very complicated proof that has not yet been fully published. Thus, universal quadratic forms over Q are now fully understood. However, there is a parallel story over number fields. We first introduce the necessary terminology. Note that Theorem 1 for the case when k is a prime or a power of 2 was proved in [KY3, Thm. 1.4]. Let us give an overview of results that directly concern Kitaoka’s conjecture. We already mentioned that by Earnest–Khosravani [EK], a universal ternary form can only exist in fields of even degree. Chan–Kim–Raghavan [CKR] fully characterised quadratic fields admitting a classical universal ternary form by proving that the only such fields are Q(√2), Q(√3) and Q(√5). Kim–Kim– Park [KKP] recently showed that there are only finitely many quadratic fields admitting a universal form in seven variables, which of course raises the question whether Kitaoka’s conjecture is not in fact valid with “ternary” replaced by “septenary”. Nevertheless, even the original Kitaoka’s conjecture continues to defy proof. The case of degree four has been studied by K.–Tinková– Zemková in [KTZ] for biquadratic fields and then fully solved in the recent preprint Kramer–K. [KK]; it turns out that there are no quartic fields admitting a classical universal ternary form. Based on this and other evidence, the authors ventured to conjecture a strengthened version of Kitaoka’s conjecture: 

Conjecture 2 ([KK, Conj. 1.2]) . Let K be a totally real number field such that there exists a classical universal ternary quadratic form. Then K = Q(√2) , Q(√3) , or Q(√5) .

Apart from evidence from low-degree fields, the only general results are the “Weak Kitaoka Theorem” by Kala–Yatsyna [KY2] that Kitaoka’s conjecture holds for fields of every fixed degree, and the recent paper [KKK] by Kala–Kramer–K. which connects Kitaoka’s conjecture with sums of squares by showing that if K̸ ∋ √2 admits a classical universal ternary form, then every element of 2 O+

One can also hope to use the present result for an attack on Kitaoka’s conjecture for a general totally real number field K: On the one hand, if K is abelian, then it is contained in some cyclotomic field Q(ζk) and hence also in its maximal totally real subfield Fk = Q(ζk)+. On the other hand, and this is probably the more promising direction, it is natural to classify K based on the real cyclotomic fields Fk it contains, and to try to extend the argument for the non-existence of a universal ternary form over a maximal subfield Fk to the same argument over K. We succeed with this idea in the case when K contains a nonsquare totally positive unit, see Theorem 10. 2. Preliminaries

First of all, we already defined a quadratic form, but many of the modern results are formulated in a more general framework of quadratic lattices. A quadratic lattice (or simply a lattice) is a pair ( L, Q ) where L is a finitely generated torsion-free OK -module equipped with a quadratic map Q : L → O K , i.e. a map such that Q(αu) = α2Q(u) and that the associated symmetric map

Every quadratic form Q can be interpreted as a free quadratic lattice ( O nK , Q ), and the notions of classicality, universality, total positive definiteness and rank carry over. This also explains why classical quadratic forms are so important – they correspond to integral symmetric bilinear forms. Conversely, every free quadratic lattice gives rise to a quadratic form simply by choosing a basis. As a trivial corollary, a field that admits a universal ternary form also admits a universal ternary lattice. Hence, Kitaoka’s conjecture is sometimes formulated in a stronger version for lattices (cf. the original wording of Conjecture 2 in [KK, Conj. 1.2]). A significant part of our results handles this more general case (see Theorem 10), but sometimes we rely on results that have not been proven for non-free lattices. We are now ready to prove the main theorem. It is interesting to note that the proof considers three separate types of Fk based on the value of k, and in each of these types, there is exactly one exceptional field that does admit a classical universal ternary form. 

Proof of Theorem 1. First of all, the fields Q(√2), Q(√3) and Q(√5) indeed admit a classical universal ternary form by [CKR]. The main task is to prove the other implication. Let Fk = Q(ζk)+. We will distinguish several cases. First, if k is a power of 2, then Fk admits a classical universal ternary form if and only if Fk = Q(√2) by [KY3, Thm. 1.4]. If k is not a power of 2 and 4 | k, then by Lemma 8(c), Fk contains a nonsquare totally positive unit. Thus, by Corollary 11, it can only admit a classical universal ternary lattice if Fk = Q(√3). Finally, if 4 ∤ k, then we can assume that k is odd, since Fk = F2k for odd k by Lemma 5. As 2 is not a divisor of k, the prime 2 is unramified in Fk by Lemma 4. Thus Fk admits no classical universal ternary form unless Fk = Q(√5) by [KKK, Thm. 1.1]. □
