SHARP CONSTANTS AND EXTREMIZERS IN HOMOGENEOUS TREE BMO 

MOHAMMADREZA MOHAJER 

Abstract. We develop a finite-tree formalism for sharp inequalities on real-valued, mean-centered L1-BMO over homogeneous q-ary filtrations. A reduction theorem identifies local and global sharp constants with the supremum of finite-depth constants. Finite anchoring gives compact optimization problems and rational extremizers under rational piecewise-linear hypotheses, while stable centering shows that imposing zero root average preserves the supremum over all depths. The principal application is the absolute-value map: we construct centered examples in which taking absolute values increases the BMO seminorm and obtain bounds strictly below the universal factor 2. Jensen-defect Carleson functionals and martingale-difference envelopes provide further applications, illustrating distinct anchoring mechanisms and the differences between finite-depth rationality, stabilization, and global attainment. 

1. Introduction 

Sharp inequalities in BMO depend on both the family of averaging sets and the choice of oscillation seminorm. The absolute-value map provides a basic example. For the mean-centered L1-BMO seminorm introduced by John and Nirenberg [7], the elementary estimate 

∥ | f | ∥ ∗ ≤ 2∥f ∥∗

holds for any family of averaging sets. We call the absolute-value map contractive if ∥ | f | ∥ ∗ ≤ ∥ f ∥∗

for every f . We say that amplification occurs when ∥ | f | ∥ ∗ > ∥f ∥∗ for some f . Korenovskii proved that the optimal constant is 1 when all subintervals of an interval are used [8]. The corresponding problem for more general averaging families is discussed by Dafni and Gibara [2, Section 5]. On a fixed dyadic filtration, the arrangement of values across generations creates different extremal behaviour. We develop a finite-tree framework for studying sharp constants of functionals on BMO. The framework connects inequalities on an entire filtration, inequalities on a rooted descendant tree, and finite-dimensional optimization over leaf values. Its main contributions concern finite reduction, attainment, rationality, and the effect of imposing zero root average. Three applications illustrate these results: Jensen-defect Carleson functionals, the absolute-value map, and martingale-difference envelopes. Our setting is a homogeneous q-ary filtration T on a measure space ( X, μ ), in which every node has q children of equal measure. Slavin and Vasyunin study rooted α-trees, allowing unequal child measures bounded below by the fraction α of their parent measure [11, Definition 1.4 and Remark 1.5]. Each rooted descendant tree in our setting is a homogeneous special case with 

α = 1 /q . Our global filtration has generations indexed by Z, so every node has ancestors at arbitrarily large scales (Definition 2.1). This bi-infinite structure accommodates both standard dyadic cubes in Rd, with q = 2 d, and balls in non-Archimedean local fields with residue cardinality prime power q. For BMO and singular integrals over local fields, see [3].

Absolute value does not increase the mean-centered L 2-BMO seminorm, since taking absolute values cannot increase variance. For L 1-BMO, the arithmetic mean need not minimize average absolute deviation, and the composition constant can exceed 1. The extension, finite reduction, anchoring, and centering methods developed here extend to Lp-BMO, 1 ≤ p < ∞, with suitable bounds. The L 1 setting has the further advantage that its finite-depth constraints are given by rational linear inequalities for which the associated finite-depth constants are rational with rational maximizer. The general framework is developed in Section 2. For compatible positively homogeneous local functionals satisfying a lower semicontinuity condition, the local and global sharp constants equal the supremum of the finite-depth constants (Theorem 2.1). Finite anchoring accounts for the dependence of a functional on additive constants and reduces the finite-depth problem to compact sets. When the functional is rational piecewise linear and the anchors are rational, the sharp constant is rational and is attained by a rational vector (Theorem 2.2). Stable centering relates the centered and uncentered problems. For hereditary functionals admitting zero-leaf anchoring, every admissible depth-m configuration gives a centered admissible configuration of depth at most m + ⌈qm/ (q − 1) ⌉ with no smaller functional value (Theorem 2.3). Consequently, the centered and uncentered constants have the same supremum over all depths. A corresponding result holds for general finite anchors (Corollary 2.2). The first application concerns Jensen defects (Section 3). The Jensen gap on a node decomposes exactly into nonnegative contributions from successive generations, and the Carleson norm of these contributions equals the supremum of the Jensen gaps. For Φ( t) = |t|, optimizing over translations recovers the input BMO seminorm and gives node anchoring, while anchoring at leaf values fails (Theorem 3.1). For every convex Lipschitz Φ, the sharp packing constant is half the difference between its limiting slopes at + ∞ and −∞ , whether or not Φ is homogeneous (Theorem 3.2). Our formalism identifies the local and global sharp constants with the supremum of the finite-depth constants. It does not determine whether a particular sequence of finite-depth constants stabilizes. By Theorem 2.3, the centered and uncentered finite-depth problems have the same supremum when taken over all depths. For absolute value, the exact value of c(q) remains the principal question. The bounds proved here are 25 24 ≤ c(2) ≤ 18 11 , ∆q + q − 2

There are several concrete remaining problems. (1) Determine c(q), beginning with q = 2. In particular, find a sharp upper bound that accounts for how the distributions on a node and its descendants constrain one another. Applying the scalar estimate of Lemma 4.1 independently at each node does not capture these multigeneration constraints. (2) Find the least depth at which absolute value increases the BMO seminorm on a binary tree, and determine further exact finite-depth constants. We know c1(2) = c2(2) = 1 and 

c4(2) ≥ 41 /40, but have not determined c3(2) or c4(2). The reduction to centered cases in Proposition 4.1 and the family of finite linear programs (4.2) provide a precise finite certification problem. The linear programs in (4.2) compute the uncentered constants. Analogous programs compute the centered constants by imposing zero root average and allowing the output oscillation to be evaluated at any node. (3) Decide whether cm(q) = c(q) for some finite m. If equality holds, rationality and attainment at depth m (Proposition 4.1) give a rational sharp constant and a finite-depth extremizer. Stable centering (Theorem 2.3) then gives a centered extremizer at a possibly greater SHARP CONSTANTS AND EXTREMIZERS IN HOMOGENEOUS TREE BMO 29 

finite depth. Whether rationality of c(q) would imply stabilization for the absolute-value family is unresolved. For general functionals, rationality alone is insufficient: the weighted example in Section 5.3 with wm = 1 − 2−m has rational global constant and no finite-depth stabilization. (4) Determine optimal centering bounds for non-homogenueous trees in which every node has at least two children and each child has relative measure at least α > 0. The construction of Theorem 2.3 gives a finite depth bound depending on α and the initial root mean; the question is the best possible dependence. Norm-preserving extension (Lemma 2.1) and finite-depth approximation (Lemma 2.2) continue to apply under the same differentiation hypothesis. The question is how many ancestor generations are needed to center an admissible configuration while keeping its BMO seminorm at most 1. If finite-depth constants are to be independent of the root, the tree isomorphisms used in Definition 2.3 must preserve relative child measures. Rational child proportions would retain rational linear constraints for the finite L 1 problems. The examples reveal different extremal behaviour within the same tree geometry. An optimal translation may be determined by a node average or by a leaf value, while finite-depth constants can behave differently as the depth grows. The formalism treats these features separately and provides a setting for studying other functionals arising in the analysis of tree BMO.
