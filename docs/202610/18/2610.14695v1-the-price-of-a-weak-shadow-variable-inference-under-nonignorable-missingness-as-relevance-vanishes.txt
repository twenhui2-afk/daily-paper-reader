Abstract 

When an outcome is missing not at random, identification is commonly restored by a shadow variable: a covariate that predicts the outcome but is excluded from the response mechanism. The literature treats the first requirement, relevance, as a yes-or-no condition. We make it continuous, parametrizing it by a scalar that scales the shadow variable’s entire contribution to the moment conditions, and ask what inference costs as that scalar vanishes. One functional of the inverse weight governs the answer. We characterize it exactly, show that it fixes the effective sample size, and use it to sharpen the rates of the estimator that avoids the weight. The characterization is concrete. Whether the functional saturates or diverges turns not on boundedness of the outcome but on whether the tilting variable has an atom at its essential supremum: a binary outcome saturates, a uniform one does not. It also bounds how far a scanned set reaches, and that reach grows only as the square root of the logarithm of the sample size, so a thousandfold increase buys one unit of tilt while the set to be scanned widens without limit. 

Keywords: Effective sample size · Exponential tilt · Identification-robust inference · Missing not at random · Nonresponse instrument. 

# 1 Introduction 

Let 𝑌 be an outcome observed only when 𝑅 = 1 , with covariates 𝑋 always observed, and suppose the response probability depends on 𝑌 itself. Such missingness is not ignorable, and without further structure neither the distribution of 𝑌 nor the parameters of a model for it are identified. The device that has become standard is a shadow variable : a further covariate 

𝑍 , always observed, that predicts 𝑌 but is conditionally independent of 𝑅 given (𝑌 , 𝑋) (Tang et al., 2003; d’Haultfœuille, 2010; Wang et al., 2014; Miao and Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2016). Under completeness conditions the model is then point identified, and a range of estimators is available: semiparametric likelihood (Qin et al., 2002; Chen and Fang, 2019), pseudo-likelihood (Zhao and Shao, 2015), and estimators that avoid the inverse weight altogether (Li et al., 2023b). In all of this the shadow variable’s predictive power enters as a condition to be assumed: com-pleteness, or non-degeneracy of a coefficient, or a rank condition. It is either satisfied or it is not. The point is clearest where the shadow variable is not given but searched for. Chen et al. (2021), Zhao et al. (2021) and Chen and Shao (2025) select one from a set of candidate covariates, and 1arXiv:2610.14695v1 [stat.ME] 10 Oct 2026 what their criteria detect is the exclusion restriction; relevance enters as the companion require-ment that the candidate be related to the outcome, which a candidate either is or is not. Chen et al. (2021) are explicit about the consequence: their criterion takes the same limiting value zero at a candidate that is excluded and relevant and at one that is excluded and irrelevant, so what it consistently recovers is the set of covariates satisfying exclusion, and relevance must be assumed away by hypothesis. One recent line drops that condition rather than grading it: Chen et al. (2026) call a shadow variable weak when completeness is not imposed, and obtain partial identification with sharp bounds. The weakness studied here is different in kind. The conditions hold and the parameter stays point identified for every positive relevance; what vanishes is not identification but the information the data carry about it. Yet in applications the condition is satisfied by margins that vary enormously, and the question of what happens in between is not addressed. The purpose of this paper is to make that question precise and answer it. That the question is worth asking is not a new observation. Nakano and Hoshino (2026) study a scalar mean under a linear shadow model and obtain confidence sets valid along sequences in which the relevance drifts to zero: as relevance vanishes their sets come out unbounded, which is what a valid set must do once the data cease to restrict the parameter. Dufour (1997) had already shown, in generality, that any valid confidence set for a parameter that is unidentified on part of the parameter space must be unbounded with probability close to its nominal level, and that Wald sets built from asymptotic standard errors have coverage zero. The observation that relevance may be weak is due to Nakano and Hoshino (2026). What we add is an account of what weak relevance does to each component of the machinery: to the weights, to the conditioning of the sieve, and to the arithmetic. Three findings organize the paper. First, in the moment system that identifies the model the entire contribution of the shadow variable factorizes, at every point of the identified set, as a fixed function multiplied by a scalar 𝜌 (Section 2); relevance is therefore a global rather than a local property, and everything can be followed as a function of it. Second, the second moment of the inverse weight decomposes exactly, with all dependence on the response model carried by a 𝜒 2-divergence Λ of an exponential tilt (Section 3); an integral representation of log Λ yields a sharp dichotomy: Λ is capped when the tilting variable has an atom at its essential supremum, and diverges along the identified set when it does not. Boundedness of the outcome is not the criterion—a uniform outcome is bounded and still diverges—though a binary outcome, having an atom, falls on the capped side. Third, Λ determines the effective sample size exactly, and thereby how far a scanned confidence set can actually be computed. That reach grows like 

(log 𝑛) 1/2 and is free of 𝜌 , whereas the set that must be scanned widens without limit as 𝜌 falls (Section 4). The procedure prescribed to reveal nonidentification therefore conceals it, and gives no sign of having done so. The remaining sections develop the estimation-side consequences. Working with the representer construction of Li et al. (2023b), which avoids the inverse weight, Section 5 shows that a cancel-lation peculiar to the shadow-variable construction makes several rates one power of 𝜌 smaller than norm bounds suggest, and that the resulting threshold for the regularization coincides with the independent condition for the design matrix to be well conditioned—a coherence that the cruder bound does not have. A theorem for a relevance sequence drifting at rate 𝑛 −1/2 shows that none of this is an artefact of fixed-relevance asymptotics. Section 6 reports a simulation study and measures the reach directly over a sixty-four-fold range of sample size. One further term, exponential tilting , names a different object in Caner (2010); Section 2.4 distinguishes the 2two. A companion paper applies the diagnostics developed here to a published credit-scoring analysis and shows that in that setting no usable shadow variable could have existed: a characteristic used by an optimizing scorer cannot satisfy the exclusion restriction. The present paper is self-contained and does not depend on it.

2.4 Two terms used differently elsewhere 

Two collisions of terminology should be disposed of before proceeding. The first was noted in Section 1, and with (4) in hand it can be put exactly. The weakness of Chen et al. (2026) is a failure of completeness, and what they report are identified regions: sets of parameter values the data cannot separate even in the limit. The weakness here is a small 𝜌 .Completeness holds, the parameter is point identified for every 𝜌 > 0 , and what is reported is a confidence set for that point—one the data may locate very poorly, but a confidence set and not an identified region. The distinction matters for what the sets mean: theirs do not shrink with the sample, ours do, but only so slowly that Section 4 becomes the binding constraint. 

Exponential tilting in Caner (2010) names the member of the generalized empirical likelihood family obtained by choosing the exponential divergence in the estimating criterion, and that paper studies it under weak instruments. The tilt in Section 3 is a tilt of the law of the outcome, induced by the response probability, and enters through the weights rather than the criterion. # 4 The reach of a scanned confidence set 

4.1 What is already known, and what is not 

Inference about 𝛾 𝑌 concerns a parameter that is unidentified when 𝜌 = 0 , so the relevant impossibility result applies directly. Dufour (1997) shows that if a parameter with unbounded range is not identified on part of the parameter space, then any confidence set of level 1 − 𝛼 must be unbounded with probability at least 1 − 𝛼 near the point of nonidentification, and that any set failing this characterization—in particular any Wald set built from an asymptotic standard error—has coverage zero. Reporting a finite interval for 𝛾 𝑌 has not been defensible since 1997. The prescribed remedy, followed here and throughout the literature that result has influenced, is to obtain the set by inverting a test, so that it comes out unbounded whenever the data do not restrict the parameter. Carrying out the prescription is itself a source of error, as Schlemper and Moreira (2026) demon-strate for linear instrumental-variable models: inverting a test on a grid can miss disconnected components and, more seriously, report a bounded interval when the set is unbounded, which the standard implementation does in more than forty per cent of the specifications they survey (their Section 3). Their remedy replaces the grid with algebra: the statistics they invert are rational in the parameter, so the set solves a polynomial inequality recoverable by root finding, and more general tests are approximated by polynomials after compactifying the parameter space by a smooth bijection, which turns the two infinite ends into points at which the statis-tic is evaluated (their Sections 4 and 5.2). The extension beyond linear instrumental variables preserves that algebraic requirement (their Section 6.1). Here the weights are exponential in 𝛾 𝑌 , so no component of (3) is polynomial or rational in it and the algebraic route is not available—not inadequate, but unavailable. Nor is the compactification 10 a way round it, although of their devices it is the one closest to the present concern: it converts the infinite ends into two points at which the statistic must be evaluated, and that evaluation is exactly what the arithmetic prevents. A point at which the statistic cannot be evaluated is no more useful for being finite. What remains is a scan, and for a scan the question is how far it can be carried. Theorem 4.1 answers it, and the answer is indifferent to which statistic is inverted and to how finely the curve is traced: beyond a point located exactly below, the functional the weights depend on exceeds the largest representable number. Where the structure permits their methods the grid ceases to be a source of error; where it does not, the binding limit is arithmetical rather than one of implementation.

4.3 Why the remedy conceals what it was meant to reveal 

The two halves of the problem move in opposite directions. By Lemma 2.1 the shadow variable contributes 𝐸{𝑔 𝑍 (𝜃(𝑠))} = 𝜌𝜑(𝑠) , so as 𝜌 falls the 𝑍 -moment flattens along the curve and the set of 𝛾 𝑌 not rejected widens without limit. By Proposition 4.1 and Theorem 4.1, the range that can be scanned is free of 𝜌 and grows only logarithmically in 𝑛 .The range one must scan grows as relevance falls; the range one can scan does not. The scan therefore terminates before the statistic does, at a point that barely moves when the sample is enlarged. The output is a bounded interval whose width is almost constant in 𝑛 , and nothing distinguishes it from a genuine finite confidence set: the procedure gives no indication whether it stopped because the statistic rose above its critical value or because the arithmetic gave out. Satisfying Dufour’s characterization in exact arithmetic does not imply satisfying it in floating point, and the failure is silent. 

4.4 The atomic case: saturation instead of overflow 

When the outcome is binary—so that the tilt concentrates on an atom, in the sense of Corol-lary 3.5—part (i) of Proposition 3.1 caps Λ at the finite limit (11), and neither the explosion of Section 4.3 nor the ceiling of Theorem 4.1 can occur. The failure inverts. Because Λ is bounded, so is the second moment of the weight, and the effective sample size of Corollary 3.1 stays a fixed fraction of 𝑛 however far the scan is carried; the arithmetic never gives out. What gives out instead is the signal. By Corollary 3.5, log Λ approaches its limit at an exponential rate, so beyond a moderate |𝛾 𝑌 | the moment condition ceases to discriminate between values of the response coefficient. The consequence is a threshold rather than a truncation. Writing the identifying signal at position 𝑠 as 𝜌𝜑(𝑠) by Lemma 2.1, and its sampling error as 𝑂 𝑝 (𝑛 −1/2 ), the scan can separate 𝑠

It is also the case that arises whenever the outcome is binary, as it is when the outcome is default on a loan.

5.5 Misspecification of the sieve 

The divergence of 𝛿 0 like 𝜌 −1 recorded in Lemma 5.1 is an instance of irregular identification in the sense of Khan and Tamer (2010): the object being estimated is a functional whose representation requires weights that grow without bound as identification weakens, and the consequence there, as here, is a departure from the regular parametric rate. What is specific to the present construction is that the divergence is indexed by a single scalar and cancels in 

𝑣 T𝑀 −1 .Condition (15) requires the representer to lie in the span of 𝑏 . 5.6 Choosing the regularization 

Rate-optimal regularization is a developed subject, and our situation is not a special case of it: Carrasco et al. (2007) and Darolles et al. (2011) choose 𝜆 from the noise level and the smoothness of the solution, for operators whose singular values decay at a given rate, whereas the degeneracy here is a uniform scaling of a finite-dimensional block (Lemma 5.1) governed by a parameter, the relevance, that does not appear in their rates. # 7 Discussion 

The literature treats the relevance of a shadow variable as a condition to be satisfied. Making it a scalar and following it through the problem changes what can be said. The identifying signal factorizes through that scalar at every point of the identified set (Lemma 2.1); the second moment of the inverse weight is an exponential-tilt divergence that determines the effective sample size exactly (Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.1) and is itself free of relevance (Proposition 4.1); and the two facts pull in opposite directions, so that the range one must scan grows as relevance falls while the range one can scan does not (Theorem 4.1). None of this is a statement about a 22 particular estimator. It is a statement about what any procedure built on inverse weights must contend with. Two consequences are practical. First, an effective sample size should be evaluated at the estimated response model, not at ignorability; the two can differ by a factor of two, in the direction that matters. Second, a confidence set obtained by scanning should record why it closed. Dufour (1997) requires unboundedness where the data are uninformative, and inverting a statistic delivers it in exact arithmetic; in floating point the scan may instead stop because 

Λ overflowed, and the two outcomes are indistinguishable in the output. Reporting the reach alongside the set costs nothing and distinguishes them. Several limitations qualify all of this, and one of them is structural. The factorization is a property of the parametrization (4), not of the model; a different parametrization of relevance would not factorize, and the results are stated for this one. The regularization of Section 5 is derived only above a transition: (24) balances a proved bias against a variance form that is established numerically, and the balance is meaningful only where the variance exists, which is where 𝜌 √𝑛 is large. That the fitted rule holds on both sides of that transition is unexplained. The binary case deserves a final word, because it inverts the paper’s main failure without re-pairing its cause. When the tilting variable has an atom at its essential supremum, as a binary outcome does, Λ saturates (Proposition 3.1), the arithmetic never gives out, and the scanned set is honestly unbounded when the data are uninformative—the behavior Dufour (1997) pre-scribes. That is the benign case. But saturation is not identification: beyond a moderate |𝛾 𝑌 |

the moment condition simply ceases to discriminate, and the threshold (14) says when. The difference between the two cases is whether the analyst is told. Two questions are left open, and both are narrower than they were. The variance form (23) on which the tuning rule rests is what the noise transmission through (̂𝑀 +𝜆𝐼) −1 suggests and what the well-conditioned cells show, but it is not derived; and nothing here explains why the fitted rule (21) should continue to hold below the transition, where the second moment is infinite and the balance (24) has no meaning. A rate-optimal penalty in the sense of Carrasco et al. (2007) would settle the first. The second is the more interesting: it asks what selects 𝜆 when there is no variance to trade against bias, and the answer cannot be a moment calculation. What the relevance parameter buys, finally, is a diagnostic vocabulary. A companion paper puts it to work on a published credit-scoring analysis and finds a shadow variable whose first-stage analogue falls an order of magnitude below the conventional standard, and an effective sample size at the published estimate of under two per cent of the observations. Neither quantity could have been computed before, and either would have stopped the analysis.

C.6 The regime boundary, and the rule out of sample 

The material behind Where the rule comes from , Section 5.7 of the body above. The design is a grid of (𝜌, 𝑛) cells. At each cell the estimator is run over a fixed ladder of eighteen ridge values on common random numbers, and the bias and spread curves are read off the resulting error matrix. Every cell uses 1200 replications, and each cell’s count is stored with its output in the replication package. One condition on that derivation deserves emphasis, because it is what defeated two earlier attempts of our own. The expansion assumes the second moment exists, and it need not. The smallest singular value of ̂ 𝑀 has mean of order 𝜌 and standard deviation of order 𝑛 −1/2 , so its distance from the origin in its own standard deviations is of order 𝜌 √𝑛 . When that is small, ̂ 𝑀 is near-singular with non-negligible probability, the error behaves like the reciprocal of a quantity with a density at zero, and its tail index is one: the variance is infinite and there is nothing for 28 a bias–variance argument to balance. Measured tail indices run from 1.00 at 𝜌 √𝑛 small to 4.79 at 𝜌 √𝑛 large, and the transition is at about four standard deviations. The same prediction that is accurate to a factor of 1.05 above the transition is out by 2 × 10 5 below it.
