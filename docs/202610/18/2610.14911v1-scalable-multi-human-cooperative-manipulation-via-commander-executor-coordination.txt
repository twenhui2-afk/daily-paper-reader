# Scalable Multi-Human Cooperative Manipulation via Commander-Executor Coordination 

Buzhen Huang, Xingchen Wu, Yuanwang Yang, Chenyi Xie and Kun Li ∗

✦

Abstract —Multi-human cooperative object manipulation is a highly challenging topic in humanoid control. While existing methods can handle simple collaborative carrying tasks, they struggle with role-dependent, long-horizon, and sequential cooperative manipulation. To address this issue, we propose a hierarchical commander-executor framework that enables multiple agents to accomplish complex col-laborative tasks in distinct ways. Specifically, a commander combines instructions and scene information for joint semantic and geometric planning. It then decomposes the task into role-specific subtasks and generates shared object-centric coordination plans, specifying execu-tion timing, target positions, and manipulation actions for each agent. Conditioned on the commander’s high-level strategy and each agent’s local observations, the executor learns a low-level physics-based policy through a solo-to-cooperative curriculum to realize coordinated human-object interactions. The planned object trajectory provides a shared co-ordination target, while root positions and grasping instructions specify role-dependent execution cues. We evaluate our method on a variety of object manipulation tasks, demonstrating its ability to execute long-horizon tasks that combine different types of collaboration and to scale from two-agent to multi-agent cooperation. Extensive experiments fur-ther show that our approach achieves higher success rates compared to state-of-the-art methods. Code will be made publicly available at https://github.com/TJU-3DV/Co-Manipulation. 

Index Terms —Cooperative manipulation, character animation, human-object interaction, motion planning, multi-person interaction. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

# LONG -HORIZON collaborative object manipulation among multiple ag-ents is a crucial capability for embodied hu-manoid systems in complex scenarios. It requires a diverse set of skills, including cooperative transportation, object handover, and collision-free coordination in cluttered or constrained environ-ments. However, enabling such collaboration remains challenging, as agents must plan long-horizon manipulation strategies while adapting their actions to one another throughout execution. Conventional kinematics-based methods [1]–[3] often produce physically implausible artifacts such as penetration and sliding, limiting their applicability to embodied interaction tasks. Recently, dynamics-based methods have been able to produce physically plausible motion, but most existing works remain limited to single-agent object interaction [4]–[6]. Only a few works have attempted to model multi-agent object interaction. However, these methods either rely on simplified object assumptions [7] for collaboration

Moreover, existing approaches mainly focus on cooperative carrying, limiting their ability to handle diverse collaborative behaviors such as handover and long-horizon interactions in complex scenarios. We observe that in real-world collaborative tasks,a commander plays a key role in ensuring successful task execution and signifi-cantly enhancing team efficiency. In a long-horizon complex col-laboration task, the commander understands the global objective, decomposes it into coordinated subtasks, assigns responsibilities to different roles, and provides a high-level strategy for collabo-ration. The performers execute the planner’s scheme by following their assigned roles, coordinating with one another through shared task references, and progressively adapting their behaviors from individual execution to cooperative interaction. Based on this observation, we propose a commander-executor framework that first generates task plans by jointly considering task semantics and scene geometry, and then trains a collaboration-aware control policy for executing diverse interaction tasks in complex scenes. Specifically, the commander first represents the scene config-urations as scene graph and uses LLMs [9] with an instruction to reason about the semantic relations among agents, objects, and goals. Since semantic reasoning alone may produce plans that are difficult to execute physically, we further incorporate scene geometry to derive feasible coordination cues, including obstacle-aware human and object trajectories and role-specific execution cues. These cues serve as a shared task reference that specifies not only the final goal, but also how different agents should coordinate over time. Conditioned on this high-level strategy and each agent’s local observations, the executor learns a control policy with sparse guidance rather than full-body motion references, reducing the dependence on scarce multi-agent interaction data. To improve stability and scalability, we train the single-agent policy with augmented objects, contacts, and trajectories, enabling it to learn robust multi-phase manipulation skills such as approaching, grasp-ing, and carrying. Since the single-agent policy shares the same object-centric strategy representation as in collaborative tasks, the learned policy can be efficiently transferred to multi-agent cooper-ation without relying on paired multi-agent interaction data. As a result, given a scene mesh and object-level task goals, our method can generate feasible manipulation strategies and control multiple humanoids to perform coordinated long-horizon interactions, such as handover and cooperative carrying, across diverse object types. 2 RELATED WORK 

Human-human-objection interaction. Human-Human-Object Interaction requires modeling both human-object interaction and multi-person collaboration. Recent datasets such as Core4D [10] and InterVLA [11] have advanced this direction. Recent gener-ative methods have explored different ways to model collabo-rative human-object-human interactions. COLLAGE [12] com-bines large language models with hierarchical latent diffusion to generate collaborative interactions. OnlineHOI [13] introduces Mamba with a memory-augmented network for online multi-person-object motion generation from historical states. SyncD-iff [14] improves coordination through synchronized motion diffu-sion, while HHOI [15] composes human-object and human-human interaction priors to synthesize human-human-object interactions. Centering on object trajectory, [1], [16] use a flow matching network to generate interactive motions between two persons and object. However, these methods do not fully consider the physical constraints. Beyond generative approaches, some methods intro-duce physical constraints or explicit interaction modeling. QP-based methods [17] require predefined contact points and often produce less natural motions, while IG-based methods [18] rely on reference motion imitation and lack generalizability. Recent physics-based methods such as CooHOI [7] and TeamHOI [8] improve multi-person carrying with object dynamics and inter-agent coordination, but remain limited to simple cooperative carrying and cannot support diverse collaboration patterns. In contrast, our method combines task- and geometry-aware strategy planning with physics-based policy execution, enabling diverse long-horizon collaborative manipulation across different objects, collaboration types, and numbers of agents. 

Kinematics-based motion generation. Kinematics-based meth-ods focus on generating visually smooth and coherent human motions, often without explicit modeling of physical laws such as gravity or contact forces. Approaches such as [2], [19]– [27] generate human motion from textual descriptions or vision clips, yet they are generally limited to single-person or person-to-person interactions, without incorporating objects. In contrast, methods like [3], [28]–[35] extend motion generation to hu-man–object interactions, producing motions conditioned on text or specific action instructions. However, these methods remain largely constrain-ed to single-person scenarios and, due to the lack of physical constraints, often suffer from artifacts such as foot sliding, penetration, and physically implausible motions. Our method addresses these issues by leveraging physics simulation, enabling physically consistent motion generation for two-person object interactions. 

Physics-based motion generation. Physics-based motion genera-tion methods typically train control policies within a physics simu-lator using reinforcement learning to produce physically plausible behaviors. Early works [5], [36]–[39] primarily focused on motion imitation, relying on carefully designed motion tracking reward functions to drive policies toward accurately reproducing given motion capture sequences. However, the capabilities of imitation learning are inherently limited by the available reference motions. To enable characters to address open-domain, task-oriented prob-lems, recent research has shifted toward reference-free generation paradigms. Representative methods based on Motion Priors [40]– [42] replace tracking of individual reference motions with a discriminator that provides style-based rewards. Combined with task-specific objective rewards, [4], [43]successfully accomplish targeted tasks in a human-like manner. Other methods like [6], [44], [45] use knowledge distillation, where a teacher policy for full-dimensional tracking is distilled into a student policy for par-tial tracking, thereby enabling diverse human-object interactions. Similar to our work, recent studies [4], [46]–[50] use rule-based systems or LLMs/VLMs as planners with physics-based policies to guide agent completing long-horizon tasks. While existing methods have achieved substantial progress, they predominantly address single-agent interactions with object and largely overlook multi-character scenarios. 3 METHOD 

As shown in fig. 2, this paper introduces a framework for multi-agent collaborative object manipulation across diverse objects and varied collaboration types in complex scenarios. Since enabling agents to understand how to collaborate is crucial for success-ful task completion, we decouple the task into a Commander– Executor formulation. The Commander provides high-level col-laborative strategies that specify how agents should coordinate their behaviors, while the Executor translates these strategies into physically plausible multi-agent interactions with the object. section 3.1 describes how to formulate collaborative strategies by integrating task semantics with scene geometry. section 3.2 introduces the training of a single-agent interaction policy to acquire basic object interaction skills. Building on this policy, section 3.3 further explains how to combine the single-agent policy with the collaborative strategy to achieve successful manipulation of diverse objects. 

3.1 Semantic- and Geometry-Aware Manipulation Strategy 

For collaborative manipulation, an explicit manipulation strategy is essential for reliable task execution. This motivation comes from real-world observations: when multiple agents manipulate an object together, the Commander provides a shared cooperative strategy that informs each agent not only of the final goal but also of how to coordinate with others during execution. If agents act based only on their own local intentions, their behaviors can easily become inconsistent, causing conflicts in motion, unstable object transport, or even complete task failure. Based on this observation, we propose a method that jointly in-tegrates semantic understanding with geometric reasoning. Specif-ically, we construct a Commander to reason over the relationships among agents, objects, and task goals, and to infer a global manipulation strategy for the overall task. This strategy provides structured guidance for the agents, allowing them to coordinate their motions according to both the semantic requirements of the task and the geometric constraints of the scene. 

Scene Graph Construction. We represent the configurations of the scene using scene graphs G = ( V , E ), where nodes denote ob-ject instances and edges encode their spatial relations. Specifically, the node set V = {vi}Ni=1 denotes the objects in the scene. Each node encodes the geometric and identity information of an object, denoted as vi = ( bi, pi, θi, id i), where bi, pi, θi, and id i represent its bounding box, position, orientation, and object identifier, respec-tively. For edge construction, we follow [51] and classify pairwise spatial relationships from object coordinates into ten categories: 

support, embed, inside/place-in, hanging, above/below, near, in-front-of/behind, left/right, between, and aligned .

Strategy Generation. We input the scene graph and task instruc-tion into an LLM [9], leveraging its strong reasoning ability to generate diverse collaborative manipulation strategies. Due to the hallucination problem of LLMs, it is difficult for them to directly generate dense object trajectories. To address this, we decompose the strategy generation task into two steps. We first leverage the LLM for semantic-level planning, decomposing the complete task into multiple subtasks. After that, we employ A ∗ [52] and hybrid A∗ [53] for geometric-level planning, converting the subtasks into concrete and executable goals. Specifically, we require the LLM to deeply understand the scene layout and task information, reason about which objects need to be moved, their destinations, and the manipulation modes based on semantic and geometric cues, and then decompose the full task into multiple subtasks. Each subtask is represented as a text-based tuple e = ( oe, me, pe, re, θe, he),where oe denotes the object to be moved, me is the manipulation mode, pe and re is a textual description of the destination and relay position, θe is the target orientation, and he describes the grasping direction of each character. Since directly asking the LLM to output coordinates is difficult, we instead let it describe positions using other static objects in the scene and then sample the actual coordinates via simulated annealing [54]. While the LLM provides high-level semantic reasoning, our method further incorporates scene geometry to ensure the feasi-bility of the generated subtasks. When executing each subtask, we first sample the contact position ˆpcontact t and the target root position for each character along the grasping direction based on the object’s actual position. We then use A ∗ to plan a path from each character’s current location to the target root position and simplify the resulting path with the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker (RDP) [55] algorithm. After that, we treat the simplified waypoints as the target standing positions ˆpstanding t for the agents, guiding 4

them to navigate toward the object step by step. For obstacle avoidance, we discretize the scene into a navigable map, where cells occupied by objects or outside the accessible region are marked as obstacles. We further inflate obstacle regions according to the character body size to ensure sufficient clearance for the agents during movement. As for the object trajectory, we adopt Hybrid A ∗ for computation. When characters carry an object, the combined entity formed by them can become long and narrow, so simple inflation would lose too much information. Therefore, we model the human-object system as a rectangle and compute the path on the non-inflated map with Hybrid A ∗. We then interpolate the path with specified linear velocity and maximum angular velocity, assign a specific height, and obtain the object trajec-tory {ore f t }nt=0, where ore f t = ( ˆpob j t , ˆθ ob j t ) represents the object’s position and rotation. During the execution of each subtask, we employ a Finite State Machine (FSM) to schedule the phases of approaching, grasping, handover, carrying and releasing, and encode a grasp flag ˆ ct for each character in each phase to indicate whether they should hold the object. These elements together form the complete collaborative manipulation strategy, which guides subsequent character carrying tasks. 

3.2 Single-Agent Manipulation with Sparse Observa-tions 

Multi-agent collaborative object manipulation data are often lim-ited and costly to obtain. Moreover, directly training a policy in the multi-agent setting, especially for asymmetric objects, can easily lead to poor local minima, where the agents fail to coordinate effectively and cannot jointly accomplish the manipulation task. Therefore, we first train a single-agent object manipulation policy to acquire basic object interaction capabilities, providing a more stable foundation for subsequent collaborative manipulation. 

Policy Training with Augmentable Data. The primary goal of the single agent is to control a humanoid to interact with an object and transport it along a specific trajectory. Although several datasets provide human object interactions, such as OMOMO [3], the recorded interactions are either too simple with very short trajectories, or are limited to small objects and do not easily transfer to other object sizes. To avoid being constrained by the limited trajectory diversity in existing datasets, we adopt custom-designed trajectories for policy training. Specifically, we define three basic motion primitives: walking with turns , circular paths ,and spinning in place . By composing these primitives, our training data can cover a diverse set of practical manipulation trajectories. Since the number of base trajectories is limited and their speeds are fixed, we apply random temporal scaling during trajectory sampling to vary the effective speed of the reference object motion. We also randomly rotate the trajectories, which forces the character to learn to interact with the object while moving forward, backward, or sideways. To improve generalization across different object types, we randomize the size and mass of the manipulated objects during training, encouraging the agent to explore diverse interaction strategies for objects with different physical properties. In particu-lar, since existing single-agent object interaction datasets typically contain relatively small objects, we further enlarge the object size while reducing its mass, allowing the agent to successfully manipulate larger objects in the single-agent setting and better transfer to collaborative manipulation of large objects. Contact positions are also randomly assigned within a plausible range, so that the character learns to grasp stably from varying configura-tions. Through these domain randomizations, our policy learns to manipulate diverse objects in a variety of ways. 

States and Actions. The policy takes ot = ( st , gt ) as input, where 

st is the character’s proprioceptive state, consisting of its root pose, joint rotations and velocities, as well as the object’s pose and velocity and gt = ( ˆpstanding t , ˆpcontact t , ˆct:t+l , ore f t:t+l ) represents the interaction strategy. Here ˆpstanding t , ˆpcontact t and ˆ ct:t+l define where, how, and when the character should grasp the object, while 

ore f t:t+l defines how the object should be manipulated. Compared to tracking full-body reference motions, sparse observations provide a more flexible supervision signal for policy learning, allowing the agent to learn task-relevant behaviors without relying on detailed human interaction motion data. In addition, the trajectories provide temporal guidance on how the object should be carried, specifying not only the target location but also the intermediate motion process. This imposes stronger constraints on policy learning and reduces ambiguity in manipulation, which is important for enabling successful multi-agent collaboration in later tasks. At each time step, the policy network predicts target joint angles at ,which are then converted into joint torques by PD controllers to drive the character in simulation.

3.3 Solo-to-Cooperative Learning 

Compared with single-agent object manipulation, multi-agent ob-ject manipulation requires each agent not only to interact with the object, but also to coordinate its actions in response to the dynamic behaviors of other agents. Such interactions can take various forms, with cooperative carrying and handover being two common scenarios. These collaborative settings introduce several additional challenges:  

> •

agents may collide with each other in the absence of proper planning or mutual awareness;  

> •

agents may fail to synchronize their actions without unified coordination;  

> •

smooth handover requires clear transfer intent and interac-tion cues. To this end, our designs in strategy generation (section 3.1) and single-agent manipulation (section 3.2) can be readily trans-ferred to the multi-agent setting to address these challenges. To avoid inter-agent conflicts, we assign each agent a target standing position ˆpstanding t and contact position ˆpt contact , encouraging the agents to approach and grasp distinct regions of the object. For coordination, both agents follow a shared object reference trajec-tory ore f t:t+l , which provides a unified target for synchronizing their manipulation behaviors. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

In this section, we quantitatively evaluate the capability of the low-level policy when two agents collaboratively transport an object, as well as the overall algorithm’s performance in terms of planning and transportation within complex scenarios. Since no baseline method supports handover, a fair quantitative comparison is not feasible, and we therefore present qualitative results in the sup-plementary video for handover task. We employ PPO [56] as the reinforcement learning algorithm for low-level policy training and DeepSeek-V4-Pro [57] as the high-level planner. Detailed training configurations are provided in the supplementary materials. 

4.1 Datasets and Metrics 

Datasets. Due to the scarcity of large-scale multi-person collab-orative object interaction data, our training minimizes reliance on such data. We use single-person motion-only datasets such as AMASS [58] to train the adversarial motion prior, providing diverse human motions for better generalization. For evaluation, we use Core4D [10], extracting object meshes and trajectories while filtering out those with abnormal velocities or accelerations to construct a reliable benchmark. Since the remaining trajectories are relatively simple, we additionally generated 200 Bezier curves, with 50 curves for each setting of 2 to 5 control points sampled within a 10 ×10 m area. For each generated trajectory, the speed was randomly sampled from 0.8 to 1.3 m/s, and the trajectory was randomly rotated to allow the object to move in various directions. For experiments in complex scenarios, we construct two spatial layouts: a simple two-bedroom one-living-room apartment (2B1L) and a more complex L-shaped configuration. These layouts are designed to evaluate the algorithm’s geometric planning and policy control capabilities in complex environments. Each layout is divided into four regions: one starting zone and three target zones. Three objects are placed in the starting zone, each corresponding to one of the target zones. For each layout, we design one precise instruction and one vague instruction to assess the algorithm’s semantic understanding ability. 

Metrics. For low-level carrying tasks, we report Success Rate (SR.), Distance Error (DE), Orientation Error (OE), Distance Tracking Error (DTE), and Orientation Tracking Error (OTE). A trial is considered successful if the final xy -plane distance error is below 0.3 m and the final orientation error is below 30 ◦.DE and OE measure the final xy -plane distance error in meters and orientation error in radians, respectively, while DTE and OTE measure their average per-frame tracking errors along the reference trajectory. For human-object interaction, following [1], we compute Contact Accuracy (Contact Acc.) to evaluate whether hand-object contacts are correctly generated. For the experiments in complex scenarios, we evaluate Semantic Success Rate (SSR.) and Carrying Success Rate (CSR.) over the three objects. A trial is considered semantically successful if the LLM correctly plans the placement of each object into its designated target zone and sample a reachable position within that zone. Carrying success is defined as successfully moving an object to the location designed by the high-level plan. 

4.2 Comparison to State-of-the-Art Methods 

To comprehensively evaluate our method on physical control, we compare it with state-of-the-art physics-based methods. We consider TeamHOI [8], CooHOI [7], and TokenHSI [4] as repre-sentative baselines for multi-agent and human-object interaction. TeamHOI and CooHOI are recent physics-based frameworks for cooperative motion generation. Since Token-HSI is originally designed for single-agent human-object interaction, we adapt it to our setting by allowing two agents to interact with the same object. table 1 presents the comparison between our method and ex-isting physics-based methods. We evaluate on a variety of objects from Core4D dataset with different shapes and sizes to demon-strate the adaptability, while also randomizing object masses in the range of 10 kg to 25 kg. We use the original trajectories from Core4D and additionally generate object trajectories using Bezier curves to further evaluate the generalization ability of our method. The results show that our method outperforms prior works in both Success Rate and Tracking Error. As illustrate in fig. 4, TokenHSI [4], originally designed for single-agent interaction, struggles to coordinate two agents when adapted to human-human-object interaction. CooHOI [7] supports dual agents but relies on sparse position as target, which makes precise trajectory tracking difficult to achieve. TeamHOI [8] generates cooperative behaviors, but its fixed grasping strategy limits generalization across objects. In contrast, our method assigns role-specific grasping locations through the planned coordination strategy, enabling robust adapta-tion to diverse object shapes and sizes. In addition, fig. 3 demonstrates the versatility of our method across different collaborative tasks. Our method can control a single agent to manipulate an object, coordinate two agents for joint carrying and relay-style handover, and further scale to multi-agent collaboration by composing multiple instances of the same shared policy. This compositional design allows more complex cooperative behaviors, such as group-based relay manipulation, without introducing task-specific policy architectures.

4.4 Solo-to-Cooperative Learning 

We ablate the training strategy of the manipulation policy. As shown in fig. 5, directly training the policy on the multi-agent cooperative task without single-agent pretraining leads to severe overfitting. In this case, one agent may quickly learn to complete its assigned motion, while the other consistently fails, preventing the agents from accomplishing the cooperative task together. Conversely, when the policy is trained only on single-agent object interaction and is not fine-tuned on the multi-agent cooperative task, it fails to acquire cooperative behaviors. Since the policy has not learned to adjust its own state according to the object state and the partner’s behavior, it cannot coordinate with the other agent during object manipulation. The quantitative results in table 3 further confirm this observation: removing either single-agent pretraining or multi-agent fine-tuning causes the success rate to drop to zero, demonstrating that both stages are essential for robust cooperative manipulation. 4.5 Collaborative Strategy from Commander 

For cooperative manipulation, it is crucial for all agents to un-derstand not only the final task objective but also their roles in achieving it. Therefore, a commander is needed to provide an explicit collaborative strategy. In our framework, we use object trajectories as this strategy, which guides the agents toward co-ordinated object transport. In table 3, we replace the trajectory guidance with only the target position. Under this setting, each agent only knows where the object should be transported, but does not receive information on how to cooperate with the other agent during manipulation. The results show that the task success rate drops substantially, demonstrating that explicit trajectory-based strategy from the commander is essential for effective multi-agent collaboration. 

4.6 Synchronized Grasping via Contact Signal 

Whether the agent can maintain stable contact with the object is crucial for carrying tasks. In this framework, we explicitly synchronize the timing of contact and release between two agents via contact signals. In table 3, we remove the explicit synchro-nization provided by the contact signals. Under this setting, the success rate drops significantly while other metrics show no major differences, indicating that contact signals effectively help agents achieve stable grasping. 

4.7 Generalization through Shape Variation 

Generalization to diverse objects is essential in practice. To im-prove the policy’s generalization capability, we randomize the shape of objects during training, thereby enhancing robustness across different objects. In table 3, we ablate the generalization to object shape. Under this setting, the policy struggles to interact with unseen objects, resulting in a sharp drop in success rate. This indicates that generalizing over object shapes during training is crucial for improving robustness. 

5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

In this paper, we present a scalable Commander-Executor frame-work for long-horizon collaborative object manipulation. The Commander jointly reasons about task semantics and scene ge-ometry to generate feasible coordination strategies, while the Ex-ecutor learns a physics-based policy conditioned on strategy cues to produce coordinated humanoid-object interactions. To reduce reliance on scarce multi-agent interaction data, we introduce a solo-to-coop-erative learning scheme that trains a single-agent policy with augmented objects, contacts, and trajectories, and transfers it to multi-agent collaboration through a shared policy. 9

Fig. 6: Our method enables diverse object manipulation in complex scenes by combining task- and geometry-aware strategy planning with physics-based policy execution. Given different scene layouts and object goals, it can generate feasible coordination strategies and control multiple humanoids to perform long-horizon interactions, including cooperative carrying, relay-style handover, and group-based manipulation across objects with different shapes and sizes. Experiments show that our method supports diverse collaboration types, generalizes across object categories, and coordinates multi-ple agents in complex long-horizon tasks. Although the proposed method enables scalable collaborative object manipulation, it still has several limitations. For the low-level policy, the use of simplified spherical hand representations restricts dexterous manipulation, making it difficult to handle slender objects such as baseball bats or ladders. It also falls short when it comes to executing highly skill-demanding maneuvers. In addition, during collaborative carrying task, if the object’s mass and size are sufficiently small, it may end up being controlled by just one agent. This happens because our policy does not distinguish between single-agent and dual-agent carrying in its input. However, as the mass and size gradually increase, this issue completely disappears. For the high-level commander, while our Commander considers scene geometry for strategy planning, the current physics-based execution mainly focuses on humanoid-object interaction and does not fully model rich physical inter-actions with complex environments, such as stairs, slopes, or deformable obstacles. Future work will explore more detailed hand-object contact models and tighter integration between scene-aware planning and environment-aware control.
