# ABSTRACT 

How can human researchers use artificial intelligence (AI) to make progress on difficult mechanics problems while retaining control of physical significance, mathematical rigor, and validation? We explore this opportunity through a quantitative stability problem rooted in the Eshelby conjecture. For a uniform dilatational eigenstrain in a homogeneous isotropic solid, we establish a nonlinear two-sided relation between whole-interior strain heterogeneity and a geometry-only distance from the ellipsoid family, with half-order Sobolev scaling that captures morphological length scale. We then develop a complementary finite-shape theory: ellipsoidal subtraction yields an exact residual-field budget from which systematically convergent upper and lower bounds are obtained for a prescribed geometry. Applied to protruded, indented, and necked inclusions, the bounds agree with independent displacement-based finite-element calculations. The study also documents a human-led, AI-assisted research process in which researchers select consequential problems, shape targets, audit arguments, require independent checks, and interpret mechanics, while AI broadens analytical exploration and supports computation. This experience also led us to develop Open Problems in Mechanics ,a versioned community-facing map that makes selected mechanics questions, their known/open boundaries, and criteria for progress explicit.

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) for scientific discovery, including mechanics, formal mathematical reasoning, and large-scale mathematical exploration, are expanding its role in research [1–5]. For mechanics, this raises a timely question: how can researchers use increasingly capable AI to make progress on difficult theoretical problems while retaining control of physical significance, mathematical rigor, validation, and scientific understanding? Mechanics is a demanding setting because important problems often span general theory, quantitative computation, physical interpretation, and engineering use. Progress at one layer does not replace the others; increased AI capability likewise does not by itself ensure scientific understanding or verification [4, 6]. The Eshelby inclusion problem provides a particularly suitable test. An ellipsoidal region carrying a uniform eigenstrain in an infinite homogeneous elastic solid has a spatially uniform constrained strain (Fig. 1A) [7], a result foundational to micromechanics [8, 9]. It also motivated the Eshelby conjecture: to what extent does a uniform internal field characterize the ellipsoid? Rigidity results have resolved the weak conjecture in important settings and substantial parts of the strong conjecture in three dimensions [10–13]. Perturbation analyses have separately characterized infinitesimal uniformity-preserving directions and leading-order elastostatic changes under small interface perturbations [14–16]. The rigidity question is exact: when the field is uniform, what shapes are possible? arXiv:2610.13400v1 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci] 9 Oct 2026 Quantitative Eshelby stability B. Ni Real microstructures pose a quantitative counterpart. Precipitates and transforming regions can develop faceting, irregularity, coalescence, and interconnected morphologies as their size, coherency, interfacial energy, and elastic interactions evolve [17–20]. Best-fit ellipsoids are useful in practice and can reproduce volume-averaged elastic quantities surprisingly well even for irregular inclusions, but the underlying nonellipsoidal geometry produces a heterogeneous internal field [19, 21]. If such a morphology is represented by an ellipsoid, how much internal strain heterogeneity is neglected? Conversely, if the field is nearly uniform, how strongly is the nonellipsoidal shape constrained? The problem is not captured by a single geometric tolerance. Changes within the ellipsoid family preserve uniformity, equal-height features at different wavelengths need not have equal effects, and infinitesimal sensitivity alone does not control a finite morphology. Nonellipsoidal inclusion mechanics has consequently been approached through exact or semi-analytical solutions for selected geometries and computational or averaged descriptions for more general shapes [22–24]. These difficulties also expose the complementary roles of theory and computation. A theorem can control all admissible modes and reveal which geometric information is fundamentally required, something finite numerical sampling cannot establish. Shape-specific analysis can then turn that structure into quantitative predictions, while an independent elasticity calculation tests them through a different formulation. Engineering interpretation determines whether the resulting strain or stress variation matters for the approximation being used. Here we develop these layers in one mechanics problem and use the research trajectory as a practical exploration of human-led, AI-assisted theoretical mechanics, not as a final prescription for scientific research. Human direction selected the problem, assumptions, targets, validation standards, and engineering interpretation; AI assistance broadened analytical search, computation, and checking (Fig. 1B). We first establish a nonlinear two-sided stability relation near the ellipsoid family and identify the half-order boundary scale required for all-mode control (Fig. 1C). We then derive a complementary finite-shape stability theory with systematically convergent bounds and test it on protruded, indented, and necked inclusions using independent finite-element calculations (Fig. 1D). # 3 Discussion 

3.1 Two notions of Eshelby stability 

The two theoretical results address different notions of stability. Section 2.1 is local but universal: after exact ellipsoidal changes are removed, it controls every admissible nonellipsoidal mode in a neighborhood of any fixed nondegenerate ellipsoid. Here local refers to proximity in shape space, not to spatial localization of the perturbation. This distinction is essential: outside such a neighborhood, near-uniform interior fields can occur for shapes that remain far from the ellipse family in related two-dimensional settings, so near-uniformity alone does not support a global geometric conclusion [26]. Its half-order metric identifies the geometric information required for two-sided all-mode control, something that cannot be established by accumulating simulations of individual shapes. Section 2.3 is shape-specific but finite-amplitude. For a prescribed geometry, the exact elastic reduction and residual-field budget give upper and lower bounds that converge to the same response as interior and exterior field information is resolved. It therefore trades the universality of the local theorem for systematically improvable accuracy on the geometry of interest. For shapes approaching an ellipsoid, the finite-shape response is consistent with the local asymptotic theory, but neither result replaces the other. The applications show why both viewpoints are useful. Cases B and C have the same maximum height/depth and footprint but different heterogeneity, while the necked shape D preserves the reference volume and produces the largest response. Volume change, maximum height, or fitted aspect ratio therefore cannot by themselves quantify loss of uniformity. The local theory identifies morphological scale as essential; the finite-shape theory uses the complete prescribed geometry to obtain a quantitative elastic statement. Together, these results connect Eshelby’s exact uniform-field property to both rigorous class-wide control and engineering-level prediction. The distinction also illustrates a broader feature of mechanics: general theory, shape-specific computation, and independent computational comparison answer different questions, and their value is greatest when they are designed to reinforce rather than substitute for one another. 

3.2 Human-led, AI-assisted theoretical mechanics 

Recent agentic systems in mechanics and materials have combined specialized language-model agents with knowledge retrieval, coding, physics-based simulation, execution feedback, critique, and adaptive team organization across mechanics problem solving and materials-design workflows [2, 27–30]. The present study explored a human-led, AI-assisted research structure in which scientific decision authority remained with the researchers, while AI enlarged the space of candidate reasoning (Fig. 1B). Human researchers selected the mechanics problem, observables, assumptions, acceptance criteria, and validation requirements, and retained respon-sibility for interpretation and final claims. AI supported literature and cross-domain search, alternative formulations and derivations, symbolic and numerical checks, coding, partial formalization, and parallel exploration. AI-generated results were treated as candidates until reviewed through complementary checks designed to expose different failure modes (Table 1); the research process and AI review protocol are summarized in Methods Section 5.5, with supporting details in SI Section S6. Several episodes show why this structure mattered. Measuring shape error from one reference ellipsoid was rejected because another exact ellipsoid can differ geometrically while retaining uniformity; the target therefore became distance from the ellipsoid family. Sharp linearized gains were not promoted directly to finite-amplitude bounds when finite-shape calculations exposed nonlinear corrections. Agreement between two implementations sharing the same potential representation was treated as a consistency check rather than independent evidence, motivating the displacement-based FEM test. These cases illustrate that AI was most useful for broadening and sustaining reasoning, while human judgment determined which question mattered, which result was insufficient, and what evidence was required before acceptance. This separation between generative breadth and scientific acceptance is important more generally: greater AI-enabled productivity does not by itself guarantee scientific understanding, while effective use in materials research still depends on domain grounding, hypothesis testing, and explicit evaluation aligned with scientific goals [6, 31, 32]. This research configuration is one explored case rather than a fixed prescription; the next section considers how such effort might be directed toward a broader set of mechanics problems.

# 4 Conclusions 

Eshelby’s classical result shows how a special geometry can reduce a nontrivial elastic field problem to a uniform interior response. Here we asked what remains when that geometry is imperfect. Near the ellipsoid family, we establish a quantitative two-sided relation between nonellipsoidal shape variation and interior strain heterogeneity, with a half-order geometry scale that makes morphological length scale part of the mechanics. The spherical spectrum makes this dependence explicit, while the finite-shape theory carries the same question to prescribed nonellipsoidal morphologies. Together, these results move Eshelby theory from exactness to robustness. That robustness also acquires a practical meaning. By separating the ellipsoidal uniform-field contribution from the residual geometry, the finite-shape formulation gives tightening bounds on the heterogeneity generated by a known morphology, consistent with independent displacement-based FEM. The relevant engineering question is therefore not only whether a shape is close to an ellipsoid, but how much strain or stress variation an ellipsoidal approximation neglects. The present results also point to a broader future program: testing quantitative Eshelby stability for general eigenstrain modes, elastic mismatch, and anisotropy, where the equivalent-inclusion and uniformity structures already become substantially richer [41, 42]; for interfaces and interacting inclusions, where the geometries supporting uniform internal fields can change qualitatively [43, 44]; and for larger finite departures from ellipsoidal geometry. Extensions to finite-deformation and nonlinear inclusion mechanics provide a further direction [45]. The study further provides one documented practice of human-led, AI-assisted theoretical mechanics. AI broadened analytical and computational exploration, while human researchers retained authority over the physical question, acceptance criteria, independent evidence, and interpretation. To extend this practice beyond the present case, we developed Open Problems in Mechanics as a public, versioned map of selected theory-rich questions with their physical motivation, established results, known/open boundaries, and criteria for progress made explicit. The aim is not to define a closed list of grand challenges, but to provide a shared resource that can be corrected, sharpened, and advanced by the mechanics community. As AI expands the scale of available reasoning, making consequential questions and evidentiary standards explicit may become increasingly important for turning that capacity into mechanics advances that remain physically meaningful, checkable, and transferable.
