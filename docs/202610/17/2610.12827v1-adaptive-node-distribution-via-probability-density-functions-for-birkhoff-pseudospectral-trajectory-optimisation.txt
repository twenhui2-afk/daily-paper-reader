# Adaptive Node Distribution via Probability Density Functions for Birkhoff Pseudospectral Trajectory Optimisation 

Andrian A. Yasintus ( B), and Xiaofeng Wu 

The University of Sydney, Sydney, NSW 2006, Australia 

B andrian.yasintus@sydney.edu.au 

Abstract 

Pseudospectral methods are a powerful framework for spacecraft trajectory optimisation that rely on fixed, problem-independent collocation nodes such as Legendre-Gauss-Lobatto (LGL) nodes. These nodes are located according to polynomial root distributions, which can result in insufficient resolution in regions of high dynamical activity, such as those encountered during collision avoidance manoeuvres. This paper presents an adaptive node distribution strategy for Birkhoff pseudospectral trajectory optimisation, where the collocation nodes are placed according to a probability density function derived from the thrust magnitude profile of the spacecraft. By concentrating nodes in regions of high dynamical variation, the proposed method simultaneously achieves feasibility preservation and improved convergence with fewer nodes in a collision avoidance case study when compared to the universal Birkhoff method, with Model Predictive Control serving as an independent validation comparator. The case study highlights the practical consequences of inter-node constraint violations and validates the adaptive node scheme as a robust and efficient alternative for fuel-optimal spacecraft manoeuvre design.

Trajectory Optimisation · Pseudospectral Methods · Optimal Control · Spacecraft Guidance 1arXiv:2610.12827v1 [math.OC] 9 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Spacecraft collision avoidance manoeuvres are one of the most critical operational challenges in trajec-tory optimisation. The growing population of orbital debris and active satellites in low Earth orbit has significantly increased the frequency of conjunctions, posing a growing threat to operational spacecraft. Therefore, the fuel cost and mission disruption associated with these manoeuvres motivates the devel-opment of efficient, reliable, and robust trajectory optimisation methods capable of producing feasible solutions under tight operational and computational constraints. A broad range of methods have been de-veloped to address this challenge, spanning impulsive and continuous-thrust formulations, deterministic and chance-constrained approaches, and single- and multi-encounter scenarios [1]. Collision avoidance manoeuvres, and spacecraft path planning more broadly, are naturally posed as continuous-time optimal control problems, with the objective of minimising fuel consumption while satisfying dynamic constraints, control bounds, and a spatial keep-out constraint centered on the debris object. Two broad classes of solution method have been employed: indirect methods, which rely on satis-fying equations derived from Pontryagin’s Minimum Principle such as primer vector theory [2], or direct methods, which transcribe the continuous optimal control problem into a finite-dimensional nonlinear programming (NLP) problem to be solved by a numerical optimiser [3]. Pseudospectral methods are a distinguished class of direct transcription that approximates states and controls using global orthogo-nal polynomials evaluated at predetermined quadrature nodes [4]. Unlike conventional direct methods, pseudospectral methods maintain a connection to necessary conditions through the Covector Mapping Principle (CMP) [5], providing theoretical grounding for the optimality of the transcribed problem and enabling the recovery of states, controls, and costate variables with spectral accuracy [6], allowing independent verification of optimality through theories like primer vector theory. Pseudospectral methods have also been continuously improved for higher accuracy and lower compu-tational effort. For example, hp -adaptive methods refine the solution by iteratively partitioning the time domain into subintervals, and adaptively increasing either the number of subintervals ( h-refinement) or the polynomial degree within each subinterval ( p-refinement) based on local error estimates, as imple-mented in solvers such as GPOPS-II [7, 8]. This error-estimation approach remains an active area of development, with recent work refining the mesh adaptation using explicit simulation based error metrics [9]. However, while effective, the hp approach is inherently reactive as nodes are redistributed in response to observed solution error rather than informed by prior knowledge of the solution structure. 2The Birkhoff pseudospectral method is a distinct formulation grounded in Birkhoff interpolation theory [10] which permits interpolation over nodes where only non-consecutive derivative information is available. When applied to Gauss-type quadrature nodes, this gives a set of well-conditioned polynomial basis functions from which the pseudospectral integration matrix (PSIM) can be derived [11]. The PSIM is the discrete inverse of the pseudospectral differentiation matrix (PSDM), whose ill-conditioning is a well-known limitation of traditional Lagrangian pseudospectral methods [12]. This foundation was developed into a complete pseudospectral framework for optimal control, with full compatibility with the CMP while also showing that the Birkhoff formulation supports arbitrary node distributions while retaining the theoretical properties of classical pseudospectral methods including spectral accuracy and costate recovery [13, 14]. The framework has since found many applications, such as two-stage ascent optimisation [15]. Despite these favourable properties, to the authors’ knowledge, the Birkhoff pseudospectral method has not previously been applied with problem-informed adaptive node placement. In collision avoidance problems, dynamic activity is concentrated near the avoidance manoeuvre interval, yet standard node distributions are unable to adapt for this structure. This paper proposes an adaptive Birkhoff pseudospectral method in which collocation nodes are placed according to a probability density function (PDF) derived from the problem structure, with concentrated resolution in regions of high dynamic activity and requires no iterative mesh refinement or error estimation. By exploiting Birkhoff support for arbitrary nodes, the proposed method aims to produce feasible collision avoidance trajectories that satisfy constraints at all points throughout the manoeuvre (not merely at collocation points), addressing a limitation that remains an active concern even in recent constraint-handling work for direct collocation methods [16]. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 presents the methodology, including the universal Birkhoff pseudospectral formulation, the proposed adaptive node placement transformation, and the resulting changes to the dynamics constraints and cost functional under the new mapping. Section 3 presents the results and discussion, including the collision avoidance simulation outcomes, the limitations of refining the collocation scheme, and comparing the adaptive method against a Model Predictive Control (MPC) baseline. Finally, the conclusions are presented in Section 4. 32 Methodology

2.4 Collision Avoidance Manoeuvre Pipeline 

With the adaptive node placement algorithm, we are now in a position to design an autonomous collision avoidance system. We denote x the spacecraft state, xt the target state, and xd a debris state, from which the spacecraft must avoid on its way to the target. We also define ρdet as the detection range of the satellite, and ρKO as the keep-out range where no debris are allowed to enter. The aim of this algorithm is to determine a nominal path so that x will rendezvous with xt, and replan accordingly if it detects and determines that xd will enter the keep-out sphere. We will denote the nominal path calculation as Phase 1, and the replanning calculation as Phase 2. However, notice that if a debris object is detected, there is not yet enough information to determine where to cluster the nodes. Let xdet 

be the position of the spacecraft when it detects the debris. 3.2 Limitations of LGL Node Refinement 

A natural follow-up question is whether the constraint violation in Phase 2a can be resolved simply by increasing the number of collocation nodes, rather than through adaptive placement, in order to reduce the inter-node gap and hence the inter-node constraint violations. Indeed, [14] highlighted how the universal formulation could handle 700 grid points for a challenging low-thrust orbital transfer problem. Figure 5 14 shows the minimum separation distance as a function of node count N for the flat-PDF replanning algorithm.    

> Fig. 5 Left: Separation history as a function of the number of nodes N. Right: zoomed-in version near the keep-out region.

As Figure 5 shows, at N = 80 the keep-out constraint performance has improved but still does not reach ρKO = 300 km, and at N = 90 , the solution fails to converge within the 2000 iteration limit, producing a solution that meets the constraints, but is not optimised - at N = 90 , the fuel consumption was ∆v = 42 .00 km/s. Even when the maximum number of NLP iterations was increased to 10,000, an optimised result was not able to be reached, suggesting that the corresponding NLP becomes increasingly difficult to solve numerically as N increases. Furthermore, warm-starting from the N = 80 solution also did not help, and instead resulted in a worse fuel consumption of ∆v = 50 .77 km/s. To investigate exactly where the breakdown occurred, a finer sweep of N was run for N = 81 to 85 .This is shown in Figure 6. Figure 6 highlights a clear transition in solver behaviour at N = 81 , which is the first node count to show this unstable behaviour. However, N = 81 is also the first node size for which the keep-out constraint is actually satisfied by the universal Birkhoff method. This suggests that the observed deterioration in convergence is strongly related to a change in the optimisation problem as sufficient discretisation 15 Fig. 6 Left: Separation history with finer resolution near N = 80 . Right: zoomed-in version near the keep-out region. 

resolution is reached to satisfy the keep-out constraint. This behaviour was also seen at N = 82 , but surprisingly not at N = 83 or N = 84 , which showed separation behaviour similar to the original, nominal path. This highlights a crucial fact about the solver behaviour under the universal Birkhoff formulation - the solver instability is not a result of larger N , but of another factor. Several potential causes for this irregular behaviour were investigated. First, the condition numbers of the Birkhoff matrix [I, −B] returned a clean O(1) curve in perfect alignment with [13], ruling out increasing ill-conditioning of the Birkhoff discretisation matrix as the cause. Next, the full equality-constraint Jacobian Jc, which includes both the Birkhoff collocation defects and the non-linear dynamics, was evaluated at two points via singular value decomposition at the initial guess and the converged (or best-so-far) solution for each N , to obtain its condition number κ(Jc). To test for a linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) breakdown, the smallest singular value of the Jacobian rows for constraints near the 300 km bound, σmin (Jactive ), was also computed. Both quantities were insensitive to N , with 

κ(Jc) remaining within a narrow band with no monotonic trend, and σmin (Jactive ) being constant at 600 for all tested N values. This rules out both intrinsic conditioning of the discretised NLP and degradation in the independence of the active keep-out constraint gradients as the cause of the N = 90 failure. This points to one remaining factor, which is that the misbehaviour must be occurring at some point 16 in the middle of the solve. The issue is that for N = 81 , the scaled dual infeasibility measure ( inf_du in IPOPT) fails to decrease towards the solver tolerance even after 10,000 iterations, remaining at magnitudes of 10 6 to 10 13 . This indicates that despite meeting the constraints, the solution is nowhere near a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) local optimum since the first-order optimality condition is being violated by orders of magnitude. IPOPT can address this using a restoration phase, where the solver temporarily abandons the primal-dual subproblem and instead minimises constraint violation to find a new iteration closer to feasibility that the regular optimisation can resume from. In this run, the restorations were triggered and successfully re-established feasibility, but the subsequent iterations drove inf_du back to large values. Therefore, we investigate whether the solver behaviour is related to the placement of the collocation nodes relative to the point of closest approach. 3.2.2 Implications for Adaptive Node Placement 

The adaptive method, in contrast to the universal Birkhoff results, achieves a locally optimal solution much faster than the constraint-violating N = 80 flat-PDF replan, and with a lower node count at N = 50 .Additionally, its violation of 0.70 km is an improvement on the 2.72 km violation generated by the universal formulation with N = 80 - this can be attributed to the closer spacing of the adaptive nodes, which reduces the temporal interval over which violations from the constraint boundary can occur. Therefore, the results of the adaptive method highlight an important limitation of the universal formulation for problems with localised constraint activity - increasing the number of nodes does not necessarily improve continuous-time feasibility, since the redistribution of nodes can produce unfavourable placements relative to the critical region while still leaving substantial inter-node gaps. The adaptive discretisation instead focuses the resolution in the region of greatest trajectory activity, which substantially reduces the inter-node violation at lower node counts. However, this clustering does not inherently eliminate the numerical difficulties associated with nodes located very close to the point of closest approach - indeed, increasing the adaptive node count can reproduce the same irregular behaviour (for example, using N = 80 nodes with the adaptive method also produces irregular behaviour). This creates competing effects, where clustering the nodes gives smaller inter-node gaps, but also potentially smaller sep (N ) as the nodes get closer to tcritical which introduces the numerical difficulties described previously. Therefore, although the adaptive method cannot guarantee solver stability for arbitrarily large node counts, its principal advantage is that it can achieve substantially improved continuous-time constraint satisfaction at a lower node count and computational cost. In this sense, the adaptive method provides a more efficient discretisation of the trajectory, allowing localised constraint activity to be resolved before the numerical difficulties associated with excessive proximity to 

tcritical become dominant. Another practical consequence of increasing nodes is wall time which increases nonlinearly with N ,from 6.8 seconds at N = 50 to 113.3 seconds at N = 80 , as shown in Figure 7. In comparison, the adaptive method with N = 50 nodes was solved in 18.8 seconds, which far outperforms

Regarding fuel consumption, because MPC uses a prediction horizon, its visibility into the future is restricted, and its control authority over treplan accordingly yields a less globally efficient solution than the adaptive node formulation, which has full-horizon knowledge by construction. Therefore, the ∆v gap is attributable primarily to finite-horizon effects (ie. horizon myopia), rather than any limitation of the MPC implementation. 

# 4 Conclusion 

This paper presented an adaptive node distribution strategy for Birkhoff pseudospectral trajectory opti-misation, in which collocation nodes are placed according to a probability density function derived from the thrust magnitude profile of the nominal solution. This was applied to a debris collision avoidance case study, where it was found that the proposed method was shown to produce a genuinely feasible avoidance trajectory, reducing the inter-node keep-out violation from 16 .6% from the standard LGL collocation to a comparatively negligible 0.23% , demonstrating the benefit of concentrating collocation resolution in regions of significant trajectory activity rather than relying only on uniform refinement of the LGL node set. The results also revealed an important limitation of LGL node refinement, where increasing the number of collocation nodes did not consistently improve continuous-time feasibility. Instead, beyond a critical node count the changes in node placement from a higher N could lead to irregular NLP solver behaviour. The adaptive formulation is also not immune to this behaviour with higher node counts also displaying irregular behaviour, but the principle advantage of the adaptive framework is in achieving substantially greater resolution of localised constraint activity with fewer nodes, which in turn reduces continuous-time constraint violations before encountering the irregular behaviour from these numerical difficulties. Finally, when compared to a Model Predictive Control baseline, the adaptive method was able to achieve similar trajectories with a 92 .7% reduction in wall time, confirming that full-horizon pseudospectral opti-24 misation outperforms reactive planning for this class of problem. These results demonstrate the potential of full-horizon pseudospectral optimisation to provide more efficient trajectory solutions than reactive planning for this class of debris collision avoidance problem, while also highlighting the importance of node placement and discretisation strategy when enforcing localised constraints. The proposed framework is not limited to collision avoidance, and future work may involve applying this adaptive node method to a variety of other contexts. Because the adaptive node transformation is independent of the problem dynamics, it is directly applicable to any trajectory optimisation scenario where dynamic activity is localised in time, such as atmospheric entry and hypersonic re-entry, powered descent and landing, and low-thrust orbit raising. In each case, the key advantage of the Birkhoff formulation is that arbitrary node distributions are supported without sacrificing spectral accuracy or costate recovery, making the adaptive method a natural foundation for these extensions. In this way, the proposed method offers a computationally efficient alternative to iterative mesh refinement across a broad class of trajectory optimisation problems.
