## Abstract 

Minimax optimization is fundamental to adversar-ial learning yet suffers from severe stability chal-lenges, often manifesting as persistent limit cycles or divergence. This paper investigates these in-stabilities through the lens of continuous-time dy-namical systems. By modeling training dynamics as a second-order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), we rigorously identify the root cause of non-convergence: the problematic eigenstructure of the loss function’s Hessian near equilibrium points. To address this, we introduce Controlled-Minimax, a novel stabilization framework that reformulates the optimization trajectory as a con-trollable physical system. Unlike heuristic regular-ization techniques, our approach derives a specific feedback controller that provides theoretical guar-antees of local exponential stability, regardless of the underlying Hessian spectrum. Consequently, our method stabilizes general minimax problems where traditional gradient descent-ascent meth-ods fail. Extensive experiments on pathological benchmarks including the Dirac-GAN, 2D Gaus-sian mixtures, and Swiss Roll datasets, as well as high-resolution image synthesis with StyleGAN2-ada, demonstrate that Controlled-Minimax effec-tively suppresses oscillatory behavior while ensur-ing fast and robust convergence. 

## 1. Introduction 

Minimax optimization is a cornerstone of modern machine learning, transcending traditional single-objective minimiza-tion tasks to address problems characterized by competing interests. This paradigm frames learning as a competition between two agents: a minimizing player controlling pa-rameters θ and a maximizing player controlling ϕ, cou-pled through a shared objective function L(θ, ϕ ). The goal is to find a saddle point (θ∗, ϕ ∗) that solves the problem

This formulation has proven to be in-credibly powerful and versatile, underpinning the success of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) in synthesiz-ing high-fidelity data (Goodfellow et al., 2020), enhanc-ing model robustness through adversarial training (Madry et al., 2017), and enabling complex decision-making in multi-agent reinforcement learning (Busoniu et al., 2008). However, efficiently solving these minimax problems in the context of deep learning remains a formidable chal-lenge. Unlike classical convex-concave settings, the opti-mization landscapes parameterized by deep neural networks are highly non-convex and non-concave, leading to com-plex local geometries. Standard approaches, most notably Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA) (Bottou, 2010), often fail to converge to a stable Nash equilibrium in such regimes. Instead of simply descending a potential surface, the opti-mization dynamics frequently exhibit rotational behaviors caused by the interaction between the two players. Con-sequently, training trajectories are prone to persistent os-cillations, limit cycles, or even divergence, which severely hampers the stability and generalization capability of the resulting models. To address the inherent instability of minimax optimization, existing literature has predominantly explored two direc-tions: refining the optimization dynamics and imposing structural regularizations. The former category attempts to correct the rotational behavior of GDA by incorporating lookahead mechanisms (Korpelevich, 1976; Mertikopoulos et al., 2018; Gidel et al., 2019), multi-step updates (Good-fellow et al., 2020), or timescale separation between the players (Heusel et al., 2017). The latter seeks to smooth the optimization landscape by enforcing Lipschitz constraints or adding gradient penalties (Gulrajani et al., 2017; Mescheder et al., 2018; Miyato et al., 2018). Nevertheless, rigorous theoretical guarantees for these methods remain elusive, par-ticularly in the non-convex non-concave settings typical of neural networks in deep learning. While local convergence can be proven under strict as-sumptions—such as local convexity or linearity (Nagara-jan & Kolter, 2017; Heusel et al., 2017), global conver-gence properties are often neither guaranteed nor understood (Mescheder et al., 2017; Daskalakis & Panageas, 2018; Far-nia & Ozdaglar, 2020). Furthermore, regularization-based 1arXiv:2610.12915v1 [cs.LG] 9 Oct 2026 Controlled-Minimax: Stabilizing Adversarial Optimization via Control Theory 

methods tend to introduce inductive biases that hamper model expressivity (Mescheder et al., 2018; Arjovsky et al., 2017), while sophisticated dynamic adjustments frequently incur high computational overheads without ensuring sta-bility in general cases. Consequently, developing a solver that is both theoretically grounded and practically robust remains an open challenge. In this paper, we depart from the traditional view of de-signing better discrete optimizers and instead approach the instability problem through the lens of continuous-time con-trol theory. We model the minimax training dynamics as a second-order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), allow-ing us to rigorously diagnose the root cause of persistent oscillations: the specific eigenstructure of the loss func-tion’s Hessian, which inherently precludes convergence in standard settings. Based on this insight, we propose 

Controlled-Minimax , a novel framework that treats the optimization trajectory as a controllable dynamical system. Unlike heuristic regularizers that passively penalize model weights, our method actively injects a designed feedback controller into the dynamics. This controller forces the system’s energy to dissipate, providing a theoretical guar-antee of local exponential stability regardless of the under-lying Hessian structure. We validate our approach on tasks ranging from the pathological Dirac-GAN, 2D Gaussian and Swiss Roll to high-resolution image generation with StyleGAN2-ada, demonstrating that Controlled-Minimax ef-fectively eliminates oscillatory behavior and ensures robust convergence where traditional methods fail. Our contribution can be summarized as: • We bridge the gap between discrete optimization al-gorithms and continuous dynamical systems by trans-forming the adversarial training process into a second-order Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). This shift in perspective allows us to apply classical tools from control theory to the typically unstable dynamics of GAN training. • We identify that the instability in adversarial learning stems from specific pathological eigenstructures of the Hessian that standard gradient fields cannot correct. • We introduce a novel stabilization mechanism called the Controlled-Minimax that views optimization as a control problem. By designing a specific feedback con-troller on gradient, we prove that our method achieves local exponential stability for general minimax prob-lems, offering a rigorous guarantee that is absent in heuristic regularization methods. • We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach across a spectrum of tasks, ranging from fundamental benchmarks (Dirac-GAN, 2D Gaussian, Swiss Roll) to high-dimensional training with StyleGAN2-ada. Our Controlled-Minimax consistently eliminates oscilla-tions and accelerates convergence, validating its practi-cal effectiveness alongside its theoretical soundness. 

1.1. Related Work Convergence Analysis and Static Bounds Many works on minimax optimization relies on strong assumptions re-garding the curvature of the objective function. Conver-gence rates have been established for settings such as convex-concave (Nemirovski et al., 2009), strongly-convex-strongly-concave (Palaniappan & Bach, 2016), and specific nonconvex-nonconcave case (Yang et al., 2020). Within these structured landscapes, Daskalakis & Panageas (2018) and Gidel et al. (2019) provide rigorous analysis of Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA), though requiring specific step-size conditions or negative momentum. Similarly, Mertikopou-los et al. (2018) explore extra-gradient methods in coherent games, while Hsieh et al. (2019) analyze convergence in non-convex non-concave settings. However, these guarantees falter in deep learning because they depend on rigid curva-ture profiles or specific timescale separations (Mescheder et al., 2017; Heusel et al., 2017). Furthermore, treating opti-mization steps as isolated updates, ignoring the accumulated trajectory and inertia of the parameters. 

Heuristic Regularization and Algorithmic Variants To address the rotational dynamics common in zero-sum games, Daskalakis & Panageas (2018) propose the Optimistic Mir-ror Descent, which effectively utilizes negative momentum to cancel out interaction terms, a technique further analyzed by Mokhtari et al. (2020) through the lens of proximal point approximations. Regularization strategies have also been pivotal. Nagarajan & Kolter (2017) analyze the local conver-gence properties of gradient penalties, introducing terms that stabilize the Jacobian of the vector field near equilibrium points. However, a key limitation of these approaches is that their convergence guarantees often rely on local linearity or strong operator assumptions. 

Control Theory and Dynamical Systems Xu et al. (2020) and Luo et al. (2023) have attempted to actively con-trol training dynamics using feedback loops. Chavdarova et al. (2019) further explored how differential equation solvers can reduce noise in game dynamics. Furthermore, Li & Orabona (2019) and Su et al. (2016) have drawn connec-tions between Nesterov acceleration and differential equa-tions, though primarily in single-objective settings. ## 6. Experiments 

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive experimental analysis ranging from low-dimensional pathological exam-ples to high-dimensional image synthesis tasks to empiri-cally validate the theoretical guarantees of the Controlled-Minimax framework. We begin by examining convergence behavior on synthetic benchmarks known to induce rota-tional dynamics. Specifically, we visualize optimization trajectories and verify local exponential stability on the Dirac-GAN (Mescheder et al., 2018) in Figure 1, the Swiss Roll manifold in Figure 2, and the 2D Gaussian mixtures (Rasmussen, 1999) in Figure 3. Subsequently, we evaluate the scalability and robustness of our method on large-scale adversarial image genera-tion using the StyleGAN2-ada (Karras et al., 2020) archi-tecture across three standard datasets of varying complex-ity: CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), LSUN (Bedroom and Cat categories) (Yu et al., 2015), and Flickr-Faces-HQ (FFHQ) (Karras et al., 2019). By employing the Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID) score (Heusel et al., 2017) to quan-titatively assess sample quality and diversity, we demon-strate that our Controlled-Minimax effectively suppresses oscillatory behavior and accelerates convergence relative to baseline methods without compromising generation fidelity in Figure 4 and Table 1.

## 7. Conclusion and Discussion 

In this work, we have addressed the fundamental instability of minimax optimization through the lens of continuous-time dynamical systems. We identified that the persistent limit cycles and divergence often observed in adversarial learning stem from the problematic eigenstructure of the loss function’s Hessian near equilibrium points. To over-come these inherent structural limitations, we introduced the Controlled-Minimax framework. Our approach leverages control theory to derive a specific feedback controller that actively steers the optimization trajectory. Theoretical analysis confirms that this framework guaran-tees local exponential stability regardless of the underlying Hessian spectrum, effectively stabilizing problems where standard gradient descent-ascent methods fail. Empirically, our extensive validation ranging from pathological synthetic benchmarks like the Dirac-GAN, 2D Gaussian and Swiss Roll to high-dimensional image synthesis with StyleGAN2-ada demonstrates that our Controlled-Minimax not only suppresses oscillatory behavior but also ensures robust and rapid convergence. Collectively, these results bridge the gap between control theory and deep learning, offering a principled solution to one of the most persistent challenges in adversarial training. Several promising directions remain for future exploration. The current framework relies on fixed control parameters which guarantee local stability; however, designing an adap-tive controller that adjusts to the local geometry could offer a pathway toward global stability guarantees. Furthermore, acknowledging the inherent discrepancy between contin-uous ODE models and iterative algorithms, future work should rigorously address discrete-time stability. Establish-ing stability conditions directly for the discretized update rules will be essential to fully reconcile the theoretical con-trol perspective with practical algorithmic behavior. 8Controlled-Minimax: Stabilizing Adversarial Optimization via Control Theory
