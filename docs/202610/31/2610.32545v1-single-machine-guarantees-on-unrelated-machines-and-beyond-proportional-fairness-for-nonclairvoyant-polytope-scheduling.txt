Single-Machine Guarantees on Unrelated Machines and Beyond:
Proportional Fairness for Nonclairvoyant Polytope Scheduling
Sven Jäger∗
Alexander Lindermayr†
Bart Zondervan‡
Abstract
The Polytope Scheduling Problem (PSP) is a general framework for preemptive scheduling
in which feasible instantaneous job allocations form a packing polytope. We study PSP in the
nonclairvoyant online setting, where job processing requirements are unknown until completion.
In this setting, Proportional Fairness (PF), a practical allocation rule with deep connections to
market theory, is the leading general-purpose algorithm. On a single machine, PF coincides with
weighted Round-Robin, which is 2-competitive for weighted completion time with uniform release
dates (optimal for nonclairvoyant algorithms) and (2 + ε)-speed O(1/ε)-competitive for weighted
flow time with arbitrary release dates. Beyond a single machine, PF was known to be 3.62-
competitive on unrelated machines and 4-competitive on polymatroids for weighted completion
time with uniform release dates (SICOMP 2026), and (e + ε)-speed O(1/ε2)-competitive on
polymatroids for weighted flow time (JACM 2018). This raises a central question: Are these
heterogeneous environments intrinsically harder than a single machine under nonclairvoyance?
We show that they are not: PF retains both single-machine guarantees on the novel class
of support-submodular polytopes, which includes unrelated machines and polymatroids. On
unrelated machines, this refutes a belief (JACM 2018, SODA 2025) that PF is not O(1)-speed
O(1)-competitive for weighted flow time. Our main technical contribution is an equivalent
characterization: a polytope is support submodular if and only if, for all job weights, the optimal
weighted completion time is concave in the processing requirements. This structural insight
underlies both guarantees.
For general PSP, the picture changes: we give the first lower bound above 2, showing that
every randomized nonclairvoyant algorithm has a competitive ratio of at least 2 + 10−5 for
weighted completion time, even with unit weights and uniform release dates. On the positive
side, we substantially improve the best known guarantee for weighted completion time from 27
(SICOMP 2026) to 4 via a new dual-fitting analysis of PF. For weighted flow time, we show that
PF achieves the first O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive nonclairvoyant guarantee for graph scheduling,
where jobs are the edges of a multigraph and feasible allocations form its matching polytope.
More generally, this extends to every packing polytope between a binary packing polytope with
constant column sparsity and its integer hull.

Introduction
Schedulers must often allocate scarce resources without knowing which jobs will arrive next or how
much work current jobs require. Such uncertainty is central to operating systems, cloud computing,
and network resource allocation. Its two canonical forms are unknown arrival times rj (online
arrivals) and unknown processing requirements pj (nonclairvoyance) [HSSW97; MPT94; SWW95].
The Polytope Scheduling Problem (PSP), introduced by Im, Kulkarni, and Munagala [IKM18],
provides a common language for preemptive scheduling. It views scheduling as instantaneous resource
allocation over time, where active jobs compete for limited resources. Formally, at each time t, a
scheduler irrevocably selects a rate vector y(t) from a downward-closed polytope P ⊆Rn
≥0 that
encodes all feasible allocations. Job j ∈[n] completes at the first time Cj such that

A feasible schedule must complete all jobs, so we assume that every job j has yj > 0 for some y ∈P.
Despite its compact formulation, PSP captures a wide range of independently studied models, includ-
ing multidimensional scheduling [GZH+11], multicommodity flow scheduling [KMT98], fractional
broadcast scheduling [BKN14; EP03], graph scheduling [DM20; LLM26; RS25; RS26], and scheduling
with concave speedup curves [Edm00]; see [CIP25; IKM18; JLM26] for an overview. Thus, results
for PSP apply across many diverse scheduling environments. A prominent example is unrelated
machines [AGK12; BSS16; HSSW97; LST90; Sku01], where machine i processes job j at a (known)
speed sij ≥0. Its rate polytope arises from fractional bipartite matchings between jobs and machines.
Most work on PSP focuses on two important quality-of-service metrics: weighted completion time
and weighted flow time. Before this work, the state of the art on nonclairvoyant online algorithms
for major subclasses of PSP can be summarized as follows:

matroids, an O(1/ε2)-competitive algorithm with speed augmentation e+ε is known [IKM18].1
All of these positive results are achieved by the Proportional Fairness (PF) algorithm, a prominent
practical scheduling algorithm [GZH+11; IKM18; KMT98] with deep connections to market theory
and fair allocation [EG59; JV10; Nas50]. At each time t, PF chooses the rate vector ypf(t) =

completion time and (2+ε)-speed O(1/ε)-competitive for weighted flow time [Edm00; KC03; MPT94].
For general PSP, it has remained open whether the factor of 2 is achievable [JLM26] and which
polytopes admit an O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive algorithm [CIP25; IKM18]. For unrelated machines,
Jäger, Lindermayr, and Megow conjectured that PF is 2-competitive for weighted completion
time [JLM26], whereas for weighted flow time, PF is “believed not to be O(1)-competitive” even
with O(1) speed augmentation [CIP25; IKM18]. This raises the following natural questions:
For which polytopes does PF retain the (optimal) single-machine guarantees of Round-
Robin? Are rich polyhedral allocation environments such as unrelated machines and
polymatroids intrinsically harder than a single machine under nonclairvoyance?

1.1
Our Results
Support-submodular PSP.
We identify a novel class of support-submodular polytopes on which
PF is 2-competitive for weighted completion time and (2 + ε)-speed O(1/ε)-competitive for weighted
flow time. This class contains both unrelated machines and polymatroids. Moreover, it captures
generalized flow scheduling [CIP25] and resource scheduling with substitutes (RS-S) with separable
utilities [IKM18], including concave speedup curves (Figure 1 and Section 4). Informally, support
submodularity requires that, for every nonnegative linear objective over rate allocations, the maximum
value attainable by a set of jobs exhibits diminishing returns as jobs are added.
Definition 1.1 (Support-submodular PSP). A downward-closed polytope P ⊆Rn
≥0 is support
submodular if for every α ∈Rn
≥0 the support function Hα : 2[n] →R≥0, U 7→max{⟨α, y⟩| y ∈PU},
is submodular, where PU := {y ∈P | yj = 0 ∀j /∈U}. A support-submodular PSP is a polytope
scheduling problem whose rate polytope is support submodular.
Our main algorithmic results rest on a characterization of support submodularity via the optimal
weighted completion time, viewed as the function p 7→OPT(p) of processing requirements p ∈Rn
≥0.
Theorem 1.2 (Characterization). A downward-closed polytope P ⊆Rn
≥0 is support submodular if
and only if, for every weight vector w ∈Rn
>0, the optimal weighted completion time p 7→OPT(p)
with uniform release dates is concave on Rn
≥0.
This characterization allows us to apply a simplified variant of the superadditivity framework of
Jäger, Lindermayr, and Megow [JLM26] to obtain the following result.
Theorem 1.3. Proportional Fairness is 2-competitive for minimizing weighted completion time in
support-submodular PSP and 3-competitive with arbitrary release dates.
Since the single-machine polytope is support submodular, no nonclairvoyant algorithm can achieve a
ratio below 2 for the entire class [MPT94]. Thus, Theorem 1.3 determines the optimal nonclairvoyant
competitive ratio for weighted completion time with uniform release dates on unrelated machines
and on all of support-submodular PSP. We next put this result into perspective from three angles.
• Unrelated machines vs. a single machine. Offline, Smith’s rule solves the problem
efficiently on a single machine [Smi56]. On unrelated machines, the problem remains solvable
in strongly polynomial time for unit weights without preemption [BCS74; Hor73], but becomes
APX-hard with preemption and migration [Sit17] or with arbitrary weights [HSW01]. Never-
theless, this increase in computational complexity causes essentially no loss in nonclairvoyant
competitiveness, even among polynomial-time algorithms.
• Nonclairvoyant vs. clairvoyant. With arbitrary release dates, our nonclairvoyant ratio
of 3 matches the best known guarantee of deterministic clairvoyant online algorithms on
unrelated machines with preemption and migration [BKL21], which learn the exact processing
requirements upon job arrival.
• Nonclairvoyant vs. offline.
Our results imply that PF is a polynomial-time (2 + ε)-
approximation for support-submodular PSP,2 which precisely matches the best known offline
approximation ratio for weighted completion time in PSP [JLM25; LLM26].
The structural insights underlying Theorem 1.2 are also the key to our main result for flow time.
Theorem 1.4. For every ε > 0, Proportional Fairness is O(1/ε)-competitive for minimizing weighted
flow time in support-submodular PSP with speed augmentation 2 + ε.

We improve both the required speed and the dependence on ε relative to [IKM18] for polytopes that
are both PF-monotone and support submodular, such as polymatroids, generalized flow scheduling,
and RS-S with separable utilities. The two classes are incomparable (Figure 1), as RS-S [IKM18]

our theorem strictly enlarges the set of polytopes for which PF is known to be O(1)-speed O(1)-

on unrelated machines. This refutes the belief of Chen, Im, and Petety [CIP25] that PF is not
O(1)-competitive on unrelated machines with O(1)-speed augmentation. The belief is in line with the
result of Gupta, Im, Krishnaswamy, Moseley, and Pruhs [GIK+12], who showed that the most natural
extension of weighted Round-Robin to heterogeneous machines is not O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive
for weighted flow time, concluding that scheduling heterogeneous processors “isn’t as easy as you
think”. In this paper, we show that with Proportional Fairness, it is as easy as you think. A more
complex nonclairvoyant algorithm is known to be (1 + ε)-speed O(1/ε2)-competitive on unrelated
machines [IKMP14], but it is tailored to this setting.
General PSP.
We show that no (randomized) nonclairvoyant algorithm can be 2-competitive for
total completion time with uniform release dates. To our knowledge, this is the first lower bound for
PSP beyond the single-machine lower bound of 2 from 1994 [MPT94].
Theorem 1.5. The competitive ratio of every randomized nonclairvoyant algorithm for total comple-
tion time in PSP is at least 2 + 10−5, even for graph scheduling with uniform release dates.
Since this bound holds for every nonclairvoyant algorithm, the separation between support-
submodular PSP and general PSP is intrinsic to the problem rather than an artifact of PF or
of our analysis. Together, Theorems 1.3 and 1.5 resolve the open question discussed above in both
directions: unrelated machines are no harder than a single machine for nonclairvoyant weighted
3


completion time, whereas PSP in general is strictly harder.
On the technical side, our concavity-based approach does not yield any competitiveness guarantee
for general PSP: by Theorem 1.2, whenever the polytope is not support submodular, the optimal
weighted completion time is not concave for some weights, and in general it does not even admit a
constant-factor concave approximation (Appendix B). Instead, we give a novel dual-fitting analysis
of PF, which significantly improves the previous bounds of 27 [JLM26] and 128 [IKM18].
Theorem 1.6. Proportional Fairness is 4-competitive for minimizing weighted completion time in
PSP with arbitrary release dates.
Finally, for weighted flow time, we give the first O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive nonclairvoyant
algorithm for graph scheduling [DM20; LLM26; RS25; RS26], where jobs are the edges of an
undirected multigraph and rate vectors must lie in the graph’s matching polytope. Graph scheduling
is in general neither support submodular nor PF-monotone, its optimal weighted completion time
need not be concave, even with unit weights (cf. Appendix C.3), and by Theorem 1.5 it is strictly
harder than a single machine for completion time. Nevertheless, we establish a relaxed structural
property that yields the following result.
Theorem 1.7. For every ε > 0, Proportional Fairness is (4 + ε)-speed O(1/ε)-competitive for
minimizing weighted flow time in graph scheduling.
In fact, Theorem 1.7 is a special case of a more general result. The matching polytope is the integer
hull of the fractional matching polytope (the degree-constraint relaxation), whose constraint matrix
is the incidence matrix of the graph and has column sparsity 2. We show that, for every c ∈N, PF
is (2c + ε)-speed O(c/ε)-competitive on every downward-closed polytope between a binary packing
polytope with at most c ones per column and its integer hull (see Corollary 6.12).
1.2
Further Related Work
Kashiwabara and Takabatake [KT03] studied downward-closed polyhedra whose support function is
submodular on the entire nonnegative lattice. Support submodularity only requires submodularity
among the restrictions α ⊙1U of a fixed nonnegative vector α, where 1U denotes the indicator
vector of a job set U and ⊙the coordinate-wise product, and to our knowledge it has not been
studied previously. We show that the condition of [KT03] implies support submodularity and PF-
monotonicity and holds for polymatroids, generalized flow scheduling, and separable RS-S (Section 4).
Unrelated machines are support submodular but not PF-monotone [JLM26], so they violate the
condition of [KT03], and hence support-submodular PSP is strictly broader.
Nonclairvoyant weighted completion time.
PF is known to be 2-competitive on a single
machine [KC03; MPT94], on identical parallel machines [BBEM12; DGBL00], and, with unit weights,
on related machines and machines with restricted assignment [JLM26]. Nonclairvoyant completion-
time scheduling with precedence constraints on a single machine has also been studied [GGKS19;
JW24; LLMS23]. With arbitrary release dates, PF has a competitive ratio of at least 2.19 even on a
single machine [JLM26], although a different algorithm is 2-competitive in this setting [JSWW25].
For minimizing makespan, PF is O(log n)-competitive, which is best possible [LLM26; SWW95].
Clairvoyant weighted completion time.
In clairvoyant online scheduling, processing require-
ments are revealed when jobs arrive.
On unrelated machines with preemption and migration,
Bienkowski, Kraska, and Liu [BKL21] refined the doubling framework of [HSSW97], improving the
competitive ratio from 4 [HSSW97] to 3 for deterministic algorithms and from 2.886 [CPS+96]
to 2.443 for randomized algorithms. Thus, PF matches their deterministic clairvoyant bound of 3,
and, in contrast to the algorithm of [BKL21], PF can be implemented in polynomial time while
4


losing only a factor of 1+ε (see Appendix D). The previously best known polynomial-time guarantee
for clairvoyant weighted completion time was 4.62 [JLM26].
Nonclairvoyant weighted flow time.
Every randomized nonclairvoyant algorithm has a com-
petitive ratio in Ω(log n), even with unit weights [MPT94], and this bound is achieved [BL04]. In
their seminal paper, Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs [KP00] showed that a simple nonclairvoyant
algorithm for unit weights is O(1)-competitive with speed 1 + ε; such an algorithm is called scalable.
This was later extended to weighted jobs [BD07; KC03] and identical parallel machines [CGKK04].
On unrelated machines, SelfishMigrate is (1 + ε)-speed O(1/ε2)-competitive for weighted flow
time [IKMP14]. Several related models have received problem-specific treatment. For example,
scalable nonclairvoyant algorithms are known for unweighted flow time with sublinear speedup
curves [EP12], broadcast scheduling [BKN14], and more general flow-time norms [EIM11]. For
related machines and unit weights, Gupta, Krishnaswamy, and Pruhs [GKP11] showed that PF
is (2 + ε)-speed O(1/ε3)-competitive, and a scalable algorithm is known [GIK+12]. Beyond these
models, Im, Kulkarni, Moseley, and Munagala [IKMM16] gave an (e + ε)-speed O(1/ε2)-competitive
algorithm for weighted flow time on monotone multicluster PSP that uses PF on each cluster. Con-
stant guarantees in the unit-weight setting on a single machine can also be achieved via processing
time predictions [ALT21; ALT22; GKPW26], ε-clairvoyance [GKL+25], or both [GKL+27].
Clairvoyant weighted flow time.
Anand, Garg, and Kumar [AGK12] showed that a greedy
algorithm is scalable and O(1/ε)-competitive on unrelated machines. Chen, Im, and Petety [CIP25]
use gradient descent on residual scheduling objectives to obtain scalable clairvoyant algorithms under
substitutes conditions. We analyze nonclairvoyant PF through an exact characterization of concavity.
Our support-submodularity condition is strictly weaker than their general rate-polytope substitutes
hypothesis, which implies the lattice submodularity studied in Section 4.2. They treat unrelated
machines separately. We give more details on this comparison in Appendix A. For graph scheduling,
Dinitz and Moseley gave a (2 + ε)-speed O(1/ε2)-competitive algorithm [DM20]. Without speed
augmentation on a single machine, total flow time can be solved optimally with Shortest-Remaining-
Processing-Time first (SRPT) [Sch68]; for weighted flow time, no deterministic online algorithm is
O(1)-competitive [BC09], but parameterized upper bounds are known [AT18; BD07; CKZ01].
Offline approximations.
In the offline setting, where all jobs and their processing requirements
are known in advance, there is a (2 + ε)-approximation for weighted completion time in general
PSP [JLM26; LLM26]. For the special case of unrelated machines, a stronger 1.698-approximation is
known [Sit17], and for related machines, even a PTAS is possible [CK01]. There is also a PTAS for
minimizing weighted flow time on a single machine [ARW26].

2.3
A Randomized Lower Bound for General PSP
The factor 2 for weighted completion time on support-submodular polytopes cannot extend to
general PSP, even for randomized nonclairvoyant algorithms (Theorem 1.5).
Our construction uses graph scheduling on a path with four vertices, replacing its three edges by
groups A, B, and C of N, 3N, and 2N parallel unit-weight jobs, respectively. We choose random
processing requirements such that every nonclairvoyant algorithm completes in expectation at most
one job per unit of used capacity of the two inner vertices, and at most 2N of these completions are
attributed to the vertex shared by A and B. Hence, at most min{2t, t + 2N} jobs complete by time t
in expectation, and the expected total completion time is at least 10N2. Processing each group
at rate 1
2 in nondecreasing order of processing requirements achieves 5N2 + O(N) in expectation.
After A has completed, however, B and C are constrained only by their shared vertex, and with
probability 1 −O(1/N) group B then processes shorter jobs than C. Processing B before C during
a time interval of length Θ(N) thus reduces the total completion time by Ω(N2), and Yao’s principle
yields the ratio 10/(5 −9 · 10−5) > 2 + 10−5.

Online algorithms for PSP.
So far, we have described PSP as an offline problem, in which
the entire instance is known in advance. In the online setting, the instance is instead revealed
over time. An online algorithm learns about a job j only at its release date rj, at which point the
weight wj and the column Bj become known. The algorithm constructs the schedule over time,
that is, at every time t it has to fix the rate vector y(t) based only on the jobs released until time t,
without any knowledge of future jobs, and it cannot revise this decision later. A clairvoyant online
algorithm additionally learns the processing requirement pj at time rj, whereas a nonclairvoyant
online algorithm does not learn pj until job j completes at time Cj. Even though an online algorithm
13


does not know the full matrix B at time t, it can choose a rate vector y(t) ∈P, because unreleased
jobs have rate zero, and thus the constraints By(t) ≤1 only involve the columns of released jobs. In
our analysis, we therefore always work with the polytope P of the entire instance.
Definition 3.1 (Competitive ratio and speed augmentation). Fix either objective and a class of
instances. For an instance I, let ALG(I) denote the objective value of the schedule produced by an
online algorithm ALG, and let OPT(I) denote the optimal objective value among all feasible schedules
for I. If I is clear from the context, we omit it. The algorithm is c-competitive if ALG(I) ≤c·OPT(I)
for every instance I in the class. Its competitive ratio is the infimum of all such c.
With speed augmentation s ≥1, the algorithm constructs an s-speed feasible schedule, whereas the
offline optimum remains restricted to feasible schedules, that is, to speed 1. Let ALGs(I) denote
the objective value of the schedule produced by the algorithm with speed s. We call ALG s-speed
c-competitive if ALGs(I) ≤c · OPT(I) for every instance I in the class. For randomized algorithms,
both inequalities are required in expectation over the algorithm’s internal randomness, for every
fixed input I independent of that randomness.

PF-monotone PSP.
Im, Kulkarni, and Munagala [IKM18] studied a class of polytopes in which

a job never decreases when another job leaves the system.
Definition 3.3 (PF-monotone PSP). For fixed positive job weights, a PSP instance and its rate
polytope are PF-monotone if yi(S \ {j}) ≥yi(S) for every S ⊆J, every j ∈S, and every i ∈S \ {j}.

paths and even cycles. Since j and k are unmatched in P, each is either isolated in P △Q or an
endpoint of an alternating path. Moreover, their possibly trivial path components are distinct.
Indeed, if they shared a path, it would have even length because both vertices lie on the job side of
the bipartition, forcing one of them to be matched in P, a contradiction.
We now construct two new matchings P ′ and Q′ by redistributing edges along each component
of P △Q. If the path containing j is nontrivial, assign the edge incident to j to P ′ and alternate
edges along the path between P ′ and Q′. Similarly, if the path containing k is nontrivial, assign the
edge incident to k to Q′ and alternate thereafter. On all remaining components, keep edges from P
in P ′ and edges from Q in Q′ (edges in P ∩Q are kept in both P ′ and Q′).
By construction, P ′ is a matching in M × (U ∪{j}) and Q′ is a matching in M × (U ∪{k}). Every
edge of P △Q belongs to exactly one of P ′ and Q′, while every edge of P ∩Q belongs to both,
exactly as for P and Q. Thus w(P ′) + w(Q′) = w(P) + w(Q). Therefore,
g(U) + g(U ∪{j, k}) = w(P) + w(Q) = w(P ′) + w(Q′) ≤g(U ∪{j}) + g(U ∪{k}).
Hence g is submodular. For wij = αjsij, we have g = Hα by the discussion above, so unrelated

Weighted Flow Time for Support-Submodular PSP and Beyond
By Theorem 5.6, the optimal weighted completion time of a support-submodular PSP instance with
release dates 0 is the minimum of the linear functions p 7→⟨απ, p⟩. For p ∈Rn
≥0, let the support
be supp(p) := {j ∈J | pj > 0}. If an optimal schedule for p completes the jobs in the order π,
then π attains this minimum, and supp(p) is empty or a suffix of π (see the proof of Theorem 6.6),
so Hαπ(supp(p)) = w(supp(p)) by Lemma 5.1. We use these properties to show that PF is (2 + ε)-
speed O(1/ε)-competitive for minimizing weighted flow time on support-submodular polytopes.
Relaxing the equality Hαπ(supp(p)) = w(supp(p)) to the inequality Hα(p)(supp(p)) ≤c · w(supp(p))
for suitable prices α(p) yields a more general result, whose speed and competitive ratio depend on c
and which covers polytopes beyond the support-submodular class. We show that every downward-
closed polytope between a binary packing polytope with column sparsity c and its integer hull admits
a c-approximate certificate. As a special case, we obtain the first O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive
nonclairvoyant algorithm for weighted flow time in graph scheduling, which is not support submodular
in general (see Proposition C.3). Throughout this section, OPT0(p) denotes the optimal weighted
completion time of the instance with processing requirements p and all release dates equal to 0.
Definition 6.1 (c-approximate certificate). Let P be a polytope and w ∈Rn
>0.

Application: Sparse Binary Packing Constraints
We now identify a class of polytopes admitting approximate certificates. This class is characterized by
the property that each job occupies a bounded number of unit-capacity resources. The corresponding
packing constraints form a binary matrix with sparse columns, and we also allow any strengthening
that preserves all integral allocations.
Definition 6.7 (c-sparse binary polytope). A downward-closed polytope Q = {y ∈Rn
≥0 | By ≤1}

7.2
Dual Fitting for General PSP
The previously best known competitive ratio for general PSP is 27, which is achieved by PF [JLM26].
Our concavity-based approach does not extend to general PSP, because even approximating OPT by
a concave function up to a constant factor is impossible (see Proposition B.2). Instead, we analyze
PF directly via dual fitting against the standard mean-busy-time relaxation, as in [IKM18; JLM26].
In contrast to these analyses, which split the unfinished jobs at a global quantile of their normalized
rates, our dual solution discounts the credit of each job based on its own normalized rate. This
improves the guarantee to 4, even with arbitrary release dates.

Conclusion
Support submodularity characterizes concavity of the optimal weighted completion time for all job
weights and yields improved guarantees for Proportional Fairness.
Weighted completion time.
PF is 2-competitive on support-submodular PSP, where the crucial
ingredient of our analysis is the concavity of the optimal-value function. This result is optimal,
since no nonclairvoyant algorithm can do better than 2 even on a single machine [MPT94]. Thus,
unrelated machines and a single machine admit the same nonclairvoyant guarantee despite their
different offline complexities [Sit05; Sit17; Smi56].
For arbitrary release dates, we show that PF is 3-competitive on support-submodular PSP. In
contrast to the case of uniform release dates, PF is not better than 2.19-competitive even on a
single machine [JLM26]. PF is therefore not optimal in this setting, as 2-competitive nonclairvoyant
algorithms exist for a single machine with arbitrary release dates [JSWW25].
For general PSP, our dual-fitting argument improves PF’s competitive ratio from 27 [JLM26] to 4.
Moreover, we show that every randomized nonclairvoyant algorithm has a competitive ratio of at
least 2 + 10−5, even with unit weights and uniform release dates. Hence, general PSP is strictly
harder than a single machine under nonclairvoyance.
Weighted flow time.
Even on a single machine, speed augmentation is necessary: without it, no
nonclairvoyant algorithm is O(1)-competitive, even for unit weights [MPT94], and no deterministic
clairvoyant algorithm is O(1)-competitive for weighted flow time [BC09]. For support-submodular
PSP, we show that PF with (2 + ε)-speed is O(1/ε)-competitive via a potential-function argument
that leverages the concavity of the optimal-value function. Our analysis is tight for PF [Edm00].
In particular, PF is O(1)-speed O(1)-competitive on unrelated machines, contrary to the belief
stated in [CIP25]. This improves the previous (e + ε)-speed O(1/ε2) guarantee [IKM18] on the
intersection with PF-monotone PSP, including polymatroids, generalized flow scheduling, and RS-S
with separable utilities.
For graph scheduling, PF is (4 + ε)-speed O(1/ε)-competitive, giving the first O(1)-speed O(1)-
competitive nonclairvoyant algorithm. Furthermore, for every downward-closed polytope between a
binary packing polytope with at most c ones per column and its integer hull, our potential-function
argument shows that PF with (2c+ε)-speed is O(c/ε)-competitive. For general PSP, no deterministic
nonclairvoyant algorithm can be O(1)-competitive with speed o(√log n) [IKM18].

machines by separate arguments [CIP25, Sections 5.5–5.7].
Potential functions.
The potential of [CIP25] combines the residual optimum of the algorithm’s
remaining jobs with that of a joint instance containing one copy of each job for the algorithm and
one for the optimal schedule. Supermodularity of the residual optimum ensures that this potential
does not increase when a job arrives [CIP25, Lemma 4.4], and the gradient-descent direction controls
its drift. Our potential instead evaluates OPT0 on the lag between PF and an arbitrary fixed feasible
schedule (Section 2.2). An arriving job has zero lag, so arrivals do not change the potential, and the
drift is controlled by the concavity of OPT0 and its supporting prices from Theorem 5.6. PF itself
never computes a residual optimum and only depends on the set of active jobs, their weights, and
their columns Bj.

Polynomial-Time Implementation of Proportional Fairness
PF recomputes its rates by solving (CP) for the current set of active jobs whenever a job is released
or completes (see Section 3.2). We show that approximate solutions, which can be computed in
polynomial time, suffice for all our guarantees up to a factor of 1 + ε.
Computational model.
Our computational claims assume rational input and an explicit matrix B,
or a well-described rational rate polytope with a polynomial-time separation oracle. For unrelated
machines and generalized flow scheduling, the compact extended formulations in Sections 4.2 and 4.3
supply the required representation. Graph scheduling admits polynomial-time separation for its
matching polytope [KV18, Theorem 12.21].
54


Approximate rates.
Since each job is released once and completes once, PF solves at most 2n
instances of (CP). However, PF cannot always be executed exactly, because for some rational
inputs the optimal solution of (CP) is irrational [JV10, Example 48]. For a nonempty active set S
and δ > 0, the ellipsoid method yields feasible rates ey(S) with |eyj(S) −yj(S)| ≤δ for every j ∈S
in time polynomial in the input encoding length and log(1/δ) [JLM26]. The maximum rate ymax
