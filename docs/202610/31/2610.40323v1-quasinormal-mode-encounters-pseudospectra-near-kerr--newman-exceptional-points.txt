Exceptional points (EPs), at which two quasinormal frequencies and their eigenvectors coalesce,
can strongly enhance the spectral instability of black hole quasinormal modes. We investigate this
behavior in Kerr–Newman spacetimes through pseudospectra and condition numbers for neutral
massless scalar fields and coupled gravitoelectromagnetic (GE) perturbations. For the scalar field,
we follow the (ℓ, m) = (1, 1) spheroidal branch and derive the energy norm from the stress-energy
tensor. For GE perturbations, we use a coupled four-field system equipped with a positive radiative
energy. At the selected near-EP backgrounds, the condition numbers of both modes in the close pair
exceed those of the two more-damped modes shown for comparison at the same background. Along
a matched GE parameter path, the frequency splitting follows a square-root law and the condition
numbers grow as the inverse square root of the distance to the EP. The pseudospectral contours
resolve the transition from individual-mode sensitivity to the collective response of the pair. Within
the discretized problems and energy norms considered here, these results quantify the amplification
of spectral instability near EPs and provide a basis for studying their contribution to black-hole
ringdown.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Quasinormal modes (QNMs) describe the resonant re-
sponse of a black hole (BH) to perturbations [1–3]. The
real and imaginary parts of their frequencies determine
the oscillation and damping rates, which depend on the
remnant’s mass, spin and electric charge.
Measuring
these frequencies is the basis of black-hole spectroscopy,
through which one can infer remnant properties and test
general relativity [4–8]. A QNM frequency alone does not
determine the excitation of the mode or its sensitivity to
changes in the evolution equations. These questions re-
quire information beyond the spectrum, supplied by tools
such as pseudospectra, condition numbers and transient-
growth analysis [9–16].
Studies of several BH systems
have found QNM shifts that are disproportionately large
compared with the perturbations of the equations that
produce them [17–26].
The structure of the BH response extends beyond
ordinary QNM resonances, encompassing algebraically
special solutions [2, 27], late-time tails [28–33], direct
waves [34–36], and exceptional structures in the QNM
spectrum [37–45].
An EP occurs at particular back-
ground parameters where two QNM frequencies and their
eigenvectors coalesce. As the modes approach one an-
other, their individual contributions can become highly
sensitive even when their combined response remains fi-
nite [46, 47].
Relating this behavior to an observable
waveform requires treating the pair as well as its sepa-
rate members [48].
The coupled gravitational and electromagnetic spec-
trum of Kerr–Newman (KN) BHs offers a setting in which
to study these effects. Earlier calculations mapped this
spectrum and identified interactions between photon-
sphere and near-horizon modes, together with eigenvalue
repulsion [49–51]. Scalar Kerr and KN spectra also ex-
hibit mode interactions and EPs [15, 41, 52, 53]. Pseu-
dospectral scaling near exceptional structures has been
studied in a modified Regge–Wheeler potential [54], and
coupled GE KN EPs and mode exchange have been re-
ported in Ref. [42].
The choice of norm and allowed perturbations is es-
sential to a pseudospectral calculation. Even separated
equations and accurate QNM frequencies [55, 56] do not
fix a response measure on their own. We use an energy
obtained from the scalar stress current and a positive
energy for the coupled four-field GE system, accounting
for the conversion from an equation source to forcing of
the evolution state. Each near-EP pair is compared with
two more-damped modes at the same background. We
also follow a matched GE parameter path toward coa-
lescence, which allows the EP scaling to be tested with-
out changing the mode identification between successive
backgrounds.
We introduce the common definitions in Sec. II, fol-
lowed by the scalar formulation and results in Secs. III
and IV. Sections V–VI A develop the coupled GE cal-
culation.
The physical interpretation and convergence
requirements are discussed in Sec. VII, before the impli-
cations for ringdown and evolving backgrounds are con-
sidered in Sec. VIII. Derivations and numerical checks
are collected in the appendices.

A.
Resolution and the outgoing-wave problem
As Fig. 6 illustrates, convergence of the frequencies
alone does not establish convergence of the condition
numbers. The latter also depend on the left vectors, en-
ergy weights and source conversion. Higher arithmetic
precision reduces roundoff within a chosen discretization
but cannot remove radial or angular truncation error. Ei-
ther truncation can shift the critical background near an
EP. The varying-N curves combine resolution effects with
a changing distance from the EP selected at N = 200,
Lang = 6, and cannot be interpreted as a continuum
condition-number limit. Angular convergence at the dis-
played GE pair-gap scale has yet to be established.
The outgoing-wave spectral problem also requires end-
point conditions beyond a positive energy integral. An
unwanted solution may decay so rapidly at a compact-
ified endpoint that ordinary smoothness tests admit it.
Specifying the allowed endpoint behavior is essential in
this setting.
Hyperboloidal resonance expansions and
rigorous Kerr results clarify the issue [59, 60], but the
corresponding coupled KN construction is still needed.
Appendix E describes the endpoints for the fields used
here. The figures represent the discretized response, with
separate frequency, energy, arithmetic and interpolation
checks reported in Appendices B and D.
B.
Meaning of the radiative energy
The scalar norm follows from the field’s stress-energy
tensor, whereas the GE norm assigns a positive energy
to the chosen curvature and connection fields. Their rel-
ative weights and normalization determine which field
changes count as small. Relating these changes to phys-
ical Einstein–Maxwell perturbations requires satisfying
the constraints and reconstructing the metric and elec-
tromagnetic variables. The GE norm used here has not
been identified with the canonical energy of those per-
turbations or with the energy needed to produce an ar-
bitrary physical source.
Relevant approaches include positive energies for ax-
isymmetric KN perturbations [61], coupled physical-
space estimates [62, 63], canonical-energy methods [64]
and radiative-amplitude reconstruction [65]. Each uses
different states or assumptions.
To obtain a physical
gravitational or electromagnetic signal from the response
studied here, one must supply the appropriate source and
extraction maps. Until that calibration is available, the

The coupled finite-generator condition numbers mea-

may produce a smaller frequency shift. For the stored

VIII.
CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK
Our calculations show enhanced energy-norm spectral
sensitivity near EPs in neutral massless scalar and cou-
pled GE KN perturbations. At the selected backgrounds,
both members of each close pair have larger condition
numbers than either of the two displayed comparison
modes. The smaller pair value exceeds the larger com-
parison value by approximately 96.6 in the scalar case
and 48.09 in the GE case. Each comparison uses a fixed
background and discretization. Along the matched GE
path, the frequency gap and condition numbers follow the
expected square-root and inverse-square-root laws. The
contours and condition numbers together identify EPs as
amplifiers of local spectral sensitivity in these numerical
problems, without establishing a universal ordering of all
QNMs or overtones.
The collective response is particularly relevant to BH
spectroscopy. At a second-order EP of the generator, the
coalesced pair contributes

The selected GE pair illustrates the difficulty of infer-

noise could test recovery of the pair center and splitting
using ordinary ringdown models and regular two-mode
models [48, 66, 67]. Such tests should include additional
QNMs, the prompt response and tails where relevant [34].
Another question is how a gravitational-wave response
changes when the background evolves near an EP. Be-
cause an EP is a degeneracy in the parameter space of the
evolution operator, neither spatial propagation of a wave
packet nor a sweep of the real driving frequency on a sta-
tionary KN background takes the background through it.
A passage would require a physically admissible history
g(T) = (a/M, Q/M) approaching the critical parame-
ters. The stationary path parameter in this work is not
physical time. Absorption-driven changes in a Kerr BH’s
mass and spin can already alter mode excitation with-
out an EP passage [68]. A KN calculation would require
consistent flux balance and charge conservation, together
with the additional terms introduced by time-dependent
coordinates and field normalizations. Even at conserved
electric charge Q, the ratio Q/M can change through the
evolution of M.
An EP in a generic complex non-Hermitian family re-
quires two real tuning conditions [69]. An exact cross-
ing along a one-parameter history would require corre-
sponding tuning. Even a nearby passage calls for an as-
sessment of the coupling induced by background evolu-
tion before replacing the dynamics with QNMs of suc-
cessive stationary BHs [70]. Direction-dependent mode
conversion occurs in other non-Hermitian dynamical sys-
tems [71, 72], but has yet to be established for the
KN gravitational-wave response.
Exchange of station-
ary eigenvalue branches, dynamical state transfer and


12
hysteresis are distinct phenomena. The stationary pseu-
dospectra calculated here do not demonstrate the lat-
ter two effects. Their astrophysical relevance would also
depend on whether admissible histories approach the
charged near-extremal backgrounds considered in this
work.
Further work should establish the outgoing-wave con-
tinuum problem and test convergence of the full re-
sponse while retuning the EP as radial and angu-
lar resolution change.
Physical sources and gravita-
tional/electromagnetic waveform extraction can then be
used to determine whether the enhanced spectral sensi-
tivity leaves a measurable collective ringdown signature.
