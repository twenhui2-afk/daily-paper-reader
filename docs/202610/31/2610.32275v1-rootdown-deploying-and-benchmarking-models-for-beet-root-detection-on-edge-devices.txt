RootDown: Deploying and Benchmarking Models
for Beet-Root Detection on Edge Devices
Gerardus Croonen , Andreas Trondl , Julia Simon , and Daniel Steininger
AIT Austrian Institute of Technology, Giefinggasse 4, 1210 Vienna, Austria
Center for Vision, Automation & Control
gerardus.croonen@ait.ac.at
Abstract. Quality inspection of sugar beets after harvest and storage
is critical to preserve sugar content and ensure optimal processing ef-
ficiency. The transfer of harvested beets to field clamps or trucks pro-
vides an opportunity to visually inspect individual beets automatically
on loader conveyors. However, this is a challenging task as the large
number of beets causes partial overlap and occlusion, complicating both
annotation and detection. In this work, we propose an end-to-end image-
based pipeline for detecting partially occluded sugar beets in loading
scenarios as a first step toward automated quality inspection. We intro-
duce a dedicated benchmark and present an automatic annotation strat-
egy that leverages the SAM 3 foundation model to generate bounding
boxes. We compare models trained on manually annotated and auto-
matically generated labels. Additionally, we investigate deployment con-
siderations by benchmarking multiple inference configurations, including
execution on an edge device, under realistic operational constraints re-
garding precision, inference time and power consumption. Our results
show that, despite substantial occlusion and inherent annotation am-
biguity, foundation-model-based labels can substitute for a substantial
share of manual annotation, trading labeling effort for data volume at a
modest accuracy cost. Furthermore, we demonstrate that a Raspberry Pi
5 equipped with a Hailo accelerator enables high-performance, low-power
inference, making it a practical solution for mobile outdoor deployment.

Introduction
The availability of sugar beets during harvest far exceeds the processing capacity
of sugar factories, resulting in most beets being stored in field clamps, which
typically consist of several hundred thousand beets. After storage periods of up
to 90 days, the clamps are transferred to sugar factories by truck, where a human
estimator assesses beet quality by visual inspection, focusing on soil adhesion,
topping quality and the presence of mold, rot and vegetation. Detached soil
settling at the bottom of truck beds during transfer reduces the effectiveness of
visual inspection, which is limited to the top layer of beets. Internal evaluations

A vital first step towards automated visual inspection of sugar beets is their
robust detection and separation. Therefore, the aim of this work is to develop,
evaluate and deploy a real-time image-based method for detecting beets with
moderate to high visual overlap on conveyors. We present an end-to-end pipeline
that integrates data acquisition, automatic annotation, training, evaluation and
deployment across various hardware platforms, and characterizes performance
in terms of inference time, precision and power consumption (Fig. 1), providing
the following key contributions:
1. We introduce RootDown, the first benchmark for partially overlapping
sugar beets captured on conveyors during realistic loading operations, and
propose a scalable annotation strategy that integrates a foundation model
to substantially reduce manual labeling effort.1
2. We quantify the impact of manually curated and automatically generated
annotation sources, recording conditions and camera viewpoints on detection
performance through systematic experimentation.
3. We deploy an end-to-end system for sugar-beet detection, systematically
measuring detection accuracy, inference time and power consumption across
a diverse range of embedded and general-purpose hardware platforms.

Related Work
Weather-based decision-support tools predict soil moisture and stickiness to op-
timize harvest timing [14] and reduce soil tare. Operational optimizations of beet
lifters, such as inducing beet motion, reduce soil adhesion in wet clay soils [37].
In-loader cleaning mechanisms maximize soil removal through mechanical impact
while limiting beet damage [11]. Camera-based quality assessment of agricultural
produce has been studied for decades [4,7,33], yet applications to root vegeta-
bles such as sugar beets remain limited. A camera-based yield-estimation system
based on ellipsoid reconstruction of potatoes is proposed in [18]. The Semantic-
SugarBeets dataset [9] focuses on segmentation but is limited to non-overlapping
beet scenarios. Early automated inspection systems employed classical machine-
vision pipelines that combined segmentation, handcrafted features and classifiers
such as k-NN, PCA, LDA, SVM or RVM [2,16,29,40]. While these approaches
showed promising results, they were typically evaluated under unrealistic condi-
tions, including controlled backgrounds and complete object isolation.
CNN-based methods have substantially improved detection and segmenta-
tion performance in agricultural applications such as fruit counting, nutrient
deficiency detection [39], defect segmentation, yield estimation and seed coat-
ing inspection [6]. Modern detectors such as Faster R-CNN [5,34], YOLO vari-
ants and transformer-based architectures perform well in cluttered environments
but require large, carefully annotated datasets. Conveyor-based object detection
has been studied in industrial domains such as mining, food processing, recy-
cling and retail. Produce inspection systems for tomatoes, oranges, mangoes and
lentils have been reported under low-throughput conditions with fixed lighting
and no object overlap [28,32,36,38]. A YOLO-based foreign-object detector for
cut vegetables is presented in [22]. Geometric reconstruction and size estima-
tion of aggregate materials on conveyors using morphological operators is pro-
posed in [21]. Occlusion-aware detection has been addressed through part-based
models [1, 12, 42], attention mechanisms [17, 24, 41] and augmentation strate-
gies [13, 20, 44]. Explicit overlap-modeling approaches include occlusion-aware
human pose estimation [31] and UOAIS [3], which models visible, amodal and
occlusion masks to infer object ordering. Such methods are more challenging to
apply to natural objects such as sugar beets because of their high intra-class
variability and lack of a stable semantic part structure.
Foundation vision models such as Segment Anything 3 (SAM 3) [8] and
CLIPSeg [26] demonstrate strong zero-shot segmentation capabilities but are
unsuitable for real-time edge deployment due to their high computational de-
mands. Recent work applies such models to annotation bootstrapping and do-
main adaptation, accelerating dataset creation; prompt-ensemble fusion further
improves mask quality [23,27,43]. Although (semi-)automatic annotation, inter-
active segmentation and active learning reduce labeling effort, their performance
depends on strong initial proposals and degrades under heavy clutter and oc-
clusion [30, 35]. Despite growing interest in agricultural vision systems, the de-
tection of partially overlapping natural objects, particularly root crops in oper-
ational settings, remains underexplored. Existing methods are rarely evaluated

dedicated benchmark, a semi-automatic annotation workflow and an in-depth

RootDown Benchmark
As no publicly available image dataset exists for sugar-beet detection on convey-
ors, we acquire image data under operational conditions across various camera
positions, loading locations and lighting conditions. Using efficient manual an-
notation strategies and, for comparison, a fully automatic annotation method,
we create a dedicated benchmark for sugar-beet detection on conveyors.
Fig. 2: Camera positions and viewpoints used in three scenarios: Front (A/B, top
view), Side (C, side view) and Back (C, top view).
3.1
Image Acquisition, Sampling and Meta Annotation
Image acquisition is performed on the conveyors of a commercial beet loader
during unscripted loading operations. Three GoPro Hero 9 cameras are mounted
at selected positions on the front and back conveyors (Fig. 2) to capture multi-
ple viewpoints for subsequent comparative evaluation. Recordings are conducted
over three days and 52 loading sessions using both regular (30 fps) and time-lapse
(1 fps) camera modes. After recording, sequences are sampled uniformly at 1 fps
and trimmed to exclude periods during which the conveyor is stationary. To
complement the operational recordings, high-resolution images of soil-covered
beets are captured on the ground using a Sony Alpha S camera. Finally, 597
images are randomly selected from the extracted material to balance the view-
point distribution and minimize redundancy. The selected images are resized to
a height of 1080 pixels while preserving the aspect ratio.

Meta Parameters. To facilitate the analysis of benchmark characteristics and
model performance under varying conditions, all images are manually annotated
with the following attributes:
– Scenario denotes the camera viewpoint, with Front referring to camera
mount points on the loader’s front conveyor, Back and Side referring to
mount points on the rear lifting conveyor, and Ground denoting manually
captured images acquired in the field. Fig. 4 shows representative recordings
for each acquisition scenario.
– Lighting defines the natural lighting conditions as Sunny, Diffuse or a com-
bination of both (Mixed).
– Location differentiates between four recording sites.
Fig. 3: Relative distributions of annotated benchmark samples across acquisition sce-

The distributions of the annotated meta-parameters, summarized in Fig. 3,
indicate that the benchmark covers a realistic range of lighting conditions. Diffuse
lighting is common due to the frequent occurrence of foggy and overcast weather
during the autumn and winter beet harvest seasons. Parameters such as Scenario
and Location are fairly evenly distributed, with the exception of the rare Side
scenario, which results from severe vibrations causing the camera to rotate from
the Back viewpoint into a side-facing orientation.
3.2
Manual and Automatic Annotation
Manual annotation is performed using CVAT [10] by assigning bounding boxes
to individual beets in 312 randomly selected images, resulting in a total of 6,336
annotated instances and requiring approximately 25 hours of annotation effort.
The SAM 3 foundation model [8] is employed to automatically extract object
masks from all 597 images using text prompts. Counterintuitively, neither highly
specific terms such as sugar beet, beet root, beet or root, nor generic descriptions
such as round objects or balls reliably produced accurate masks. In contrast, the

Methodology
Object detection constitutes the first stage of the proposed real-time quality-
inspection pipeline due to its computational efficiency and versatility. Accurate
bounding boxes provide the foundation for downstream tasks such as object
counting, region-based classification and soil-adhesion estimation from semanti-
cally segmented individual beets or aggregated per-beet statistics. As these tasks

depend on reliable object localization, object detection serves as a natural entry
point for the pipeline. The present work focuses on this detection stage, while
downstream components are left for future development.
4.1
Benchmark Ablation
To assess the influence of automatic annotation on model performance, we con-
struct several benchmark variants. Our baseline variant, Core-Manual, consists
exclusively of manually annotated data and uses a 50:20:30 split for training,
validation and test sets, respectively. Analogously, Core-Auto uses the same im-
age split but replaces the manual annotations in the training and validation
sets with the corresponding automatically generated labels. To investigate the
effect of additional automatically annotated data, we extend Core-Auto with
extra SAM 3-annotated images, resulting in the Ext-Auto variant. Finally, we
define Full-Auto, which uses all automatically annotated images available in the
benchmark. An overview of all benchmark variants, including image counts, split
ratios and annotation types, is provided in Tab. 1.

4.2
Hardware Setup
To evaluate model performance across a range of computational scenarios, we
select hardware platforms with varying levels of processing power, energy con-
sumption and mobility. Our selection, summarized in Tab. 2, includes laptop and
workstation CPUs, a workstation GPU, and a single-board computer (SBC),
both with and without a neural processing unit (NPU). For the SBC platform,
we select the Raspberry Pi 5, which offers native support for Hailo AI edge accel-
erators (Fig. 6). In particular, the Hailo-8L and Hailo-8 modules provide up to 13
and 26 TOPS of compute, respectively, combined with low power consumption.
The Hailo toolchain converts pretrained models into a dedicated binary format
(HEF, Hailo Executable File), which can be executed directly from a Python
environment. Version 4.20 of both the Hailo PCIe driver and the associated tool
suite is used for all experiments.

4.3
Model Training
The model architecture used throughout the experiments must be compatible
with all deployment targets, with the edge platform imposing the most restrictive
constraints. We therefore employ the YOLO11 architecture [19] for all experi-
ments, as YOLO-based detectors are well supported and achieve competitive
accuracy on Hailo hardware [15]. The Ultralytics YOLO11 framework [19] is
used for training, as it provides stable model variants, an established train-
ing pipeline and seamless model export. Pretrained YOLO11-Large models are
fine-tuned for 100 epochs using a batch size of eight and input resolutions of
6402 and 10242 pixels. Unless otherwise stated, all hyperparameters are kept at
their default values. Benchmark annotations and model predictions are stored
as COCO-format JSON files for evaluation. The precision metrics reported in
this work are computed using the standard pycocotools implementation.

4.4
Model Deployment
The Python-based Ultralytics training framework supports multiple export for-
mats for x86-64, ARM and CUDA-based platforms. We select ONNX due to its
broad compatibility with CPU- and GPU-based systems, supporting both FP16
and FP32 inference. OpenVINO is additionally evaluated because it provides
optimized INT8 quantization specifically targeting CPU deployment. TensorRT
is excluded, as it is optimized exclusively for CUDA-enabled GPUs, prevent-
ing a direct comparison across all target platforms. INT8-compressed models
are obtained through post-training quantization using calibration data from the
training set to determine suitable scaling parameters and biases. Deployment on
Hailo devices relies on the Hailo Dataflow Compiler (DFC). The parsed model’s
non-maximum suppression (NMS) block is replaced by a hardware-optimized
equivalent, and the network is quantized using a calibration set of randomly
selected training images. Models are compiled separately for the Hailo-8 and
Hailo-8L to evaluate both accelerators independently.
4.5
Real-Time Optimization and Power Constraints
Power consumption is a critical consideration for edge devices, particularly in
mobile and outdoor applications. Total power draw can be decomposed into idle
power, consumed regardless of workload, and marginal power, defined as the
additional power required to perform useful computation. In practice, however,
measuring idle and marginal power separately is technically challenging and of
limited practical relevance. For real-world applications, the key metric is the total
energy consumed per unit of useful work while processing a continuous camera
stream at a fixed frame rate. This measure directly determines system-level
design constraints, such as the battery capacity required to sustain operation
for a specified duration.
Runtime Measurements. To characterize runtime behavior, we measure in-
ference times across all evaluated devices using trained YOLO11 models span-
ning a range of complexities from Nano to X-Large. To ensure consistent and
reproducible measurements, inference time is measured for each device–model
combination over 500 frames using randomized dummy input tensors. An addi-
tional 100 frames are processed as a warm-up phase before timing begins, and
the reported values are averaged across all measured frames.
Power Requirements. To estimate power and energy requirements under re-
alistic conditions, we measure total power consumption and the number of pro-
cessed images over time on a Raspberry Pi 5 single-board computer paired with
a Hailo-8 accelerator. From these measurements, we derive the average energy
cost per inference. Idle power draw is recorded separately to distinguish baseline
consumption from workload-dependent power usage.

5.3
Power Usage
We evaluate system-level energy consumption on a Raspberry Pi 5 with 16 GB
RAM equipped with a Hailo-8 accelerator running Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm.
A Microsoft LifeCam Cinema USB camera is used for image acquisition and
an external USB 3.0 storage device remains connected throughout the experi-
ments. All nonessential peripherals, including mouse, keyboard and monitor, are
disconnected prior to measurement. To measure total system power consump-
tion, a fully charged USB power bank supplies the device while a Hailo-optimized
YOLO11-Small model continuously processes live camera input at 30 fps. At the
applied input resolution of 6402 pixels, this model variant achieves an mAP50–95
of 52.9%. The system operates for 7 hours, 32 minutes and 33 seconds, consum-
ing 62.6 Wh with an average power draw of 8.29 W. During this period, 825,390
images are processed, corresponding to an average energy cost of 0.0758 mWh
or 272.9 mJ per frame.
In a second experiment, idle power consumption is measured while the sys-
tem remains fully connected but performs neither image capture nor inference.
The power bank delivers 63.2 Wh over 18 hours, 34 minutes and 5 seconds, cor-
responding to an average idle power draw of 3.4 W. This baseline consumption
reflects the energy requirements of the CPU, camera, NPU and storage sub-
system even in the absence of useful computation. These results demonstrate
that a Raspberry Pi 5 paired with a Hailo-8 accelerator can sustain continuous
inference for more than 7.5 hours using a consumer-grade 20,000 mAh (3.7 V)
USB power bank. Operating time can be extended substantially when infer-
ence is intermittent, user-triggered or combined with low-power modes, making
this hardware configuration suitable for mobile and outdoor deployments with
moderate energy constraints.

Conclusion
This work demonstrates that foundation-model-based annotation can substan-
tially reduce manual labeling effort in agricultural object-detection pipelines.
Although domain-specific prompts such as sugar beet did not yield reliable seg-
mentation, SAM 3 produced high-quality masks for overlapping sugar beets


14
G. Croonen et al.
when prompted with the broader term vegetable, recovering 90% of instances
and achieving 46.1% mAP50–95 relative to manual annotations. These results
highlight the potential of foundation models to support large-scale benchmark
creation with minimal human intervention. For sugar-beet detection on convey-
ors, a YOLO11-Large model trained on fully manual annotations reaches 56.9%
mAP50–95 at 6402 and 55.3% at 10242, while models trained exclusively on auto-
matically generated labels reach 50.6% and 52.6%, respectively. The remaining
gaps at matched resolution must be considered in light of the approximately 25
hours required for manual bounding-box annotation, highlighting the efficiency
of automated annotation at scale. Our analysis further shows that deployed-
model performance depends strongly on platform-specific optimizations. On x86-
64 CPUs, OpenVINO INT8 models provide the lowest inference times without
sacrificing accuracy, while OpenVINO FP16 performs best on the Raspberry Pi
5 CPU. Integrating a Hailo-8 accelerator enables efficient, sustained and fully
untethered operation using commodity USB power banks, providing a practical
and energy-efficient edge solution for real-time quality inspection in agricultural
environments.
Outlook. Future work should explore the scalability of automatic annotation
and its combination with manual annotations, focusing on how benchmark size
affects the gap between supervised and automated training. A systematic scal-
ing study would reveal whether automatically generated labels narrow this gap
and whether performance saturates due to label noise or limited data variability.
Improvements could come from confidence-based filtering, iterative self-training
or prompt-ensemble strategies to reduce sensitivity to domain-specific phrasing.
Semi-synthetic 2D or 3D rendered images could cover edge cases and improve
robustness under challenging conditions, such as high contrast, dim or night-
time lighting or wet beets. Beyond detection, integrating instance and seman-
tic segmentation would enable estimation of soil adhesion, beet tare, damage
and vegetation. Object tracking and scene-scale estimation can further enhance
operational metrics. Evaluating robustness across illumination, occlusion and
conveyor speeds will increase practical applicability. Finally, additional edge-
optimization efforts such as model compression, energy-efficiency analysis and
device connectivity will improve feasibility for sustained autonomous field oper-
ation in real-world settings.
