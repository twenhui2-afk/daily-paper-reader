Beyond Data Schedule: Fast LLM Pretraining on Data
Mixtures by Loss-Reweighting
Jiazheng Li1,2,∗, Huaqing Zhang1, Jing Xu1, Tianle Cai2, Jingzhao Zhang1,†
1Tsinghua University, 2ByteDance Seed
∗Work done at ByteDance Seed, †Corresponding authors
Abstract
Large language models often acquire specialized capabilities through successive pretraining stages,
but these gains can come at the cost of forgetting earlier knowledge. In this work, we investigate
whether such trade-offs can be improved by changing how strongly each domain contributes to
learning while keeping its data exposure fixed. We find that, in controlled knowledge–mathematics
experiments, loss reweighting produces a single checkpoint that surpasses the best knowledge and
reasoning scores attained along either stage trajectory. This reveals an independent control that
data-mixture ratios alone do not capture. Building on this finding, we propose Lare (Loss-Adaptive
Reweighting under fixed Exposure), which automatically adjusts domain weights from running loss
statistics. Motivated by a theoretical analysis of gradient noise, its normalized inverse-square-root
rule balances relative loss scales without changing the sampled mixture or requiring additional
runs to search for weights. Across knowledge, mathematics, and code, Lare improves all five
evaluated benchmarks for a 1.5B model trained on 240B tokens, averaging +7.84 pp over a matched
direct mixture, including gains of +17.06 pp on GSM8K and +9.00 pp on MATH. These findings
demonstrate that adjusting domain loss scales can improve joint capabilities without additional
domain exposure.

Introduction
Large language models are increasingly expected to master multiple capabilities simultaneously—from broad
world knowledge to mathematical reasoning, code generation, and instruction following. Meeting these
demands has pushed pretraining beyond a single stationary data distribution: practical recipes now begin with
broad-coverage corpora and subsequently introduce midtraining or continued-pretraining stages enriched with
curated, domain-specialized data [17, 34, 40, 49]. Yet whether successive stages are the best way to combine
multiple domains remains an open question. Emphasizing a new domain can rapidly improve the corresponding
capability, but these gains often come at the cost of forgetting what was learned earlier [26, 45, 70]. In effect,
multi-stage training selects a point on an acquisition–retention trade-off rather than producing a model that
is simultaneously strong across domains.
A natural alternative is to train all domains jointly from a stationary mixture, replacing stage ordering

sampling proportions [13, 37, 61, 66]. By keeping every domain active throughout training, joint mixtures
avoid the abrupt distribution shifts introduced by stage boundaries—yet they do not automatically recover
the strongest outcome of each specialized stage. Different ratios favor different domains, so a direct mixture
often settles at an interior compromise (see 1). Recent work further shows that domain learning can exhibit
threshold behavior as mixture shares vary, while repeated exposure yields scale-dependent returns [16, 30, 31].
Optimizing the mixture ratio therefore searches for a better point on the trade-off rather than eliminating it.
Our work starts with the identification of a central limitation of ratio tuning: a single control variable is
asked to serve two fundamentally different roles. Increasing a domain’s sampling share changes both its
exposure—how much data the model sees—and, under the standard unweighted objective, its optimization
scale—how strongly prediction errors in that domain shape parameter updates. These two quantities are
conceptually distinct [53], and their preferred allocations need not coincide: the amount of data needed
for sufficient coverage may differ from the relative loss weight that yields the strongest joint model. This
observation motivates a different intervention: hold the sampled-data mixture fixed and adjust domain loss
coefficients directly, decoupling what the model sees from how strongly each domain drives learning. Figure 2
visualizes this distinction: remixing and reweighting trace different paths through the capability plane.
Figure 1 illustrates the opportunity created by this decoupling. With the sampled-data mixture and token
budget held fixed, a suitable mathematics loss coefficient improves both MMLU and GSM8K over direct
mixing—and even surpasses the best value attained on each benchmark along the multi-stage trajectories.
A fixed coefficient, however, merely trades one search problem for another: its optimal value is unknown a
priori, expensive to sweep at pretraining scale, and may not transfer across data compositions or training

We therefore introduce Lare (Loss-Adaptive Reweighting under fixed Exposure), an online domain-level
controller that derives relative loss weights from running domain-loss statistics. After learning-rate warmup, it
applies a normalized inverse-square-root rule that adapts relative optimization scale without globally rescaling
the training objective. Under this rule, the 7:1 adaptive run reaches a competitive region of the fixed-weight
trade-off in a single training run, eliminating the need for a post-hoc oracle grid. The core contributions of
this work are threefold:
• We disentangle domain exposure from optimization scale in multi-domain pretraining. Under matched
30B-token exposure, stage scheduling, direct mixing, and loss reweighting yield markedly different endpoints;
a suitable fixed loss coefficient simultaneously exceeds the best knowledge and mathematical-reasoning
scores attained along either stage trajectory (Figure 1 and Section 2).
• We introduce Lare, a lightweight online controller that derives domain coefficients from running loss
statistics. Its normalized inverse-square-root rule adapts relative optimization scale after warmup while
keeping sampled exposure fixed and the mixture-averaged coefficient at one. The controller reaches a
competitive region of the fixed-weight trade-off in a single run, without auxiliary search runs (Section 3).
• We validate the method across optimizers, sampled-data ratios, domain compositions, and training budgets
(Section 4). At 4× Chinchilla with K:M = 7:1, Lare improves MMLU by 0.39 points, GSM8K by 13.57
points, and MATH by 8.40 points. Extending training with mathematics and code to 8× Chinchilla yields
gains of 0.83, 17.06, 9.00, 6.71, and 5.60 points on MMLU, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, and MBPP,
respectively, over direct mixing (Table 2).

Mixture Ratios Couple Exposure and Scale
Data-mixture ratios determine both which domains the model observes and how strongly their losses enter a
standard unweighted update. We first show empirically that changing loss weights at fixed exposure reaches a
broader capability frontier than changing the mixture itself. We then formalize why these interventions are
not interchangeable and return to matched-exposure training trajectories to isolate their consequences.

Lare: Loss-Adaptive Reweighting under Fixed Exposure
Section 2 establishes that loss scale matters, but a fixed weight does not provide a practical rule for choosing
the coefficients. Grid search requires a separate pretraining run for each candidate, and the best setting may
not transfer across data mixtures, compute budgets, or optimizers. We therefore seek an online alternative
that infers and adapts relative domain weights from signals already available during training, eliminating
auxiliary search runs.

3.3
Validating the Reweighting Dynamics
We validate Lare in the controlled 30B-token, 7:1 knowledge–mathematics setting used for the fixed-weight
sweep. The model, sampling distribution, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and token budget are all held
constant; the loss-weighting rule is the only experimental variable. Figure 5 compares MMLU and GSM8K
trajectories of the full fixed-weight grid against Lare, alongside the relative weight wM,t/wK,t selected online.
Together, the three panels address two questions: whether the controller is stable after warmup, and whether
the scale it selects occupies a useful region of the fixed-weight trade-off.
After warmup, the selected weight ratio remains stable near the fixed 1:2 and 1:3 settings (Figure 5). At the
final checkpoint, Lare improves direct 1:1 weighting by 0.84 MMLU and 12.13 GSM8K points. Its endpoint
is competitive with fixed 1:2, showing that the controller can select a useful loss scale in one run without an
auxiliary sweep.

Ablating over Optimizers
Alongside Lare, we introduce Activation Gradient Descent (ActGD), a base optimizer that constructs row-wise
updates in a whitened activation geometry and applies the same update family to every learned matrix.
Algorithm 1 summarizes the practical update, while Appendix F provides its derivation and implementation
details.
To select the optimizer for the subsequent experiments, we compare AdamW [39], Muon [36], and ActGD
under the same 1.5B model, 1× Chinchilla budget, and K:M = 7:1 direct-mixture objective. This comparison
deliberately excludes Lare so that it isolates the base optimizer rather than its interaction with loss reweighting.
Because optimal hyperparameters do not generally transfer across optimizers, unequal tuning can confound
the comparison [60]. We therefore sweep the learning rate or rates independently for each optimizer and select
its best configuration at the full training budget instead of transferring a common learning rate; for the hybrid
Muon setup, the Muon and auxiliary AdamW learning rates are tuned separately. All remaining model, data,
and schedule settings are matched.

Conclusion
Multi-domain pretraining has two controls that standard mixture design conflates: domain exposure and
optimization scale. At fixed exposure, loss reweighting produces endpoints beyond the observed stage schedules
and expands the observed remix Pareto frontier. Our analysis attributes this separation to domain-specific
effective learning rates, which can reach loss trade-offs unavailable to a shared-rate mixture. We therefore
introduce Lare, an online controller that converts running domain losses into normalized inverse-square-root
weights while preserving the sampled mixture and fixing the mixture-averaged coefficient at one. Lare selects
a competitive scale in one run without a coefficient sweep. Experiments cover base optimizers, mixture
ratios, domain compositions, and 30B–240B token budgets. At 8× Chinchilla, the 1.5B model improves all
five evaluated benchmarks by 7.84 points on average over matched direct mixing, and every gain exceeds
its 4× counterpart. These results support treating data allocation and loss scaling as separate pretraining
design variables: exposure determines what the model sees, whereas reweighting determines how strongly
each domain shapes learning.
Ethics statement
This work studies domain-level loss reweighting for language model pretraining. Models trained with this
approach may inherit biases, harmful content, and privacy risks from their training data. Reweighting
domain losses may also change the influence of these properties, which is not evaluated in this study. Our
reported improvements concern downstream task performance and should not be interpreted as evidence
of improved safety or fairness. Models developed using this approach require appropriate safety evaluation
before deployment.
Reproducibility statement
To ensure reproducibility, we provide the code, dataset and model in the supplementary materials and anony-
mous github https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Fast-LLM-Pretrain-on-Data-Mixture-by-Loss-Reweighting-F7F5.
In the README.md file included with the code, we present a step-by-step guide for reproducing our results.

Appendix
A
Related Work
Multi-stage pretraining.
Domain-adaptive pretraining and modern midtraining recipes introduce specialized
data in later stages to strengthen target capabilities [17, 34, 40, 49]. However, this temporal specialization
raises a fundamental retention question: studies of continual pretraining report substantial forgetting in some
regimes, while showing that self-supervised continuation can preserve prior knowledge more robustly than
supervised adaptation in others [11, 26, 45, 70]. These results indicate that forgetting is regime-dependent
rather than an inevitable consequence of continued training.
Data-mixture ratio selection.
The dominant approach to multi-domain data mixing optimizes the sampling
ratio itself [9, 38]. Offline methods such as DoReMi and DoGE infer domain proportions from proxy losses
or generalization estimates, while MDE evaluates candidate mixtures with domain-expert models [3, 13, 61].
Online methods instead adapt sampling during training: Skill-it! builds skill curricula, DGA uses target-
gradient alignment, Data Mixing Agent learns a transferable policy, and AC-ODM and HDS optimize schedules
from domain interactions or multiple training signals [6, 12, 14, 41, 65]. Further work refines the control space
by reconstructing domains from topics, estimating importance in a representation space, modeling domain
interactions, or unifying multiple data operations [33, 51, 62, 64]. All of these works change what the model
sees. Loss reweighting offers a complementary lever: it changes the contribution of already-sampled data to
the gradient, with precedents in task-level balancing and example-level language-model training [8, 27, 54, 55].
Importantly, sampling and loss coefficients can interact through gradient variance and generalization, and
may become coupled under importance correction or particular bilevel formulations [47, 53, 56].
Scaling laws for data mixtures.
A ratio selected in one configuration need not remain optimal as model
size, training budget, or domain reuse changes. RegMix and Data Mixing Laws fit performance-response
models from smaller proxy runs, and recent studies explicitly characterize how useful allocations shift with
scale, model capacity, and repeated use of limited domain data [16, 25, 30, 31, 37, 66]. Related systems seek
lower-cost transfer across configurations: Olmix reuses mixture knowledge as the available domain set evolves,
whereas DeMix and MergeMix use separately trained domain models or model merging as proxies for mixture
quality [7, 32, 59]. This body of work underscores why a single sampling ratio is difficult to transfer across
scales.

C
Data Sources and Processing
We use three data domains: general knowledge (K), mathematics (M), and code (C). All corpora are tokenized
with the GPT-2 BPE tokenizer, and every stored token carries an aligned loss mask and domain ID (0 for
K, 1 for M, and 2 for C). This appendix describes the source pools, filtering rules, QA conversion, and the
distinction between sampled-token exposure and loss-bearing tokens.
C.1
Sources and Filtering
Knowledge.
We use full-text web documents [50] as the knowledge-domain corpus. The prepared pool
contains 289.27B tokens.
Mathematics.
We collect mathematical problems and worked solutions from multiple sources [15, 24, 28,
29, 35, 46, 57, 58, 67–69]. We first remove examples with 13-token n-gram overlap against GSM8K [10],
MATH [20], AIME 2024 [43], AIME 2025 [44], HMMT 2025 [21], AMC 2023 [42], or the English split of
OlympiadBench [18]. We then deduplicate normalized (question, answer) rows in a fixed source order, retaining
the first occurrence. This conservative rule preserves distinct solutions to the same question. After filtering
and deduplication, the prepared pool contains 17.20B tokens.
Code.
We collect code-oriented prompts, responses, and source files from multiple sources [15, 23, 48]. We
remove examples with 10-token n-gram overlap against HumanEval [5], MBPP [1], GSM8K [10], or MATH [20].
We then perform exact cross- and intra-source deduplication on code answers of at least 200 characters,
followed by exact (question, answer) row deduplication at every length. The final training pool contains
245.16B tokens.

C.2
Training Records and Loss Masking
Record format.
After source-specific processing, every mathematics or code example is serialized as [q, a],
where q is the prompt and a is the target response. Any record whose complete serialization exceeds 4,096
GPT-2 BPE tokens is discarded.
Mathematics.
The source problem forms q. After a blank line, we append the following fixed instruction:
Please put the answer in \boxed{}.
The target response a retains the worked solution supplied by the source when one is available and always
ends with a normalized \boxed{...} line containing the final answer. Rows from which no reliable final
answer can be extracted are omitted.
Code.
We parse each code source according to its native format and add no shared instruction. For the
instruction corpus with explicit reasoning tags, q is the text before <think> and a is the complete response
after </think>; the intervening reasoning trace is removed. For corpora organized around fenced code blocks,
we split at the first fenced code block: the preceding text becomes q, while the fence and everything after it
become a. For the corpus of bare source files, each file is wrapped in a Python code fence and stored with an
empty q, reducing the record to [a].
24


Loss masks.
For mathematics and code, the stored mask is zero on eot and prompt tokens and one on
response tokens. For full-text knowledge, it is zero on document-separating eot tokens and one on document
text. During training, a second mask suppresses the first 100 prediction targets after every eot encountered
within a loader window. The distance from a preceding eot is computed only within that window and is not
carried across window boundaries. The effective loss mask is the intersection of the stored and training-time
masks.
Masks and domain IDs are attached to prediction targets. Thus, for the causal pair (xt, xt+1), the loss reads
the mask and domain ID associated with xt+1. A masked target contributes neither to the language-model
loss nor to the per-domain statistics used by Lare. Records are concatenated into a padding-free stream, and
the token, mask, and domain-ID arrays remain positionally aligned.

Activation Gradient Descent
ActGD is the base optimizer used in the main experiments, whereas Lare is the domain-level objective
controller studied in this paper. The two act at different points in the training pipeline: Lare determines
how strongly each domain contributes before the batch gradient is formed, while ActGD maps that gradient
to a parameter update. We include the derivation here both to make this separation explicit and to specify
the optimizer underlying the results in Section 4.
F.1
From Activation Matching to a Whitened Update
ActGD is motivated by the view that an optimizer should control the change induced in a layer’s activations,
rather than only the Euclidean displacement of its parameters. This view parallels layer-wise activation
matching in model merging [63], but the construction below is local to one optimization step and does not
assume an equivalence between model merging and training. More broadly, the observation that different
optimization geometries can yield different downstream behavior at similar pretraining loss motivates making
this geometry explicit [4].

F.4
Parameter Coverage and Distributed Implementation
For a linear map, forward hooks flatten the leading activation dimensions and estimate Ct = X⊤
t Xt/n in
FP32. When the number of activation rows exceeds the input width, the main configuration samples n′ = din
rows with replacement before forming the covariance. Query, key, and value projections in the same attention
block consume the same input tensor, so their covariance is computed and communicated once and then
reused for all three weights. The covariance EMA and the gradient EMA are stored in FP32; the inverse is
evaluated with four fixed Newton–Schulz polynomial iterations.
The embedding layer is the one-hot special case of the same construction. Its input second moment is diagonal,
so we store only the empirical token-frequency vector and apply its inverse by row-wise scaling in the stored
embedding orientation rather than materializing a vocabulary-sized covariance matrix. The language-model
head is treated as an ordinary linear layer using the second moment of its hidden-state inputs. Because the
architecture used here has bias-free linear maps and parameter-free normalization, all learned parameters are
matrices and no separate one-dimensional fallback optimizer is required.
In distributed training, activation statistics are averaged across data-parallel ranks before the update is
constructed. Each parameter’s inverse and update are computed on one deterministically assigned owner rank,
after which the disjoint update shards are combined with a sum reduction. The communicated update buffer
uses bfloat16, while the persistent EMA states remain FP32. The sharded optimizer checkpoints store the
step counter and both EMA tensors for each owned parameter; forward-hook statistics, shared-covariance
aliases, and temporary inverse buffers are step-local and are cleared after each update.
ActGD stores a gradient-sized first-moment tensor and an input-covariance tensor for each matrix, and
its additional computation is dominated by covariance formation and the approximate inverse. Activation
subsampling, query/key/value covariance reuse, cached identity matrices, and distributed owner sharding
