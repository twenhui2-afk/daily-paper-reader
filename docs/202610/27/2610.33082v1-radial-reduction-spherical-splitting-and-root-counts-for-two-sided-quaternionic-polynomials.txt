RADIAL REDUCTION, SPHERICAL SPLITTING AND ROOT COUNTS FOR TWO-SIDED QUATERNIONIC POLYNOMIALS 

R. LANIEWSKI 

Abstract. We study quaternionic polynomials whose coefficients may occur on both sides of the variable, using their degree in the four real coordinates. Such polynomials can have no roots, or many more isolated roots than their degree. A simple root has an invertible real derivative, and its sign is the sign of that derivative’s determinant. For a radial family, the roots over a non-real target reduce to one real equation. We obtain a sharp root bound and follow a change in the signed root count as roots escape to infinity. The main results concern spherical root sets for which the polynomial derivative is invertible in the two normal directions. Under a small polynomial perturbation, a sphere splits into simple roots corresponding to the zeros of a tangent vector field, when the derivative of that field is invertible at every zero. We obtain a sharp bound in terms of the perturbation degree and a signed count of two or of minus two. For even degrees, the construction yields polynomials with a single leading power and lower-degree terms whose simple root counts, divided by the fourth power of the degree, tend to one as the degree grows. An explicit quartic has 132 simple roots, and this count persists under small coefficient perturbations. For gradient perturbations, the roots correspond to equilibrium directions of an energy on the sphere. This correspondence gives energy thresholds for root disappearance and bounds the critical directions of polynomial orientation profiles in diffusion magnetic resonance imaging.

A two-sided quaternionic polynomial can omit zero. For q = x0 + x1i + x2j + x3k, the multiplication rules give ji − ij = −2k and ki − ik = 2 j, hence qi − iq = 2( x3j − x2k). Thus 

qi − iq − 1 has real part −1 everywhere. We therefore examine the transition between root existence and rootlessness, together with the root counts along it. We look for families in which the image, the number of isolated roots, their local degrees and their persistence under perturbation can all be determined. Throughout, a two-sided polynomial expression is a finite sum of words with quaternionic coefficients interspersed with the variable. We evaluate each word in its specified order. We call its degree in the four real coordinates the real coordinate degree. Degree refers to this real coordinate degree unless Brouwer degree is explicitly named. We write d for the power exponent, 

D for a perturbation degree, and N for the total real coordinate degree. These maps differ from polynomials in a formally central indeterminate and from the one-sided polynomials appearing in quaternionic root-existence theorems. Every non-constant one-sided quaternionic polynomial has a root [Niv41]. Eilenberg and Niven also allow a single highest-degree word with non-zero coefficients, together with lower-degree terms [EN44]. The companion-matrix approach to unilateral polynomials is developed in [DLDL06]. Janovská and Opfer study the classification and numerical computation of zeros of two-sided polynomials [JO10]. The exact counts and local degrees below complement this classification for radial and imaginary perturbations. General two-sided expressions admit further possibilities, and their representations and polynomial identities require separate attention [Mie12]. Evaluation of two-sided words gives the full space of real polynomial maps R4 → R4, as shown in (4). General persistence and Bézout estimates in that space are standard real and complex polynomial arguments. The quaternionic structure considered here comes from retaining the RADIAL REDUCTION AND SPHERICAL SPLITTING 3

power map and perturbing it by a real-valued term, or by an imaginary term that depends only on the imaginary part of the variable. For a degree-n polynomial in a central indeterminate over a division ring, the Gordon– Motzkin theorem bounds the number of conjugacy classes containing roots by n [GM65]. In the quaternionic one-sided case, isolated roots and spherical zero sets are studied by Pogorui and Shapiro [PS04]. The radial two-sided expressions below belong to a broader class, so these classical bounds do not directly govern their fibers. Our point of departure is the class (1) Pd,ϕ (q) = qd + ϕ(|q|d), d = 2 m ≥ 2, ϕ ∈ R[t].

The even exponent ensures polynomiality in real coordinates. A constant ϕ gives a real translation of the power map Qd(q) = qd, of real coordinate degree and Brouwer degree d, while the bound in Theorem 3.1 is stated for deg ϕ ≥ 1. The perturbation depends only on the norm of qd, so the map factors through the quaternionic power map as Pd,ϕ = Tϕ ◦ Qd, with Tϕ(w) = w + ϕ(|w|). Both factors respect quaternionic conjugation, and Tϕ preserves the imaginary part. In this family a scalar displacement changes the image and the degree, while the power map keeps its explicit fibers. Centrality localizes non-real fibers to a complex slice. Dependence on the norm then reduces their remaining two real coordinates to one radius, producing the bound 2 N .Section 6 separates these two reductions, and it shows that a radius measured by |q|2 attains the larger planar bound. 1.1. In this finite-dimensional coefficient space there is a constant CN such that a coefficient perturbation of size δ changes the map by at most CN δ(1 + |q|N ).
