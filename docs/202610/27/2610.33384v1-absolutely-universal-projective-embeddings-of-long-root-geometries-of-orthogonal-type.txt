ABSOLUTELY UNIVERSAL PROJECTIVE EMBEDDINGS OF LONG-ROOT GEOMETRIES OF ORTHOGONAL TYPE 

A. PASINI 

Abstract. Let G be a split Chevalley group of type Bn (n ≥ 3) or Dn (n ≥ 4), let L be its Lie algebra and Γ its long-root geometry, namely Γ is the line-grassmannian of the polar space associated to G. It is well known that L hosts a projective embedding of Γ, called the adjoint embedding, which realizes Γ as a projective variety in the projective geometry of the underlying vector space of L . It is also known that Γ admits the absolutely universal projective embedding, which is such that all projective embeddings of Γ can be obtained from it as projections. When the field F over which G is defined is finite, the universal embedding of Γ is just the same as the adjoint embedding. The same is not necessarily true when F is infinite. Indeed, as we shall prove in this paper, the universal projective embedding of Γ lives in a direct sum K ⊕ L for an F-vector space K, the dual of which is isomorphic to the derivation space Der( F) of F, which in general is non-trivial. Accordingly, the adjoint embedding of Γ is universal if and only if Der( F) is trivial, namely F is either perfect of positive characteristic or algebraic over its minimum subfield. 

1. The objects to be considered in this paper 

In this paper we shall study some problems concerning projective embeddings of Lie geome-tries. First of all we must explain what we are talking about, what embeddings and Lie geometries are. 1.1. Projective embeddings. In this subsection we recall some basics on projective embed-dings of point-line geometries, referring the reader to Shult [23] and [24, Chp. 4] and Ronan [22] for more on this topic. Let Γ = ( P, L ) be a point-line geometry, with P a the point-set and L as the set of lines. We assume that Γ is connected, namely its collinearity graph is connected, and no two lines of Γ are incident with the same set of points; thus we can regard the lines of Γ as subsets of P .A projective embedding of Γ (also embedding for short) is an injective mapping e from P to the point-set of the projective geometry PG( V ) of a vector space V , such that the image e(P )of P spans PG( V ) and every line of Γ, regarded as a subset of P , is mapped by e onto a line of PG( V ). If K is the underlying division ring of V , we say that e is defined over K.Henceforth, although e maps Γ onto a subgeometry of PG( V ), we write e : Γ → V to mean that e embeds Γ in PG( V ). We will also freely use expressions as “ e embeds Γ in V ”, “ e lives in V ” or “ V hosts e” which, although incorrect, streamline the exposure a bit. Accordingly, we call dim( V ) the dimension of e and we denote it by dim( e). We say that a geometry is embeddable if it admits an embedding as defined above. In other words, a point-line geometry is embeddable if it can be realized as a projective variety in such a way that its lines are some (possibily not all) of the projective lines contained in that variety. However, in the root system of type Cn the highest positive long root corresponds to 2 λ1. Accordingly, the Lie algebra corresponds to the Weyl module W2λ1 , which can also be described as a symmetrized tensor product of two copies of V = Wλ1 . An injective mapping of the point-set of Cn, 1(F) into the point-set of PG( W2λ1 ) can be defined in the same way as natural embeddings have been defined for Chevalley geometries in a previous paragraph of this subsection. The mapping obtained in this way is called the adjoint embedding of Cn, 1(F), but it is not a projective embedding. Indeed it maps lines of Cn, 1(F) onto non-degenerate conics of PG( W2λ1 ). Mappings like these are called veronesean embeddings . Veronesean embeddings are very interesting but we prefer not to get involved with them in this paper. 2. The problem we deal with in this paper 

It is tempting to believe that nearly all embeddable Lie geometries admit the absolute embed-ding and, for most of them, that absolute embedding is one of those that we already know. In particular, we might believe that nearly all Chevalley geometries admit the absolute embedding and in most cases this is the same as the natural one. The vague phrasing of this belief allows for a few exceptions. Indeed we have known some of them for a long time. For instance, embeddable grids with lines of size at least 5 admit no absolute embedding. Not even the generalized quadrangles described by Tits in [29, 8.6, II(a)] admit absolute embedding (they admit two distinct relatively universal embeddings). The long-root geometry A2,{1,2}(F) with F finite but not prime admits at least as many relatively universal embeddings as the automorphisms of F, hence no absolute embedding (Thas and Van Maldeghem [28]). 6 A. Geometries admitting a natural embedding which however is not universal are also known. For instance, the adjont embedding of A2,{1,2}(F) is relatively uiversal if and only if F is either perfect of positive characteristic or algebraic over its minimum subfield (Smith and V¨ olklein [26]). Dual polar spaces with lines of size 3 are also couterexamples: they admit the absolute embedding, but the latter is a proper cover of the natural one (Li [19], [20]). Chevalley geometries of type Cn,k (F) with char( F) = 2 also deserve to be mentioned. When char( F) = 2 and k < n the Weyl embedding of Cn,k (F) is not relatively universal, ultimately because the Weyl embedding of Γ = Cn, 1(F), which embeds Γ in PG(2 n − 1, F) as a symplectic polar space, is a proper projection of an (absolutely universal) embedding which maps Γ as a quadric in PG( d + 2 n − 1, F), where d is the degree of F over F2 = {t2}t∈F. If moreover F is perfect then Cn(F) = Bn(F). In this case we might state that the natural embeddings of the Lie geometries obtained from the building ∆ = Bn(F) = Cn(F) are the Weyl embeddings as defined according to the Bn-setting. However this doesn’t remove all incoveniences. Indeed the Weyl embedding of the dual polar space ∆ n in the Bn-setting (namely the so-called spin embedding ) is a proper projection of the Weyl embedding of ∆ n in the Cn-setting (which is absolutely universal, provided that |F| > 2). More counterexamples certainly exist but, according to the above optimistic belief, they cannot be too many. However this is definitely wrong. For instance, suppose that the field F admits a non-trivial automorphism. Then, as proved in [21], for every n ≥ 2, no absolute embedding exists for the long-root geometry An, {1,n }(F). Most likely, the same conclusion holds true for every Chevalley geometry ∆ J of multiple type defined over F. It certainly holds true when J contains two types which are not joined by a multiple stroke in the Coxeter diagram (Giuzzi and Pasini [14]). In contrast, it follows from the main result of Kasikova and Shult [18] that every Chevalley geometry of simple type admits the absolute embedding. This result naturally leads to the next question: is the natural embedding of a Chevalley geometry relatively universal (hence absolutely universal if the absolute embedding exists)? This is indeed the case for many Chevalley geometries (see e.g. [6]), but not all of them. Appendix A. Extensions of modules 

In this appendix we fix some terminology and recall some basics on extensions of modules, focusing on extensions U = W · V where dim( W ) = 1. Throughout this appendix G and F are a given group and a given field. A (right) G-module is a pair ( V, ρ ) where V is an F-vector space and ρ is a homomorphism from G to the group of invertible linear transformations of V such that ρ(g1g2)( v) = ρ(g2)( ρ(g1)( v)) for any two elements 

g1, g 2 ∈ G and every v ∈ V . By a little abuse, if a homomorphism ρ as above is given we say that V is G-module, also that the pair ( V, G ) a module. If V is a G-module we write v · g for ρ(g)( v), for every g ∈ G and v ∈ V . We extend this notation to subspaces of V and subgroups of G in a natural way. Explicitly, let X and v asubset and a vector of V respectively and F and g a subset and an element of G. Then we put 

X · g := {v · g}v∈X , vF := {vg }g∈F and X · F = ∪v∈X v · F = ∪g∈F X · g. In particular, v · G is the orbit of v under G and if X · G = X we say that G stabilizes X. We also take the liberty of writing g(v), g(X), F (v) and F (X) instead of v · g, X · g, v · F and X · F respectively. Given a G-module V , the action of G on the dual V ∗ of V is defined as follows: (108) ξ · g : x ∈ V −→ ξ(x · g−1), ∀g ∈ G, ξ ∈ V ∗.

In short, ξ ·g = ξ ◦g−1, where ◦ stands for composition of mappings. According to this definition, we have ξ · (g1g2) = ( ξ · g1) · g2 for every choice of ξ ∈ V ∗ and g1, g 2 ∈ G.Two G-modules V and V ′ defined over the same field F are isomorphic if there is a bijective linear mapping f : V → V ′ such that F (v · g) = f (v) · g for every v ∈ V and every g ∈ G.A.1. Generalities on extensions. Given a G-module U , a submodule of U is a subspace of U

stabilized by G in its action on U . If W is a submodule of U then an action of G is naturally defined on the quotient U/W as follows: ( u + W ) · g := u · g + W for every coset u + W of W

and every g ∈ G.Given another G-module V , the module U is an extension of V if U admits a submodule W ,called the kernel of the extension, such that V ∼= U/W (isomorphism of G-modules). We write 

U = W · V to mean that U is an extension of V with kernel W .54 A. PASINI 

If dim( W ) = d we say that U is a d-extension of V . In particular, if d = 1 then U is a 1-extension of V . In case W = {0} the extension U is trivial .We say that a non-trivial extension U = W · V splits if there is an injective linear mapping 

ϕ : V → U such that ϕ(V ) is stabilized by G and π ◦ ϕ = id V , where π is the canonical projection of U onto U/W ∼= V as vector spaces. If this is the case then U is the direct sum of W and ϕ(V )(direct sum of G-modules, of course); with a little abuse, U = W ⊕ V (or U = W × V , if we like products more than sums). called a quotient of the extension U (a proper quotient if W0 ⊊ W ).

Appendix C. Derivations 

Given a field F, a derivation of a field F is an additive maping d : F → F such that (123) d(ts ) = d(t)s + td (s), ∀t, s ∈ F.

As 1 = 1 · 1, property (123) implies that d(1) = 0. Therefore if F0 is the minimum subfield of F

then d(t) = 0 for every t ∈ F0 and it is not hard to prove that if t ∈ F is algebraic over F0 then 

d(t) = 0. Note also that d(t−1) = −t−2d(t) for every t ∈ F \ { 0} and d(tp) = pt p−1d(t) for every positive integer p. In particular, if char( F) = p > 0 then d(tp) = 0 for every t ∈ F.The derivations of F form an F-vector space Der( F), which we are now going to describe. The field F admits a maximum subfield Nder (F) such that every derivation of F induces the null map on it. When char( F) = p > 0 then Nder (F) = Fp := {tp}t∈F while when char( F) = 0 then Nder (F)is the algebraic closure F0 in F of the minimum subfield F0 of F. (Note that if char( F) = p > 0then Fp ⊇ F0.) A derivation basis of F is subset Ω of F \ Nder (F) such that every map from Ω to F is induced by a unique derivation of F. So, Der( F) is isomorphic to the space FΩ of all mappings from Ω to F.58 A. PASINI 

Every field admits a derivation basis (possibly the empty one) and all derivation bases of 

F have the same cardinality, which we call the derivation rank of F, denoting it by drank( F). Explicitly, if char( F) = 0 then the derivation bases of F are its trascendence bases (in this case drank( F) is the trascendence degree of F) while when char( F) = p > 0 then the derivation bases are the subsets X ⊂ F \ Fp such that Fp ∪ X generates F as a field and X is minimal with respect to this property. In particular, drank( F) = 0 (namely Der( F) = {0}) if and only if F = Nder (F), namely F is either perfect of positive characteristic or algebraic over its minimum subfield. Given a derivation basis Ω of F, fo every ω ∈ Ω we denote by dω the derivation which maps ω

onto 1 ∈ F and all elements of Ω \ { ω} onto 0 ∈ F. We denote by Der Ω(F) the subspace of Der( F)spanned by {dω }ω∈Ω. The space Der( F) is isomorphic to the dual of Der Ω(F) in a natural way and we have Der Ω(F) ∼= Der( F) if and only if Ω is finite.
