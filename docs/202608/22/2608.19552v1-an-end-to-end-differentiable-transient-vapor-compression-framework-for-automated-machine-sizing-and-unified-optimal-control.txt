Abstract
Accelerating the electrification of thermal energy requires vapor-compression heat pumps capable of
dynamic, grid-responsive operation. However, equipment engineering remains fragmented across static
rating-point selection, stiff multi-phase transient simulation, and gradient-based optimal control. Here, we
present an end-to-end differentiable, finite-volume vapor-compression framework implemented natively
in JAX that automates machine sizing directly from stated thermal duties and unifies dynamic simulation
with predictive control under a single compiled residual ˙y = f (t,y,u). Thermodynamic evaluations bypass
runtime root-finding via bilinear (p,h) manifolds pre-flashed from Helmholtz equations of state, enabling
analytical forward-mode automatic differentiation. Mass conservation across multi-phase coils is strictly
preserved by incorporating both (∂ρ/∂p)h and (∂ρ/∂h)p partial derivatives into the dynamic pressure
differential equation. The sizer directly inverts compressor displacement, electronic expansion valve area,
and heat-exchanger tube counts via four-point cycle synthesis and ε-NTU matching using the identical
polytropic compressor map. Crucially, the compiled physics kernel is shared symmetrically between
L-stable TR-BDF2 stiff integration and implicit-Euler Model Predictive Control (MPC), eliminating plant-
controller surrogate mismatch. Validated against open-access experimental benchmarks without parameter
fitting, the framework predicts cooling capacity with 7.37% MAPE across 16 mini-split operational runs
and bounds on-period cooling error within 1.19%–1.62% on utility-scale Hardware-in-the-Loop traces.
This work provides an open-source, differentiable foundation for automated machine synthesis, dynamic
grid orchestration, and gradient-based hardware-control co-design.
Keywords: HVAC; vapor-compression systems; finite-volume method; automated sizing; differentiable
physics; automatic differentiation; model-predictive control.

Introduction
Electrification of thermal energy demands agile, grid-interactive heat pumps capable of rapid load shifting,
frequency regulation, and autonomous operational optimization [1]. Building thermal loads account for over

one-third of global primary energy consumption and nearly 40% of energy-related carbon dioxide emissions.
Addressing these demands requires resolving two persistent structural disconnects across the engineering
lifecycle: a sizing-to-simulation disconnect, where static rating-point selections are manually transcribed into
dynamic simulation models, and a simulation-to-control disconnect, where high-fidelity non-linear models are
abandoned in favor of heuristic or mismatched linear surrogates due to non-differentiable equation-of-state
lookups.
Dynamic vapor-compression modeling has historically evolved into moving-boundary (MB) and finite-
volume (FV) formulations [1,2]. Rasmussen reviewed the moving-boundary and lumped formulations that
dominate control work [1]. Bendapudi et al. compared moving-boundary and finite-volume formulations on
centrifugal chiller transients [2], and Qiao et al. subsequently formulated a finite-volume flash-tank vapor-
injection heat pump [3]. Building simulation engines such as EnergyPlus [4] and the Modelica Buildings
library [5] place equipment models in zone and fluid networks, while rating solvers such as CYCLE_D-
HX [6] size steady-state cycles from component models. Our previous work treated constructal heat exchanger
allocation and dynamic response-based thermodynamic optimization [7,8]; however, existing formulations
do not generate an end-to-end differentiable, executable residual whose compressor map directly drives the
hardware sizer.
Two fundamental computational bottlenecks follow from this state of the art. First, machine geometry is
traditionally transcribed from static catalog points or constructal sketches into simulation models rather than
being synthesized directly from load profiles and boundary conditions. Second, thermodynamic evaluations [9]
are conventionally executed as external, non-differentiable calls, preventing exact gradient propagation for
modern optimal control [10] and forcing integration and predictive control to operate on separate, inconsistent
representations.
This paper resolves both challenges by formulating an end-to-end differentiable finite-volume plant
governed by a single compiled residual ˙y = f (t,y,u). High-accuracy Helmholtz property evaluations are
pre-flashed onto discrete (p,h) manifolds during model construction [9], allowing the resulting differentiable
interpolant to be integrated via L-stable TR-BDF2 [11] and reused directly for gradient-based Model Predictive
Control (MPC). The automated sizing engine inverts displacement Vdisp, expansion valve maximum area Amax,
and heat-exchanger tube counts directly from a stated thermal duty using the identical polytropic compressor
map. Linear and nonlinear MPC reuse f through implicit-Euler time steps without unrolling multi-stage
integration graphs, while the baseline operational traces demonstrate robust setpoint tracking under a PID
cascade. Table 1 contextualizes this contribution against existing modeling and design paradigms.
The mathematical model and thermodynamic closures are detailed in Section 2, the automated sizing
engine in Section 3, and the numerical integrator and control architectures in Section 4. Section 5 presents
the baseline R32 design example, systematic sizing cases, closed-loop dynamic experiments, and unfitted
algebraic validations against experimental datasets. Physical assumptions, numerical limitations, and future
research frontiers are delineated in Section 6.

2.10
Operating mode
In heating, outdoor air sees the evaporator and indoor air sees the condenser. Cooling swaps the pair. A
reversing-valve step remaps the state—the coils keep their inventory—and resamples cell profiles if ne̸ = nc.
6


A scheduled mode signal (1 = heat, 0 = cool) may operate the valve; without it, load sign, a deadband, and a
minimum dwell are used. The controller flips action and clears windup.

4.2
Quasi-steady reduction
When tfinal ≥1h, the default reduction warms up the full DAE for 180s, then advances only the slow states (Tz
and, optionally, Wz and mfr) while holding refrigerant (p,h,Tw). On one-hour runs, the refrigerant is relaxed
by implicit Euler—slow states held—at each 30s record step, and also when |∆N| ≥0.5Hz or |∆ueev| ≥0.02,
so capacity can follow the controller. Multi-day runs relax less often. Absolute time is passed to u(t).

Discussion
The residual is a first-principles plant with named closures, not a fitted digital twin. On the Ramírez set the
algebraic capacity MAPE is 7.37% and the largest absolute capacity error is 19.23%. Electrical-power and
COP MAPE are larger (19.34% and 18.14%), in part because the source records I ×120V without a power
factor. Heating errors on the NREL set are large and are reported in Table 4. Those discrepancies are what an
unfitted nameplate sizer produces when the cabinet geometry and refrigerant are not identified in the public
record. The reverse rows of Table 2 show a separate, internal limit: taking the larger compressor pushes
cooling Qcap/Qload above the Manual S cooling ceiling.
The closed-loop traces show that the sized plant, the integrator, and the PID cascade share one residual.
Instantaneous Qz/W at part load is a diagnostic of (20) and (12); it is not an AHRI or EN seasonal rating [13].
Seasonal ratings are computed only when the user supplies a time series, and the bin hours are then the dwell
times of that file. MPC is implemented on the same residual with a different discretization and does not
appear in Figures 1 to 3; the 90 s full-DAE comparison in the appendix is a numerical demonstration.
The formulation assumes acoustic equilibrium, subcritical two-phase flow, thermodynamic-equilibrium
slip, oil-free refrigerant, a single lumped zone, and—unless humidity is supplied—dry air. Frost, when it is
turned on, is a lumped mass with Hayashi and Yonko–Sepsy closures without automatic defrost schedule.

Conclusions
A finite-volume vapor-compression plant can be written as one residual from the mass and energy balances (4)–
(9), closed with Zivi density, a polytropic compressor, an orifice EEV, and Shah-type coil heat transfer, and
sized from a stated duty with that same compressor map. A stiff integrator advances the residual. Implicit-
Euler MPC can reuse it without a second plant model; the main figures use PID, and a 90 s linear-MPC run
