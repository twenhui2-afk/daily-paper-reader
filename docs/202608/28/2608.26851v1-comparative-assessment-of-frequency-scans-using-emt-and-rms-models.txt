# Comparative Assessment of Frequency Scans using EMT and RMS Models 

Clea B¨ urgel, Gustavo Valverde, and Gabriela Hug 

Power Systems Laboratory ETH Z¨ urich 

Z¨ urich, Switzerland 

{cbuergel, gustavov, hug }@ethz.ch 

Abstract —Frequency scans of inverter and grid impedances are crucial for assessing small-signal stability and system strength in inverter-dominated power systems. This paper compares frequency-scan results for several generating units, including a synchronous generator, grid-following, and grid-forming in-verters, using fully dq electromagnetic transient and root-mean-square (RMS) models in Simulink. The scans are performed for the same device rating and operating point. Frequency scans and time-domain simulations show that the RMS models are valid only at low frequencies. We find that the current-controlled grid-forming inverter is more difficult to represent in RMS than the voltage-controlled grid-forming inverter. However, including the inner voltage-control loops improves the accuracy of the RMS representation. The paper also investigates the influence of generator and grid impedance on the total system impedance seen from the point of common coupling and relates the oscillatory modes and zeros of the system to peaks and dips in the first and second singular values of the system impedance, respectively. 

Index Terms —Electromagnetic transient models, frequency scan, grid-following converters, grid-forming converters, root-mean-square models, small-signal system strength. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The integration of renewable generation and the decom-missioning of coal-, nuclear-, and gas-fired power plants are fundamentally changing the way power systems are operated, controlled, and analyzed. The fast and slow interactions of inverter-based resources (IBRs) with the network and other system components span both electromagnetic and electrome-chanical phenomena, demanding more sophisticated simula-tion frameworks [1]. As IBRs increasingly drive system dynamics, root-mean-square (RMS) simulations, intended to assess electromechan-ical phenomena, need to be replaced by high-fidelity elec-tromagnetic transient (EMT) simulations capable of capturing a broader frequency range. However, the high computational cost of EMT simulations has hindered their widespread adop-tion, especially for large-scale power systems, and many system operators continue to rely on RMS-based models and simulations. This inevitably needs to change as IBR penetration grows, rendering EMT the standard planning tool for assessing system stability. Frequency scans of inverter and grid impedance at the point of common coupling (PCC) have been adopted to study potential dynamic interactions, identify resonance fre-quencies, as well as support the assessment of impedance-based small-signal stability with Bode plots and the Nyquist criterion [2], [3]. Additionally, they are used to assess frequency-dependent system strength beyond the fundamental frequency [4], [5]. The Australian Energy Market Operator defines system strength as the ability of the power system to maintain and control the voltage waveform at any given location in the system [6]. Consequently, in a strong sys-tem, the voltage sensitivity to changes in current should be low, a property described by the frequency-dependent system impedance. While previous works have compared frequency scans of IBR [7] and synchronous generator (SG) [8] impedances, there is limited work addressing the differences between results obtained from EMT and RMS models. This paper presents a systematic comparison of the frequency scans of a SG, a grid-following inverter (GFLI), and two variants of grid-forming inverters (GFMIs) with the same power rating, step-up transformer, and operating point using EMT and RMS models. We investigate the limitations of RMS models through frequency scans and identify which type of GFMI can be more accurately represented by an RMS model in the low-frequency range. Finally, we analyze the system impedance at the PCC and clarify how the individual generator and grid impedances shape the resulting frequency-dependent system impedance. These results offer a basis for a clearer interpretation of PCC frequency scans. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sec-tion II introduces the methodology and models used to com-pute the frequency scans of the different devices and the grid. Section III describes the test system and the main simulation results. Finally, conclusions and future work are presented in Section IV. II. M ETHODOLOGY

B. EMT vs. RMS Models 

Fig. 4 shows the frequency scans of Zdq (s) at the PCC of the SG, GFLI, CC-GFMI, and VC-GFMI in the three-bus system, obtained using EMT and RMS models. The first SV of Zdq (s) determines the maximum voltage deviation in re-sponse to a current perturbation, while the second SV captures the minimum deviation. Together, they define a frequency-dependent range of possible voltage changes resulting from a current perturbation in any direction of the dq frame. Therefore, when the first and second SVs are equal, the voltage deviation is independent of the current perturbation. From Fig. 4, we note that all EMT scans are similar at very low and very high frequencies due to the grid-impedance dominance. The RMS models can capture the low-frequency dynamics up to approximately 10 Hz , but at higher frequencies, the first and second SVs converge to the same values, thereby hiding potential high-frequency interactions. This result aligns with the recommendation in [1] that RMS simulation should not be used to model dynamics > 10 Hz .Analyzing the scans of the two GFMIs at low frequencies only, we observe that the RMS scan matches the EMT scan exactly for the VC-GFMI, but small deviations arise for the CC-GFMI. This suggests that typical RMS models used in commercial software more accurately represent the behavior of a VC-GFMI than that of a CC-GFMI. To further investigate the observed mismatch for the CC-GFMI case, we first include the inner voltage-control loops (RMS*) and, as a second step, the inner current-control loops (RMS**). The results are shown in Fig. 5 for the frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 30 Hz . Including the inner voltage-control loops significantly improves the accuracy of the RMS scan at low frequencies, whereas adding the inner current-control loops additionally has a negligible effect, and is, in any case, infeasible for RMS simulations [11]. This is expected since the EMT model of the CC-GFMI controls the terminal voltage u, while the RMS model controls the internal voltage 

v. By including the inner voltage-control loops in the RMS model, it also controls the terminal voltage u and therefore aligns with the EMT model.

IV. C ONCLUSION 

This work shows that RMS models in frequency scans cap-ture low-frequency dynamics up to 10 Hz , but they can mask potential high-frequency interactions between the generator and the grid. The typical RMS model of a GFMI captures the low-frequency behavior of a VC-GFMI. However, inaccuracies arise for a CC-GFMI even at low frequencies. The mismatch can be reduced by including the inner voltage-control loops into the RMS model of the CC-GFMI. Additionally, we show that the system impedance is dominated by the lower of the grid and the generator impedances. The peaks observed in the frequency scan correspond to oscillatory modes of the system. However, certain oscillatory modes might be masked by zeros at the same frequency.
