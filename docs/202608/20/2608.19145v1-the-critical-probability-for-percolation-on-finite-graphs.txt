# The critical probability for percolation on finite graphs 

Micha Christoph ∗ Patryk Morawski † Yuval Wigderson ‡

Abstract 

We determine the critical probability for Bernoulli bond percolation on essentially any finite graph. Namely, letting λ(G) denote the spectral radius (maximum eigenvalue) of G, we prove that the critical probability is at 1 /λ (G): above this probability there is typically a component of order Ω( λ(G)), whereas below it all components are of order at most O(p|G|). These results in particular confirm a conjecture of Krivelevich and Samotij about percolation on graphs of a given average degree, and vastly extend theorems of Bollob´ as, Borgs, Chayes, and Riordan, who proved analogous results but only for dense graphs. Our theorems are optimal in many regimes, and also demonstrate that percolation has an unexpectedly subtle behaviour on graphs whose spectral radius is roughly the square root of their maximum degree. 

# 1 Introduction 

Let G be a graph, let p ∈ [0 , 1] and consider a random subgraph Gp of G obtained by retaining each edge of G independently with probability p. This simple definition gives rise to the vast field of (bond) percolation theory, which studies the typical structure of the random graph Gp. The study of percolation was initiated by Broadbent and Hammersley [6] in 1957, and originally focused primarily on infinite lattice graphs such as Zd. The field was later dramatically transformed by the seminal paper of Benjamini and Schramm [4], who proposed studying a much more general model, in which G is any infinite transitive graph. This more general perspective has propelled the field for the past few decades, and has led to a huge number of advances and breakthroughs (e.g. [4, 9, 16, 20, 25]); we refer to [31, Chapter 7] for an overview of some of these developments. A large part of percolation theory is dedicated to understanding the size and structure of the connected components of Gp. For example, a fundamental observation is that if G is any infinite connected graph then we can observe the following phase transition at some critical probability 

pc(G) — if p < p c(G) then almost surely Gp does not contain an infinite component, whereas if p > p c(G) then almost surely Gp contains such an infinite component 1. Many of the central questions of percolation theory revolve around understanding the value of pc(G) (as a function of 

G) and the typical structure of Gp for p close to pc(G). At nearly the same time that Broadbent and Hammersley were introducing percolation, essen-tially the same concept was introduced to a different community under a different name. In 1959,

Qd, whose vertex set is {0, 1}d, and in which two vectors are adjacent if they differ in exactly one coordinate. They proved that Qd also undergoes a phase transition around pc(Qd) = 1 /d . Namely, if p < (1 −ε)/d , then a.a.s. every component of ( Qd)p is of order Oε(d), whereas if p > (1+ ε)/d , then a.a.s. ( Qd)p contains a component of order Ω ε(n), where n = |Qd| = 2 d. The technique introduced by Ajtai, Koml´ os and Szemer´ edi, combining a local analysis based on branching processes with a global argument using the expansion properties of Qd, has further proved extremely influential. Given the two positive examples, it is natural to ask whether one can show a similar phenomenon for all graphs G, or at least for d-regular graphs, noting that both Kn and Qd are regular. The answer is a quick no — for example, if we take G to be a disjoint union of many copies of Kd+1 ,then clearly we will never see the supercritical phase with a component of size Ω( |G|) in Gp. There are two solutions to this apparently serious problem. The first, and most well-studied, one is to impose some global “robust connectivity” properties on G (as used for example by Ajtai, Koml´ os and Szemer´ edi in the study of Qd), which ensure that there is a supercritical phase. The property one naturally imposes is some variant of expansion, expressed either as a spectral condition [24], a combinatorial isoperimetry condition [2, 12, 15], or a structural condition which roughly implies one of the former [13, 14, 17, 18, 19]. An alternative approach, if we really want to consider all graphs, is to weaken our expectation for a phase transition. Namely, the above example suggests it may be more natural to call a component “giant” when its size is on the order of d, rather than n, which is the approach we want to take in this paper. In this direction, Krivelevich and Sudakov [29, Theorem 4] proved the supercritical statement for regular graphs; if G is a d-regular graph and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d , then a.a.s. 

Gp contains a connected component of order Ω ε(d). To complement this with a subcritical statement, one might want to say that if p ⩽ (1 − ε)/d ,then a.a.s. every component of Gp has order o(d). Unfortunately, the same example of a disjoint union of many cliques shows that this cannot hold in general. If d and 0 < p ⩽ (1 − ε)/d are fixed, and the number of copies of Kd+1 is taken sufficiently large, then with overwhelming probability there will be some Kd+1 all of whose edges survive into Gp. In particular, Gp will have a component of size d + 1. 1.1 Main results: percolation on general finite graphs 

Our goal in the present paper is to extend the picture from Theorem 1.1 to arbitrary, not necessarily regular, finite graphs. Our first main result yields the same supercritical behaviour for graphs of average degree d.

Theorem 1.2. For every ε > 0 there exists γ > 0 such that the following holds. If G is a graph of average degree d(G) > 0 and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d (G), then 

Pr[ Gp has a component of order at least γd (G)] = 1 − o(1) ,

where o(1) tends to 0 as |G| → ∞ .

Krivelevich and Samotij [28] observed that taking p ⩾ (c + ε)/d (G) for an explicit constant c ≈ 1.6yields the same conclusion, and conjectured that the constant could be improved all the way to 1. This question was later reiterated in [15], and the theorem above answers it affirmatively. The next natural question to ask is whether 1 /d (G) is a critical probability for any finite graph 

G, as 1 /d is for all d-regular graphs. Our next result shows that the answer is no: the true value of pc(G) is smaller than 1 /d (G) essentially whenever G is “far from regular”. In order to state it, we need one more piece of terminology. Given a graph G, we denote by λ(G) its spectral radius , that is, the largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix AG. This can be thought of as a certain “smoothed” version of the average degree of G, which takes into account both the degree sequence and the geometry of G. For example, it is a standard exercise in spectral graph theory that λ(G) ⩾ d(G), with equality if and only if G is regular. Our main theorem states that, informally speaking, 1 /λ (G) is the critical probability for any finite graph G, although its conclusions are slightly weaker than those in Theorem 1.1 for regular graphs. We remark, however, that as we will shortly discuss, this reflects the reality and is not simply an artefact of our methods. 

Theorem 1.3. For every ε, δ > 0 there exist γ, L > 0 such that the following holds for any finite graph G.

I (Supercritical phase) . If p ⩾ (1 + ε)/λ (G) then 

Pr[ Gp has a component of order at least γλ (G)] ⩾ 1 − δ. 

II (Subcritical phase) . If p ⩽ (1 − ε)/λ (G) then 

Pr[ Gp has a component of order at least Lp|G|] ⩽ δ.

1.3 Proof outline 

To outline the general idea of our proofs, let us focus on the supercritical part of Theorem 1.4. That is, we fix ε > 0, let G be a graph and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/λ (G). The general strategy of our proof is as follows. First, we fix a vertex v and we would like to understand how the size of the connected component Cv of Gp containing v behaves. In particular, we would like to show that with constant probability, say c, this connected component has size at least γλ (G). Moreover, if this is not the case, then we would like to look at the graph Gp with this component removed and repeat the argument there. After repeating this k = ω(1) times, assuming that the remaining graph still behaves supercritically, we would get that the probability that Gp

does not contain a large connected component is at most (1 − c)k = o(1). To understand how the size of Cv behaves, let us first sketch the argument of Krivelevich and Sudakov [29] that works when G is d-regular, in which case λ(G) = d. To that end, let Y1, . . . , Y e(G)

5be independent random variables, each taking value 1 with probability p and 0 otherwise. We want to reveal Cv one edge at a time using the following random process taking these variables as an input. We will start with Cv = {v} and in each step t we will consider a new edge e ∈ E(G)connecting Cv to the rest of the graph. If Yt = 1, then this edge is present in Gp and we will add the corresponding endpoint to Cv. Otherwise, this edge is not present, and we proceed to the next edge. The process stops when there are no more unrevealed edges connecting Cv to the rest of the graph. Now suppose that |C v| = C < γd and so the process stops at some step t. Then, among 

Y1, . . . , Y t we have seen exactly C − 1 “yeses”, as each “yes” increases the order of Cv by exactly one. On the other hand, we must have seen a “no” for each edge of G that goes from Cv to the rest of G. As G is d-regular, there are at least C · (d − C) ⩾ (1 − γ)Cd such edges, and hence 

t ⩾ (1 − γ)Cd . Thus, at most a 1 /((1 − γ)d)-fraction of the random variables Y1, . . . , Y t are “yes”. However, if γ is sufficiently small compared to ε, a Chernoff bound shows that this is rather unlikely to happen in our sequence Y1, . . . , Y t, since p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d . In fact, by analysing these probabilities one can show that with constant probability Cv has size at least γd — and that if not, then a.a.s. 

|C v| ≪ log d, say. We now want to prove Theorem 1.2, i.e., if a graph G has average degree d and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d ,then a.a.s. Gp contains a component of size Ω( d). We split the proof into two regimes. Firstly, if

# 5 Concluding remarks 

In this paper, we determined that, under the definition explained in the introduction, 1 /λ (G) is the critical probability for all finite graphs G. In particular, we show that for any finite graph G, if 

p ⩾ (1 + ε)/λ (G), then with probability arbitrarily close to 1 the graph Gp contains a component of size Ω( λ(G)). As the examples of Ks,t and a disjoint union of cliques illustrate, the statement is the best one can hope to get for an arbitrary graph G.A natural question one can still ask is under what additional assumptions on the graph G we get a component of size Ω( |G|) in Gp. This question was addressed in the work of Chung, Horn and Lu [11], yet the conditions they put on the graph G are rather restrictive. Moreover, the way they measure the size of a component is not by the number of vertices in it, but by a function of the degree sequence, which makes it hard to translate their statements to our setting. 

Question 5.1. What natural conditions on G imply that if p ⩾ (1 + ε)/λ (G) then with constant probability Gp contains a component of size Ω( |G|)?

The main difficulty in finding such a condition is that λ(G) can be governed by a very small part of the graph. In particular, G might contain an induced subgraph H on (1 − o(1)) |G| vertices such that λ(H) ≪ λ(G), and in particular Hp will behave subcritically even if p = (1 + ε)/λ (G). Thus, simply assuming some notion of “robust connectivity” would probably not be sufficient, as it will not see such a “localization of the spectral radius”. Turning to the subcritical regime, Theorem 1.4 shows that if p ⩽ (1 − ε)/λ (G) then a.a.s. the largest component of Gp will be of size at most O(p|G|∆( G) log |G|/λ (G)). By taking G = Ks,t one can see that this bound is optimal if, roughly speaking, λ(G) ≫ p|G|. For example, if G = Ks,s 3 ,19 for which we have ∆( G) = s3 and λ(G) = s2, then one can show that for any fixed ε as s → ∞ and 

p = (1 − ε)/λ (G) a.a.s. Gp will contain a connected component of size Ω( s log s). 6

However, the bound of O(p|G|∆( G) log |G|/λ (G)) is not tight in all regimes of ∆( G) and λ(G). Theorem 1.3 shows that if λ(G) ≈ p∆( G), then the log |G|-factor is not necessary. If λ(G) is a constant and |G| → ∞ , then a similar argument to the one used to prove Theorem 1.3, but counting paths of length at least Ω(log |G|), shows that if p ⩽ (1 − ε)/λ (G) then a.a.s. the largest component of Gp will have size at most |G|1/2−δ, for some δ > 0 depending on ε and λ(G). 7 Finally, if 

λ(G) = ∆( G) and G is connected, then G is λ(G)-regular, and in particular we get a O(log |G|)bound on the size of the largest connected component in Gp.It would be interesting to know the correct upper bound for all regimes of ∆( G) and λ(G). 

Question 5.2. For a graph with given ∆( G) and λ(G), what is the tight upper bound for the typical size of the largest component of Gp, when p ⩽ (1 − ε)/λ (G)?

An interesting special case of Question 5.2 would be to consider graphs G which are “close to regular”, in the sense that ∆( G) ⩽ (1 + δ)λ(G), for some small δ, possibly being a function of |G|.Finally, it would be interesting to say more about the structure of the large components in the supercritical regime. In the binomial random graph G(n, p ), it is well-known that when p ⩾

(1 + ε)/n , the giant component not only has Ω( n) vertices, but in fact has a path and a cycle of length Ω( n) a.a.s. The technique of Krivelevich and Sudakov [29], which helped inspire our work, implies that if G is any d-regular graph and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d , then a.a.s. Gp has a path of length Ω( d), and Krivelevich–Samotij [28] showed that under the same assumptions one obtains a cycle of length Ω( d). We reiterate a conjecture of Krivelevich and Samotij [28] (see also [15]), stating that such a result should still be true if we only assume that the average degree is d.

Conjecture 5.3 ([15, 28]) . For every ε > 0 there exist d0, γ > 0 such that the following holds. If 

G is a graph of average degree d ⩾ d0 and p ⩾ (1 + ε)/d , then Gp contains a cycle of length at least 

γd a.a.s. as |G| → ∞ .

We remark that in [10], it is proved that if p ⩾ C/d , then Gp a.a.s. contains a cycle of length at least (1 − γ)d, where γ → 0 as C → ∞ . It is possible that by combining the techniques of the present paper with those of [10], one could resolve Conjecture 5.3.
