## What to Preserve, Where to Adapt: A Depth-Wise Analysis of Forgetting in Continual Gynecological Image Segmentation

Abstract 

Medical image segmentation models are typ-ically trained under the assumption that all data are available simultaneously. However, in clinical practice, datasets often arrive sequen-tially, requiring models to adapt continuously to evolving data distributions. We study this problem in gynecological image segmentation, where substantial heterogeneity across imaging modalities, anatomical structures, and annota-tion protocols creates a particularly challenging continual learning setting. Under these large distribution shifts, existing continual learning methods struggle to preserve previously learned knowledge, leading to catastrophic forgetting. To better understand forgetting in this set-ting, we investigate how different encoder– decoder regions influence segmentation perfor-mance and forgetting during continual gyneco-logical segmentation. Through block-wise ab-lation analysis, we observe that ablating early encoder and late decoder regions results in the largest performance degradation, indicat-ing that segmentation performance depends un-evenly across the network hierarchy. Using controlled adaptation experiments, we further show that forgetting remains limited when up-dates are restricted to bottleneck-adjacent re-gions, but increases sharply once shallower en-coders and decoders become trainable, even when only a small subset of parameters is up-dated. These findings suggest that forgetting in the encoder-decoder architecture is strongly in-fluenced by where updates occur across network depth during continual learning. Full code and analysis pipelines will be made publicly avail-able upon acceptance. 

Keywords: Continual Learning, Medical Image Segmentation, Catastrophic Forgetting, Encoder–Decoder Networks, Gynecological Imaging 

1. Introduction 

Deep learning has enabled major advances in medical image segmentation, with encoder–decoder architec-tures such as U-Net Ronneberger et al. (2015) and its variants Hu et al. (2018); Gao et al. (2024); Isensee et al. (2021, 2024) forming the foundation of most modern systems. These models are typically trained under the assumption that all training data are avail-able simultaneously. In clinical practice, however, medical datasets often arrive incrementally across in-stitutions, modalities, and patient populations, lead-ing to substantial distribution shifts over time Qazi et al. (2024). Continual learning is particularly challenging in gynecological image segmentation because clinical workflows rely on multiple imaging modalities, in-cluding ultrasound, MRI, and PET/CT, depending on the pathology and diagnostic objective Kinkel et al. (2006); Sala et al. (2013). These sequen-tially acquired datasets differ in segmentation tar-gets, anatomical variability, and annotation com-pleteness Liang et al. (2025); Quesada et al. (2023). Hence, sequential adaptation degrades performance on previously learned tasks, leading to catastrophic forgetting McCloskey and Cohen (1989); Qazi et al. (2024); Yuan and Zhao (2024). Existing continual learning methods typically attempt to mitigate for-getting either by revisiting previous data Zenke et al. (2017); Lopez-Paz and Ranzato (2017); Chaudhry et al. (2018), which is often impractical in clini-cal settings due to privacy and storage constraints,

or by constraining parameter updates during new-task learning Kirkpatrick et al. (2017); Li and Hoiem (2017). Yet these approaches often struggle to pre-serve prior knowledge across diverse gynecological imaging tasks and do not explicitly examine how for-getting varies across encoder–decoder depth. Encoder–decoder segmentation networks are orga-nized hierarchically, with different regions responsi-ble for distinct aspects of representation learning and segmentation reconstruction. In U-Net Ronneberger et al. (2015), shallow encoder layers capture low-level spatial information, deeper layers encode increasingly abstract representations, and late decoder layers di-rectly influence the final segmentation output. Re-cent pruning studies suggest that these regions do not contribute equally to segmentation performance, with some regions being more important than others Yang et al. (2025); Hassler et al. (2025). Furthermore, studies of continual learning in image classification have shown that forgetting is associated with uneven representational changes across network depth Ra-masesh et al. (2020); Mirzadeh et al. (2022); Zhang et al. (2022). These observations motivate investi-gating how continual adaptation interacts with the hierarchical structure of encoder–decoder segmenta-tion networks. In this work, we investigate how adaptation and forgetting vary across encoder–decoder depth during continual gynecological segmentation. We combine block-wise ablation analysis with controlled depth-constrained adaptation experiments to study the re-lationship between network depth, segmentation per-formance, and forgetting. Our analysis reveals a strong dependence of both segmentation performance and knowledge retention on the specific encoder– decoder regions involved during continual adaptation. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We demonstrate that existing continual learn-ing methods struggle under the large distribu-tion shifts present in continual gynecological im-age segmentation. 

• We identify early encoder and late decoder re-gions as the most performance-critical stages of the encoder–decoder network through block-wise ablation analysis. 

• We introduce a depth-constrained adaptation framework for continual segmentation and show that forgetting remains limited when adaptation is confined to bottleneck-adjacent regions, but increases sharply as updates extend toward shal-lower encoder and decoder stages. 

2. Related Work 

Recent work has introduced deep learning meth-ods and public datasets for gynecological image seg-mentation across diverse clinical settings Pan et al. (2024); Liang et al. (2025); Tang et al. (2024). These studies demonstrate the effectiveness of encoder– decoder architectures such as U-Net Ronneberger et al. (2015) and nnU-Net Isensee et al. (2021) for task-specific segmentation under static training con-ditions. However, existing approaches typically as-sume that all datasets are available simultaneously and do not investigate sequential adaptation across heterogeneous gynecological imaging tasks. Contin-ual learning methods aim to address this limitation by enabling sequential adaptation while preserving previously learned knowledge. Existing continual learning approaches are commonly categorized into regularization-based, distillation-based, and replay-based methods Qazi et al. (2024). Regularization methods such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) Kirkpatrick et al. (2017) constrain parameter updates according to their estimated importance to previous tasks, while distillation-based approaches such as Learning with-out Forgetting (LwF) Li and Hoiem (2017) preserve prior knowledge through output- or feature-level consistency constraints. Replay-based methods mitigate forgetting by storing or generating samples from earlier tasks Lopez-Paz and Ranzato (2017); Lemke et al. (2024). However, these approaches often require retaining or regenerating prior patient data, which is impractical in clinical settings due to privacy, storage, and data governance constraints. Although regularization and distillation approaches have demonstrated effectiveness on standard benchmarks, they often struggle to preserve prior knowledge under the distribution shifts encountered in gynecological imaging. For instance, methods such as EWC estimate which weights should change less during adaptation, but do not distinguish between updates applied to early encoder layers, bottleneck representations, or late decoder layers Qazi et al. (2024); Yuan and Zhao (2024). Recent work has begun to analyze forgetting from a representational perspective. Ramasesh et al. Ra-masesh et al. (2020) show that forgetting is not uniformly distributed across network depth, with

deeper layers undergoing substantially larger repre-sentational changes during sequential training while earlier layers remain relatively stable. Studies on layer-wise behavior have shown that deep networks exhibit heterogeneous structure, where some layers are critical to performance while others are more robust to perturbations Zhang et al. (2022). How-ever, such studies primarily analyze representational drift during sequential learning rather than systemat-ically controlling which network regions are updated during adaptation. Moreover, prior analyses have focused predominantly on image classification mod-els and do not directly address encoder–decoder seg-mentation architectures operating under the substan-tial modality and annotation heterogeneity present in gynecological imaging. In parallel, work on model compression and pruning has revealed considerable structural redundancy in encoder–decoder segmenta-tion networks. Studies on nnU-Net demonstrate that large portions of the network can be removed or sim-plified with minimal performance degradation Yang et al. (2025); Hassler et al. (2025). These findings suggest that segmentation performance is not equally distributed across network depth, raising the possibil-ity that adaptation at different depths may influence forgetting differently during continual learning. 

3. Method 

After evaluating existing continual learning meth-ods in our gynecological segmentation setting, we investigate how forgetting varies across different re-gions of the encoder–decoder architecture through two complementary analyses: (1) block-wise abla-tion analysis to measure the contribution of differ-ent encoder–decoder regions to segmentation perfor-mance, and (2) depth-constrained adaptation exper-iments in which parameter updates are restricted to specific encoder and decoder regions during sequen-tial training. We consider a task-incremental segmentation set-ting in which each task corresponds to a distinct seg-mentation dataset with its own label space. We adopt a multi-head formulation with a shared encoder– decoder backbone and task-specific segmentation heads. We partition the U-Net architecture into depth-wise encoder and decoder blocks correspond-ing to different spatial resolution levels to analyze how segmentation performance depends on network depth. Encoder blocks are indexed from shallow to deep as 

E = {E0, E 1, . . . , E n},

where E0 denotes the shallowest encoder block and 

En the deepest encoder block adjacent to the bottle-neck. Decoder blocks are indexed from the bottleneck toward the output as 

D = {Dn, D n−1, . . . , D 0},

where Dn is closest to the bottleneck, and D0 is the final output-proximal decoder block. Each encoder block Ei corresponds to a decoder block Di operating at the same spatial resolution level, as illustrated in Figure 1. The bottleneck is treated as a separate intermediate region between the deepest encoder and decoder. After training on each task in the sequential con-tinual learning sequence, encoder and decoder blocks are ablated independently during inference, while the remainder of the network remains unchanged. For a selected block, output activations are zeroed be-fore propagation to subsequent layers, and the result-ing change in segmentation performance is measured. For a given block b, the performance drop induced by ablation is defined as ∆b = Dfull − Db,

where Dfull denotes the Dice score of the unmodified network and Db denotes the Dice score after ablating block b. To compare block importance across tasks with different baseline performance levels, we report the relative block contribution 

δb = ∆b

Repeating this procedure across all encoder and decoder blocks provides a depth-wise characterization of how segmentation performance depends on different encoder–decoder regions in a continual learning setting. We then perform depth-constrained adaptation ex-periments to examine how restricting updates to dif-ferent encoder and decoder regions affects forgetting during sequential training. The network is parti-tioned into the same depth-wise blocks used in the ablation analysis, and selected subsets of blocks are frozen while the remaining regions remain trainable. 4. Experimental Setup 

4.1. Datasets 

We evaluate continual segmentation using three public gynecological imaging datasets: the Uterine Myoma MRI Dataset (UMD) Pan et al. (2024), the UTHealth Endometriosis MRI Dataset (UT-EndoMRI) Liang et al. (2025), and the Endometrial Cancer PET/CT Image Dataset for Semantic Seg-mentation and Detection of Hypermetabolic Regions 

(ECPC-IDS) Tang et al. (2024). These datasets dif-fer in imaging modality, anatomical targets, and an-notation structure, thereby introducing realistic dis-tribution shifts across tasks. For single-modality datasets, the modality is duplicated across both channels, while ECPC-IDS uses PET and CT as separate input channels. We formulate each dataset as a separate task in a sequential learning setting using the fixed task order UMD → ECPC-IDS → UT-EndoMRI .

This ordering introduces challenging shifts in both input distribution and segmentation structure, pro-gressing from a relatively consistent, fully annotated task to increasingly heterogeneous clinical settings with greater modality variation, partial annotations, and substantial anatomical variability. 

4.2. Implementation Details 

All experiments are conducted using the nnU-Net framework Isensee et al. (2021), its continual learning extension Lifelong nnU-Net Gonz´ alez et al. (2023), and the MultiTalent nnU-Net framework Ulrich et al. (2023) for joint multi-dataset training. These frame-works provide a standardized implementation for both sequential and joint training settings. Un-less otherwise stated, all models use the default 3D full-resolution configuration and are optimized us-ing stochastic gradient descent with a combined Dice and cross-entropy loss. During training, only data from the current task are accessible, with previous datasets revisited only in replay-based methods. Un-less otherwise stated, conventional continual learning baselines are evaluated using the full three-task se-quence. In contrast, the controlled depth-constrained adaptation experiments use a two-task setting com-prising UMD and ECPC-IDS to isolate the effect of adaptation depth without interference from sub-sequent tasks. Architectural design, preprocessing, optimizer settings, and training schedules are kept identical across all methods to ensure fair compar-ison, with differences arising only from the contin-ual learning strategy. Each task is trained for 100 epochs in both single-task and continual learning set-tings. For joint multi-dataset training using Multi-Talent nnU-Net Ulrich et al. (2023), training is ex-tended to 300 epochs to account for the increased data volume. All experiments are performed on an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU with 24GB of mem-ory. 4.3. Evaluation Metrics 

Segmentation performance is evaluated using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC). To quantify forget-ting, we report the average performance degradation across the continual learning sequence on previously learned tasks. Lower forgetting values indicate bet-ter knowledge retention, while negative values cor-respond to positive backward transfer. Quantitative results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across samples in the evaluated dataset.

5.2. Block-Wise Ablation Analysis Across Encoder–Decoder Depth 

Figure 4 and Table 4 show how ablating encoder and decoder blocks at different network depths affects seg-mentation performance across the continual training sequence. After training on UMD, removing the shal-low encoder blocks ( E0–E2) almost completely col-lapses segmentation performance ( δb ≥ 0.99), indi-cating that the model relies heavily on these regions for accurate prediction. Similarly, ablating the final decoder stages ( D0–D2) produces maximal degrada-tion ( δb = 1 .00). In contrast, ablating deeper encoder stages and the bottleneck produces comparatively small performance changes, with the bottleneck con-tribution remaining close to zero across all tasks. Fol-lowing adaptation from UMD to ECPC-IDS, shallow encoder and late decoder regions remain dominant contributors ( E0 = 1 .00, D0 = 1 .00), whereas deeper encoder stages contribute negligibly to segmentation performance ( E3–E5 ≈ 0), indicating that their re-moval has little measurable impact on performance. A similar concentration pattern re-emerges after sub-sequent adaptation to UT-EndoMRI, motivating the following experiments in which adaptation is directly restricted to specific encoder–decoder depths.

6. Conclusion and Future Work 

This work investigated continual gynecological im-age segmentation under shifts in modality, anatomy, and annotation structure. Existing continual learn-ing methods showed limited ability to preserve previ-ously learned tasks in this challenging clinical set-ting, motivating a deeper analysis of how forget-ting varies across encoder–decoder depth during se-quential adaptation. Through block-wise ablation experiments, we showed that segmentation perfor-mance depends disproportionately on early encoder and late decoder regions, whereas deeper bottleneck-adjacent stages contribute comparatively little. Us-ing depth-constrained adaptation experiments, we 

10 What to Preserve, Where to Adapt? 

further showed that restricting updates near the bot-tleneck largely preserves previously learned tasks, while extending adaptation toward shallower en-coder and decoder stages leads to abrupt forgetting, even when only a small subset of parameters re-mains trainable. Post-adaptation analyses further re-vealed that different adaptation constraints lead to distinct depth-wise importance profiles, highlighting that adaptation can modify how segmentation perfor-mance depends on different encoder–decoder regions. These findings suggest that forgetting in continual gynecological segmentation is strongly influenced by where adaptation occurs within the encoder–decoder hierarchy. More broadly, they indicate that effec-tive continual learning may benefit from adaptation strategies that explicitly account for the uneven func-tional importance of different network regions. Fu-ture work may build on these observations to develop continual learning methods that selectively preserve retention-critical representations while enabling effi-cient adaptation to new clinical tasks and distribu-tions.

A.1. Dataset Statistics 

Figure 9 summarizes the dataset-level annotation and preprocessing statistics used in our experiments. These statistics provide additional context for the observed variation in segmentation performance and forgetting across tasks. 

A.2. UT-EndoMRI Sequence Selection 

The UT-EndoMRI dataset contains four MRI se-quences: T1, T1FS, T2, and T2FS. However, se-quence availability varies substantially across pa-tients after preprocessing and quality control. T1FS and T2 are the most consistently available sequences and therefore provide the largest usable subset of cases for experimentation. Table 7 compares single-dataset nnU-Net performance across different se-quence configurations. T1FS achieves the best per-formance on UT and EM, the primary anatomical targets considered in the continual learning analy-sis. Although T2 performs slightly better for OV and CY, segmentation performance for these struc-tures remains substantially lower overall, likely due to anatomical variability and limited annotation avail-ability. Based on these results and the greater avail-ability of T1FS samples, we use the T1FS configura-tion in subsequent experiments.
