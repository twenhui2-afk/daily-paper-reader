(Dated: August 31, 2026) Nanoelectromechanical actuators operating at sub-100-nm gaps collapse through a pull-in insta-bility set by competing electrostatic and Casimir forces. The quasi-static fold that bounds their safe operating range has been known in closed form for three decades, together with the Casimir ceiling above which no static equilibrium survives, which fixes the smallest gap a given stiffness and area can hold open against the quantum vacuum. That fold does not give the threshold reached from rest, its dependence on damping, or the design sensitivities of either. We train a physics-informed neural network in a rapidity coordinate that maps the movable pull-in pole to infinity, which keeps the residual bounded across the collapse threshold where fixed-step Runge–Kutta integration steps into unphysical states. Differentiating the trained surrogate returns pull-in-voltage sensitivities that match the closed-form fold to a relative error of 3 × 10 −6 and inverts a device specification to a gap of 97 .036 nm at a target actuation voltage. Applied to the from-rest boundary, which carries no closed form once the damping is finite, it supplies the same sensitivities where no analytic root exists. We prove that this boundary is bracketed by two closed-form curves, that it is nondecreasing in the damping ratio, that it merges with the fold once the damping ratio exceeds 2 −1/4, and that the gap closes as ( τ∗ − τ )2/5. Numerically the merger already occurs at 0 .396, and the growth of the collapse time changes there from logarithmic to inverse square root. The classical-limit bound on the thermal Lifshitz derating is at the percent level, and the physical shift at these gaps lies orders of magnitude below it. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Electrostatically actuated nanoelectromechanical sys-tems (NEMS) and their larger microelectromechanical (MEMS) counterparts convert an applied voltage into controlled mechanical displacement, and they underpin capacitive switches, resonant sensors, and tunable-gap actuators. In every such device a movable electrode is driven across a narrow vacuum gap toward a fixed elec-trode, and the useful travel range is bounded by an elec-tromechanical collapse known as pull-in: beyond a crit-ical bias the elastic restoring force can no longer bal-ance the attractive load, the electrode snaps to con-tact, and the device may stick. At gaps below 100 nm two attractive loads compete. The electrostatic pres-sure of a parallel-plate capacitor scales as ( d − x)−2

with the instantaneous gap d − x, while the quantum-vacuum Casimir force [1] scales as ( d−x)−4 and therefore dominates as the gap closes. Both are softening: their gradients reinforce the displacement rather than oppose it. Precision measurements of the Casimir interaction in MEMS torsional oscillators [2, 3] and the review of

All three require the trajectory, and the trajectory is what the movable pole obstructs. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) offer amesh-free route to this problem. A PINN represents the solution of an ordinary differential equation (ODE) by a neural network and trains it by minimizing a loss that embeds the ODE residual together with the initial and boundary constraints [17–21], so that the governing physics is enforced directly rather than through a dis-cretized time step. This formulation is attractive here because a fixed-step integrator such as the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4) becomes stiff and loses ac-curacy as the trajectory nears the movable pole, where the right-hand side grows without bound. A network whose loss is aware of the singularity can remain accu-rate in the same regime. The pull-in barrier has a structural counterpart in rel-ativistic dynamics. As ξ → 1 the dimensionless gap 1 − ξ plays the role of the factor 1 − v2/c 2 for a charged particle accelerated in a laser field, where the velocity approaches the light cone v → c [22]. This correspon-dence, which rests on the rapidity description of rela-tivistic particle motion [23], motivates the regularization used here. For the relativistic problem we have trained a physics-informed network on the same kinematics with the Lorentz invariant imposed as a soft penalty [24]; the present work instead builds the rapidity into the parametrization, so that the movable pole is removed rather than penalized. The applied payoff is concrete, and it lives in the de-sign and methodology layer over pull-in physics that is al-ready well characterized. Locating the fold across ( α, β )yields the pull-in voltage as a design curve and quantifies how the always-on Casimir load limits miniaturization at a fixed stiffness and area. From this chart we assem-ble a differentiable design pipeline for Casimir-limited NEMS actuation: the Casimir ceiling β∗ sets a floor on the achievable gap, automatic differentiation through the trained surrogate returns exact design sensitivities, and gradient descent through the same object inverts a device specification. Two features carry the contribu-tion. The surrogate is singularity-aware: parametrized in a rapidity coordinate, it stays well posed on a pole-free domain in the near-collapse regime where fixed-step integration steps into unphysical states, an advantage that does not depend on whether a closed form exists. And because the static fold has a closed form, we use it as a ground-truth oracle that checks every link of the pipeline, a strength rather than a limitation. Once finite-temperature Lifshitz corrections are included, the chart 3also bounds a temperature dependence of the stability threshold. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Sec. II sets out the physical model, its dimen-sionless form, the thermal correction, and the relativistic analogy; Sec. III describes the PINN methodology and its verification against RK4; Sec. IV presents the trajec-tories, phase diagram, and thermal boundary shift; and Sec. V summarizes the findings.  

> II. PHYSICAL MODEL

We model the actuator as a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) lumped oscillator [25]: a movable electrode of effective mass m, linear stiffness k, and mechanical damp-ing γd, displaced by x toward a fixed electrode across an initial vacuum gap d. The two electrodes are treated as parallel plates of area A, the standard approximation when the gap is small compared with the lateral size.

On the right, the elec-trostatic term is the parallel-plate capacitor attraction under an applied bias V0, with ε0 the vacuum permittiv-ity, and the Casimir term is the pressure between ideal conductors, π2ℏc/ [240 ( d − x)4], integrated over the plate area [1, 26]. Both loads are attractive and diverge as the gap d − x closes. The material and geometry dependence of the Casimir pressure, neglected in the ideal-conductor form used here, is reviewed in Refs. [4, 5, 27]. Two modeling choices enter the force model. First, the analysis assumes the parallel-plate (Decca-type) configu-ration [3]. Sphere–plate experiments [2] carry a different force exponent and are treated through the proximity-force approximation, which would rescale the geometric prefactor. Second, the ideal-conductor Casimir pressure used here overestimates the real gold force at a 100 nm gap by close to a factor of two: the finite-conductivity re-duction factor at that separation is ηF = 0 .48 [28], while surface roughness accounts for about 1% [4, 27]. Con-sequently β, VPI , and the thermal shift computed below are ideal-conductor upper bounds. Substituting realis-tic optical data for gold would reduce β and rescale all dependent predictions accordingly.

> III. PHYSICS-INFORMED NEURAL-NETWORK METHODOLOGY

We approximate the single-trajectory solution ξ(τ ) of the pull-in oscillator with a physics-informed neural net-work (PINN), a parametric surrogate whose parameters we fit by minimizing the governing ordinary differen-tial equation (ODE) residual, evaluated through the net-work’s own automatic derivatives rather than through la-beled solution data [17]. The surrogate is smooth, mesh-free, and analytically differentiable. Trained in the rapid-ity coordinate of Sec. III C, it represents the approach to pull-in on a pole-free domain. 

Definition 1 (Collocation residual; movable pole) 

The collocation residual is the pointwise value of the governing ODE operator applied to the network output at a sampled collocation point. The mean square of this residual over the sampled points is the physics loss. A movable pole is a singularity of the solution whose location depends on the initial data and parameters, as opposed to a fixed singularity of the ODE coefficients.  

> A. Architecture

The map τ 7 → ξ(τ ) is a fully connected multilayer per-ceptron (MLP) with four hidden layers of 64 units each, tanh activation, and Xavier initialization. The scalar time input is affinely rescaled to [ −1, 1] over the train-ing window and, when needed, augmented with Fourier features {sin( kωτ ), cos( kωτ )}, with ω the dimensionless natural frequency. We train in double precision. The net-works are small enough that this carries negligible cost while easing the ∼ 10 −3 accuracy target. The initial conditions are imposed by construction, through a hard ansatz. Writing g(τ ) = 1 − e−τ , so that 

g(0) = 0 and ˙ g(0) = 1, we set 

ξNN (τ ) = ξ0 + v0 g(τ ) + g(τ )2 N (τ ; Θ) , (8) with N the raw network of weights Θ. Then ξNN (0) = ξ0

and ˙ξNN (0) = v0 hold by construction, so the initial-condition loss can be dropped. We optimize with Adam for warm-up and refine with L-BFGS. All trainings are CPU-only and deterministic from a fixed seed (42). The complete per-regime protocol, including collocation 5counts, optimizer schedules, and wall-clock times, is given in the Supplemental Material [33], Sec. S1. Plain tanh networks proved adequate for short and moderate windows, but the long-horizon stable regime (T = 60) floored near E∞ ≈ 6.5 × 10 −3: a depth-limited tanh MLP spends its capacity resolving many oscillation periods. Adding two Fourier input modes tuned to the damped natural frequency ωd = p1 − ζ2 ≈ 0.8 (not the undamped ω = 1), together with a longer L-BFGS pol-ish, restored the target accuracy ∼ 10 −3. The input en-coding, not raw depth, sets the attainable error on long trajectories.

τ∗ ≈ 2.6226) without overshoot or non-finite values: an error-controlled solver refines its step where the field steepens and does not blow up. Against this reference the PINN attains supremum-norm ( E∞) displacement errors of 1 .05 × 10 −3 (stable), 1.95 × 10 −3 (growing), and 2 .83 × 10 −3 (pull-in, rapidity coordinate). > IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

All dimensionless quantities refer to Eq. (2) and the control parameters ( α, β, ζ ) of Table I.  

> A. Time trajectories and the three dynamical regimes

Figure 1 shows the displacement ξ(τ ) released from rest for three operating points that span the qualitative behavior of Eq. (2). A stable point ( α = 0 .05, β = 10 −4,

ζ = 0 .6) executes a damped approach and settles onto the stable node at ξ ≈ 0.056, overshooting before the viscous term removes the excess energy. An intermediate point (α = 0 .0724, β = 0 .0407, ζ = 1 .0) climbs monotonically to a larger equilibrium near ξ ≈ 0.29 without collapsing: the bias sits below the fold, but the Casimir pre-load pushes the fixed point well past the small-displacement range. A supercritical point ( α = 0.20, β = 0.10, 

ζ = 0 .5) lies above the fold and collapses at a finite time. The collapse is abrupt rather than gradual: the last stretch ξ : 0 .74 → 1 occupies only the final ∼ 2% of the trajectory. The movable pole fixes the rate, 1 − ξ =(25 β/ 6) 1/5(τ∗ − τ )2/5 to leading order (Theorem 4), so the velocity diverges as ( τ∗ − τ )−3/5. Against the adap-tive DOP853 reference the physics-informed neural net-work (PINN) reproduces all three trajectories to maxi-mum pointwise errors below 3 × 10 −3, the pull-in case in the rapidity coordinate of Eq. > B. Pull-in phase diagram

Figure 2 maps the (α, β ) plane at fixed damping 

ζ = 0 .1. The color scale gives the dynamic pull-in time 

τPI (α, β ) obtained by integrating Eq. (2) from rest until the gap closes; the solid curve is the analytic static fold of Eq. (5). The grid, the integrator settings, the pull-in cri-terion, and the summary statistics quoted below are col-lected in Sec. S3 of the Supplemental Material [33]. The dynamic boundary separates initial conditions that pull in from those that settle, and it sits inside the static fold by a mean gap ∆ α ≈ 0.0117 in the electrostatic strength (median 0 .0121, maximum 0 .0141), so a device released from rest collapses at a bias below the quasi-static thresh-old. The excess is kinetic: a mass released above its final equilibrium overshoots and crosses the barrier that a slow voltage ramp would not reach. Raising the damping sup-presses the overshoot, monotonically in ζ (Corollary 1; for β = 0 see also Ref. [36]), and above a finite damping ratio the two boundaries coincide exactly, because the or-bit released from rest then has no speed left at the fold. That the two never coincide, asserted in Refs. [36, 37], does not hold; the point is settled in Appendix efsec:app-dyn. Theorem 3 proves this for ζ ≥ 2−1/4 at every β; the threshold itself is lower, ζc = 0 .3959 at β = 0, and the band closes quadratically in ζc − ζ. The offset measured at ζ = 0 .7, ∆ α = −6.6 × 10 −5, is one fifteenth of a grid cell and of the opposite sign, and is therefore sweep resolution about an exact zero. Of the sampled plane, a fraction 0 .73 pulls in dynamically against 0 .68 stati-cally, the difference being the overshoot band (5 .3% of cells). The fold endpoints reproduce the analytic values 

αc = 4 /27 ≈ 0.148 at β = 0 and β∗ = 256 /3125 ≈ 0.082 at α = 0 to within one grid cell. The pull-in time is largest immediately past threshold, up to τPI ≈ 15 .8against a floor of 2 .2 deep in the collapse region, but the mechanism at this damping is not the critical slow-ing down of a saddle-node. At ζ = 0 .1 the boundary lies strictly inside the fold, the equilibrium that the orbit passes is a hyperbolic saddle, and the transit time grows as −λ−1+ ln( α−αdyn ) rather than as ( α−αc)−1/2 (Propo-sition 2). Fitting the cells nearest the boundary returns a logarithmic law in every sampled row, with prefactors 

−1.44 to −1.26 against the predicted −λ−1+ = −1.362 at 

β = 0. The inverse-square-root law is recovered above 

ζ ≈ 0.4, where the two boundaries merge. This slow, force-limited pull-in just above threshold is the dynami-cal counterpart of the softening reported for electrostat-ically and Casimir-loaded microstructures [5, 7, 8]. A parametric PINN trained as a pure-residual equi-librium surrogate reproduces the fold locus of Eq. (5) to a mean deviation |∆α| ≈ 8 × 10 −8. The fold is a double-root (tangency) locus, where the pull-in indica-tor is second-order insensitive to the field, so boundary agreement flatters the surrogate. The fair measure of the learned equilibrium field is its root-mean-square er-ror in the pull-in gap, RMSE( u∗) ≈ 3 × 10 −3 (the point-wise maximum reaches ≈ 0.13, confined to the degener-ate α, β → 0 corner where the equilibrium minimizer is ill-conditioned). Both numbers describe the same net-work, and we quote them together to avoid overstating the accuracy.  

> C. Thermal Lifshitz shift: a classical-limit bound

Figure 3 bounds how temperature displaces the fold through the classical-limit Lifshitz correction of Eqs. (6)– (7). The curves are a high-temperature upper bound, not the physical shift: they evaluate Eq. (6) at a d = 100 nm gap far below the thermal wavelength λT = 7.6 μm(300 K), where the classical term is not quantitatively valid. Within this bound the boundary contracts mono-tonically as T rises, entirely on the Casimir side: the 

β = 0 endpoint is temperature independent, while the Casimir-only intercept moves from β∗ = 0 .0819 (0 K) to 0.0799 (300 K). The bound is one-sided and destabilizing, since heating enhances the effective Casimir attraction through the βκT / (1 − ξ)3 term of Eq. (7). At a repre-sentative β = 0 .03 it caps the pull-in-voltage reduction (with VPI ∝ √αc) at ∆ VPI /V PI ≈ − 0.24% (100 K) and 

−0.71% (300 K). This bound is β-dependent, ranging across the individual devices of Table IV from −0.003% for the electrostatic-dominated device C to −1.07% for the most Casimir-loaded device B. For the sub-100-nm devices studied here, however, d ≪ λT and the true cor-rection to the ideal-metal Casimir force is higher-order in 

d/λ T and sub-percent [4, 27, 30]. It displaces the actu-ation threshold measurably only as the gap approaches 

λT (micron scale) or at elevated temperature [29–32].  

> D. Numerical verification and the movable pole

Figure 4 collects the training diagnostics: the composite-loss histories converge under Adam warm-up and L-BFGS refinement to residuals of order 10 −6–10 −7,and the step study contrasts the integrators at the pole. At every tested step h ∈ { 0.05 , . . . , 0.001 } the fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) scheme steps across the movable pole into the unphysical region ξ ≥ 1, while the adaptive DOP853 reference and the rapidity-regularized PINN, which keeps ξ < 1 by construction on the transformed, pole-free domain of Eq. (12), both stay well posed. The point is representational rather than a blanket accuracy claim (Sec. III D).  

> E. Implications for device design

For gold-coated NEMS capacitive actuators and Casimir oscillators the fold locus supplies predictive pull-in-voltage estimates through VPI = p2αckd 3/ε 0A: the representative parameters of Table IV give VPI ≈ 0.33 V for the 100-nm and 50-nm gaps and ≈ 3.7 V for the stiffer 200-nm device, with the Casimir pre-load cutting 8the threshold below the classical β = 0 estimate at the smaller gaps. Ignoring it over-predicts the stable window and invites stiction [2, 8]. Because β ∝ A/ (kd 5) and no static equilibrium survives above the Casimir ceiling β∗,shrinking the gap collapses the available voltage window as d−5 and fixes the smallest gap that a given stiffness and area can hold open against the quantum vacuum—a constraint that stiffness or area engineering must respect. The thermal shift of Sec. IV C is a classical-limit upper bound, and for the sub-100-nm devices considered here (d ≪ λT ) the realistic derating is sub-percent: derating and the associated on-chip thermometry enter the design budget only as the gap approaches λT or at elevated tem-perature. The critical slowing down near the fold makes the threshold region a soft, high-responsivity operating point for near-threshold force, mass, and voltage sens-ing [9–11]. The differentiable surrogate is itself a design tool. Fig-ure 5 shows two uses that a direct integrator does not provide. First, because the parametric fold network is smooth in its inputs, automatic differentiation returns exact design sensitivities at no extra cost. The bound-ary slope dα c/dβ obtained by differentiating through the network matches the closed-form value −1/u 2 to a rel-ative error of ∼ 10 −6, and the same chain rule delivers the pull-in-voltage elasticities ∂ ln VPI /∂ ln x with respect to gap, area, and stiffness [+2 .79, −0.76, +0 .76 for de-vice A of Table IV; Fig. 5(b)], again validated against the analytic pipeline to ∼ 10 −6. At device B, which carries twice the Casimir loading, the same elasticities are +6 .04, −1.41, and +1 .41, so the surrogate tracks a quantity that more than doubles across the tabulated devices. Second, the surrogate inverts a device specifi-cation by gradient descent: fixing area and stiffness and targeting VPI = 0 .30 V, Adam on the gap through the differentiable network reaches its tolerance in 201 steps [Fig. 5(a)] at d = 97 .04 nm, reproducing the analytic root to femtometer accuracy. Each inverse solve here takes 1.4 s, so the advantage over a one-dimensional root find is not raw latency; it is that the same trained, differen-tiable object supplies boundary evaluations, exact sensi-tivities, and backpropagated inverse designs within one framework. On this problem the fold has a closed form, and we use it as a ground-truth oracle: it is the analytic root that every sensitivity and inverse solve above is val-idated against. The construction itself does not require that oracle. Applied to the from-rest dynamic pull-in boundary αdyn (β) at ζ = 0 .1, a threshold defined only implicitly by the integrated equation of motion once the damping is finite, a small differentiable surrogate fitted to 33 RK4-located boundary points (fit RMSE 1 .5×10 −4 in 

α) returns the design sensitivity dα dyn /dβ by automatic differentiation as −2.06, −1.80, and −1.64 at β = 0 .01, 0.03, and 0 .05, each matching a central finite difference of the independently recomputed boundary to within 0 .51% (Fig. 6); the largest of the three deviations is 0 .5006%, at 

β = 0 .05. At zero damping the threshold is not implicit: McLellan et al. solve the same double-root condition in closed form and prove that the resulting curve is the unique one separating oscillation from finite-time touch-down [38], and its β → 0 limit is the classical undamped value α = 1 /8 at ξ = 1 /2 [39]. That curve and the fold of Eq. (5) bracket the from-rest threshold at every damp-ing, since dissipation can only raise it and no equilibrium survives above the fold. One caveat governs how these dynamic results transfer to the devices of Table IV. Those sit at ζ = 5 × 10 −5 to 5 × 10 −4, set by quality factors of 10 3 to 10 4, whereas the phase diagram and the closure of the band are computed at ζ = 0 .1 to 1; a damping ratio of 0 .4 would mean 

Q ≈ 1.25, a squeeze-film-limited device rather than a resonant one. At the tabulated quality factors the from-rest threshold sits essentially on the undamped curve, so the operating bound to use is the lower edge of the bracket, and the kinetic-overshoot margin to budget is its full width, 4 /27 − 1/8 = 0 .023 at β = 0, rather than the 0 .0117 found at ζ = 0 .1. 

Limitations. Several idealizations bound the scope of these estimates. (i) The dynamics is a lumped single-degree-of-freedom reduction; a distributed clamped can-tilever carries a spectrum of modes, and the pole struc-ture survives only per mode. (ii) The force model is parallel-plate (Decca-type); a real sphere–plate or rough-ened geometry carries a different force exponent, cap-tured only approximately by the proximity-force approx-imation. (iii) The pull-in boundary is computed from the quasi-static fold and its from-rest dynamic counter-part, not from a full driven-and-damped response un-der a specific actuation waveform. (iv) The Casimir load is the ideal-conductor form; finite conductivity, sur-face roughness, and the low-temperature thermal regime are neglected, so β, VPI , and the quoted thermal shifts are upper bounds (Secs. II and IV C). (v) The PINN velocity remains the weakest link near collapse, with 

E∞( ˙ξ) ≈ 3.4 × 10 −2. Quantitative benchmarking against a specific fabricated device, and relaxation of these ide-alizations, are left to future work.  

> V. CONCLUSIONS

We reduced a gold-coated nanoelectromechanical (NEMS) actuator under competing electrostatic and Casimir loads to the single dimensionless equation of motion (2), governed by the electrostatic strength α,the Casimir strength β, and the damping ratio ζ. In the ( α, β ) plane the static saddle-node fold (5) bounds the stable operating range, running from the classical electrostatic pull-in αc = 4 /27 to the Casimir ceiling 

β∗ = 256 /3125, above which no equilibrium survives at any bias. Integrating from rest places the dynamic pull-in boundary inside this fold by a mean gap ∆ α = 0 .0117 in the underdamped regime, a kinetic overshoot that shrinks monotonically with damping and vanishes at a finite damping ratio, proved for ζ ≥ 2−1/4 and located numerically at ζc = 0 .3959, above which the two bound-9

For the raw parametrization, where the barrier is active, N ∼ 512–1024 collocation points and λ2 ∼ 10 are adequate starting values.  

> S2. Parametric surrogate for the fold locus

The second network takes the control pair ( α, β ) as in-put and returns the equilibrium gap, trained on the force-balance residual alone with no labeled solution data. Ta-ble S2 gives its architecture and its accuracy against the closed-form fold. Two accuracy figures describe the same network, and quoting either alone misrepresents it. The mean de-viation of the learned fold from the analytic locus is 7.9 × 10 −8 in α. That number flatters the surrogate: the fold is a double-root locus, where the pull-in indicator is second-order insensitive to the underlying field, so a field error of order ϵ displaces the boundary by order ϵ2. The fair measure is the error of the equilibrium field itself, RMSE( u∗) = 3 .1 × 10 −3, with a pointwise maximum of 0.126. That maximum is confined to the corner α, β → 0, where the equilibrium sits at ξ → 0 and the minimizer that defines u∗ is ill-conditioned, since the force balance there is flat in u over a wide interval. Away from that corner the field error stays below 10 −2.Cell-by-cell classification separates the two error scales. Against the static fold the surrogate labels 100 .00% of grid cells correctly. Against the from-rest RK4 outcome it reaches 94 .70%, which is exactly the score of the static fold itself on the same test: the residual 5 .30% is the kinetic-overshoot band of Sec. S3, not a defect of the network: a surrogate fitted to the static problem cannot see a dynamic effect.

S3. Numerical construction of the phase diagram 

The phase diagram integrates the equation of motion from rest on a 200 × 200 grid, once at ζ = 0 .1 and once at ζ = 0 .7. Table S3 records the grid, the integrator settings, and the summary statistics quoted in the main text. Three choices set the reported numbers. The pull-in event fires at ξ ≥ 1 − ϵ with ϵ = 10 −6, which corresponds to a physical gap of 0 .1 pm at d = 100 nm and is far inside any regime the continuum force model describes; the event time τPI is insensitive to ϵ because the last decade of gap closure occupies a few parts in 10 3 of the trajectory. The horizon τmax = 200 is 20 damping times at ζ = 0 .1, long enough that a cell not having collapsed by then has settled. A guard flags any trajectory reaching 

ξ < −10 −9; no cell triggered it in either sweep. The dynamic boundary lies inside the static fold by ∆α, with mean 0 .0117, median 0 .0121, and maximum 0.0141 over 182 boundary samples at ζ = 0 .1. The overshoot band covers 5 .30% of the sampled plane at ζ = 0 .1, matching the classification deficit of Sec. S2 to the last digit, as it must, since both count the same cells. S5. Design sensitivities by automatic differentiation 

Differentiating the trained fold surrogate with respect to its inputs returns design sensitivities without a finite-difference stencil. Table S5 compares three of them against a central difference of the closed-form pipeline 23 at devices A and B, which bracket the Casimir loading of the tabulated devices. The three elasticities ∂ ln VPI /∂ ln p read +2 .789 for the gap, −0.758 for the area, and +0 .758 for the stiffness. The last two are equal and opposite because VPI depends on A and k only through the ratio A/k at fixed β, so the surrogate reproduces a symmetry of the problem it was never told about. The gap elasticity is not the naive 3 /2of the purely electrostatic law VPI ∝ d3/2: the Casimir loading makes αc itself a function of d through β ∝ d−5,and the chain rule adds the difference. That correction grows with the loading: at device B, where β = 0 .052, the gap elasticity is +6 .042 against +2 .789 at device A, while the area and stiffness elasticities move to ∓1.408 and keep their equal-and-opposite relation. The quantity being differentiated is therefore far from constant over the device range, and the surrogate follows it: the relative error against the closed form is 3 .4 × 10 −6 or better at device A and 1 .4 × 10 −6 or better at device B. The boundary slope dα c/dβ is available in closed form as −1/u 2 and provides a stricter test, since it is a deriva-tive of the fold rather than of a quantity built from it. Differentiating through the network gives −2.0691, 

−1.8854, and −1.7117 at β = 0.01, 0 .026, and 0 .05, against analytic values that differ in the sixth digit; the largest relative error over the three points is 7 .3 × 10 −6. 

> S6. Gradient-based inverse design

The same surrogate inverts a specification. Fixing A =100 μm2 and k = 0 .5 N /m and targeting VPI = 0 .30 V, Adam descends on the gap through the differentiable boundary. Table S6 lists the outcome. The optimizer reaches its tolerance after 201 steps and 1.42 s, recovering d = 97 .0364 nm against the analytic root 97 .0364 nm, an absolute error of 1 .7 × 10 −5 nm. The realized pull-in voltage lands within 8 .8 × 10 −9 V of the target. Applied instead to the from-rest dynamic bound-ary αdyn (β), which is defined only implicitly by the inte-grated equation of motion, a surrogate fitted to 33 RK4-located boundary points with a fit RMSE of 1 .5 × 10 −4

in α returns dα dyn /dβ as −2.06, −1.80, and −1.64 at 

β = 0 .01, 0 .03, and 0 .05. Each matches a central finite difference of the independently recomputed boundary to 0 .09%, 0 .01%, and 0 .5006% respectively, at a finite-difference step h = 2 .5 × 10 −3 and a bisection tolerance of 10 −6 in α.

> S8. Closure of the kinetic-overshoot band

The band between the from-rest threshold and the static fold shrinks with damping and closes at a finite damping ratio. Table S8 resolves that closure at β = 0 on eleven values of ζ, so the approach can be read rather than inferred from the four values quoted in the ap-pendix. The widths are located by bisection on the crossing of the saddle, a finite-time decidable event, and the classifi-cation is safe because the node below it is hyperbolic and is reached on a damping time. That criterion is unrelated to the backward continuation of the strong stable mani-fold used in the appendix, and the two agree to 6 ×10 −6 at the two damping ratios where both were run. The scan stops at ζ = 0 .35, where the width is still 3 .6 × 10 −4:closer to ζc the width falls below what a finite integra-tion horizon resolves, which is the one weakness of this criterion. The closure is quadratic. Fitting αc −αdyn = C(ζc −ζ)2

over ζ ≥ 0.20 with ζc = 0 .3959 gives C = 0 .167, against 

C = 0 .173 from the four appendix values over a wider range; the two differ by 4%, the spread expected from fitting a leading-order law on different intervals.
