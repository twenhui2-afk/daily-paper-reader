Abstract: Optimal transport potentials are fundamental objects in statis-tics, economics, and machine learning: their gradients generate optimal transport maps, while the potentials themselves act as location-dependent dual prices and sensitivity variables. We study the estimation of the quadratic optimal transport potential when a fixed absolutely continuous reference distribution μ is transported to an unknown distribution ν, accessed to via its empirical measure. Our main ingredient is a stability inequality that controls the L1(μ) distance, modulo additive constants, between a strongly convex potential φ and a convex potential eφ by a weak dual norm of (∇ eφ)#μ − (∇φ)#μ, together with a second-order Wasserstein remainder of logarithmic type. This separation between the leading empirical-process term and the Wasserstein remainder yields faster convergence for potentials than for the corresponding transport maps. Under smoothness and uniform convexity assumptions, the exact semidis-crete Brenier potential converges in L1(μ) at rate n−1/2 for d ≤ 3, at rate 

n−1/2(log n)5/2 for d = 4 , and at rate n−2/d (log n)(d+2) /d for d ≥ 5. The polynomial exponents are sharp. In dimensions d ≤ 3, we further establish a nondegenerate function-space central limit theorem and prove consistency of the nonparametric bootstrap. These results yield joint root-n inference for every fixed finite collection of normalization-invariant weighted con-trasts of the potential, including regional shadow premia in reference-based risk problems. Finally, we prove matching upper and lower bounds of order 

ε log(1 /ε ) for the normalization-invariant sum of the entropic dual poten-tials.

1. Introduction 

Optimal transport (OT) has become a common framework for comparing, match-ing, and transforming probability distributions across statistics, machine learn-ing, economics, and nonlinear partial differential equations (Panaretos and Zemel, 2019; Peyré and Cuturi, 2019; Galichon, 2016; Benamou and Brenier, 1998). Its dual potentials play a central role: for quadratic transport, their gradients gen-erate the optimal assignment, while the potentials themselves record the local 

1arXiv:2608.00649v1 [math.ST] 1 Aug 2026 /Empirical optimal transport potentials 2

value of the marginal constraints. They therefore retain information that is lost when OT is reduced to a single distance or cost. Turning to applications, potential-level quantities arise as integrated multi-variate quantiles and risk or inequality indices (Ekeland, Galichon and Henry, 2012; Bercu, Bigot and Thurin, 2024; Hallin and Mordant, 2025), equilibrium payoff and price schedules (Chiappori, McCann and Nesheim, 2010; Galichon, 2016; Galichon and Salanié, 2022), and learned convex functions in generative modeling (Makkuva et al., 2020; Paty, d’Aspremont and Cuturi, 2020). Phys-ical examples include semi-geostrophic geopotentials (Benamou and Brenier, 1998; Bourne et al., 2022) and Monge–Ampère–Kantorovich reconstruction of the primordial gravitational potential from galaxy catalogues (Lévy, Mohayaee and von Hausegger, 2021; Nikakhtar et al., 2023), see below for a more detailed exposition. In these domains, one distribution is often fixed while the other is learned from data. In practice, the potentials are typically computed after replacing the unknown distribution by an empirical or other plug-in estimate, often with entropic regularization. This raises the following statistical question: 

How well can we estimate the optimal transport potential as a function of the sample size? 

We now introduce the setting. We consider quadratic transport from a fixed absolutely continuous reference distribution μ to an unknown target distribu-tion ν. 1.2. Relationship with previous work and literature review 

1.2.1. Potentials as objects of interest. 

The transport value, map, and potential answer different questions. The value is one number, and the map records the assignment. The potential is the dual optimizer: its gradient generates the quadratic transport map, and its averages give the shadow values of changes in the marginal constraints. Thus, the poten-tial links the optimal value, the assignment, and the effect of local changes in a distribution. This role is explicit in several applications. In matching models, potentials are equilibrium payoff and price schedules (Chiappori, McCann and Nesheim, 2010; Galichon, 2016; Galichon and Salanié, 2022). In multivariate risk, quan-tiles, and inequality measurement, integrated or averaged potentials are the re-ported quantities (Ekeland, Galichon and Henry, 2012; Bercu, Bigot and Thurin, 2024; Hallin and Mordant, 2025). In machine learning, convex potentials are learned and differentiated to produce transport maps (Makkuva et al., 2020; Paty, d’Aspremont and Cuturi, 2020). Potentials also appear as geopotentials in semi-geostrophic models (Benamou and Brenier, 1998; Bourne et al., 2022). Our limit theorem applies directly when the reference distribution is fixed and smooth, the target distribution is sampled, and the quantity of interest is a fixed normalization-invariant weighted average of the potential. It therefore gives joint root-n inference, in dimensions d ≤ 3, for regional shadow premia and local distribution-shift audits, as developed in Section 3. It also covers smooth-reference versions of integrated-quantile and inequality summaries. For learned transport potentials, it describes the sampling error of the exact semidiscrete optimizer; approximation and optimization errors require separate control. Cosmological reconstruction gives the potential a direct physical meaning. In the idealized real-space Zel’dovich model, an initial uniform mass field is transported to the present matter distribution and 

Φ( q) = 12 ∥q∥2 − ϕI (q),/Empirical optimal transport potentials 5

where ϕI is the primordial gravitational potential (Lévy, Mohayaee and von Hausegger, 2021). Real surveys also involve biased or weighted tracers, unob-served mass, survey geometry, and redshift-space effects (Nikakhtar et al., 2023). This makes cosmology a clear target for extensions of our results. 

1.2.2. Empirical costs and maps. 

The statistical OT literature first concentrated on the optimal value. Limit laws for empirical transport costs are available in discrete, semidiscrete, and smooth settings; see del Barrio and Loubes (2019); del Barrio, González-Sanz and Loubes (2024); Sommerfeld and Munk (2018) and the review of del Barrio et al. (2026). A cost is one scalar and therefore does not recover the spatially indexed shadow values studied here. A separate literature estimates the Bre-nier map: Hütter and Rigollet (2021) establish minimax rates, Manole et al. (2024) study plug-in map estimators, and Manole et al. (2023) prove point-wise limit theorems for smoothed map estimators on the flat torus. Cazelles, Pauwels and Portales (2026) construct maps and couplings from discrete Bre-nier potentials. These results target ∇φ or its induced coupling. Our loss is instead imposed directly on the smoother primitive φ, modulo constants, and our limit is function-valued; neither result implies the other. 

1.2.3. Stability and convergence rates. 

Delalande and Mérigot (2023) control Brenier maps, and consequently potentials through Poincaré’s inequality, by a Wasserstein discrepancy. Extensions cover log-concave sources and John domains (Letrouit and Mérigot, 2024), general power costs (Mischler and Trevisan, 2024), and squared Riemannian distance (Kitagawa, Letrouit and Mérigot, 2025). In the smooth–smooth regime, Caja-Lopez, Delgadino and Kitagawa (2026) obtain Lipschitz stability and linear response when both marginals vary; that regime excludes the atomic empirical target considered here. Applied to an empirical target, the available Wasserstein-based potential bound gives essentially n−1/d in high dimension. Our stability inequality re-places its leading term by the empirical-process norm dual to C1,LogLip and retains Wasserstein distance only in a second-order remainder. It yields n−1/2

for d ≤ 3 and n−2/d , up to logarithms, for d ≥ 5. The polynomial exponents are sharp: the low-dimensional lower bound follows from our nondegenerate functional limit, while the high-dimensional lower bound follows from approx-imating a strongly convex function by a polyhedral function with at most n

active pieces. Thus the comparison is not only an improved upper bound; it identifies the correct polynomial scale for the empirical potential. 

1.2.4. Limit theory for potentials. 

del Barrio, González-Sanz and Loubes (2024) prove a central limit theorem for /Empirical optimal transport potentials 6

semidiscrete dual weights when the target support is fixed and finite. Random-ness there changes a finite vector of weights. Here the empirical target has n

random sites, so the number and locations of the Laguerre cells both change with n. For fixed entropic regularization, González-Sanz, Loubes and Niles-Weed (2022); Goldfeld et al. (2024) obtain a functional central limit theorem for smooth Schrödinger potentials, while Mordant (2024) lets the regularization de-crease and balances regularization bias against stochastic error in pointwise and 

L2 limits. Our theorem instead treats the exactly unregularized semidiscrete estimator and gives convergence in Lp′

(Ω) /⟨1⟩ for d ≤ 3. It therefore yields joint inference for fixed Lp-weighted contrasts, but deliberately does not claim pointwise inference or a limit theorem for the map. 

1.2.5. Entropic regularization. 

Strong L1 convergence without a rate is proved by Nutz and Wiesel (2022). López-Rivera (2026) derives local uniform rates for potentials and their gradients under convexity assumptions, whereas Malamut and Sylvestre (2025) gives sharp asymptotics for regularized values and plans. Statistical limit laws for fixed regularization are available for the value (Mena and Niles-Weed, 2019) and the potentials (González-Sanz, Loubes and Niles-Weed, 2022; Goldfeld et al., 2024). Our result answers a different bias question: for the normalization-invariant sum of the two potentials, Theorem 2.12 proves matching upper and lower L1 bounds of order ε log(1 /ε ). The upper bound comes from our stability inequality and the lower bound from the value asymptotics of Malamut and Sylvestre (2025).
