SOME KNOTS WITH NO SU(2)-ABELIAN SURGERIES                                        

> GIACOMO BASCAPE
> Abstract. A knot in S3is said to be not SU (2)-abelian knot, if every non-trivial surgery along it yields a 3-manifold whose fundamental group admits an irreducible SU (2)-representation. We provide examples of knots in S3that are not SU (2)-abelian. A knot Kis said to be SU (2)-clean
> if whenever the fundamental group of the integer r-surgery π1(K(r)) has no SU (2)-irreducible representation, then the Alexander polynomial of Kdoes not vanish at any r-th root of unity. We show that if Kis a non-trivial SU (2)-clean knot, the connected sum K#Kis never SU (2)-abelian. Finally, combining known results on SU (2)-abundant knots and a classical epimorphism between knot groups, we show that every non-torus knot with at most 9 crossings is not SU (2)-abelian, with at most the two exceptions of 9 47 and 9 49 .

1. Introduction 

In this work, we consider only compact and oriented 3-manifolds. For a given property P of closed 3-manifolds, we say that a knot in S3 is a P-knot if there exists a non-trivial surgery on it yielding a 3-manifold with property P. For instance, the right-handed trefoil 3 1 is an L-space knot , since the 1-surgery on it yields a Heegaard Floer L-space. For the definition of Heegaard Floer homology, we refer the reader to [OS01] and [OS04]. A natural question in knot theory is to characterize which knots in S3 are P-knots. Among the various properties that a 3-manifold may possess, those defined in terms of SU (2)-representations of the fundamental group have proven to be particularly deep and powerful. The study of SU (2)-representations plays a central role in low-dimensional topology, provid-ing access to subtle geometric and topological features. A classical result such as the proof of Property P [KM04] highlights the strength of SU (2)-representation techniques in distinguishing 3-manifolds and detecting essential topological phenomena. In this perspective, understanding when a 3-manifold is SU (2)-abelian, meaning that its fundamental group admits only abelian 

SU (2)-representations, provides a natural direction of inquiry. Therefore, in this work, we take 

P to be the property of being SU (2)-abelian and, in particular, we shall study knots that are 

not SU (2)-abelian; said differently, we will study knots in S3 such that every non-trivial surgery yields a 3-manifold that is not SU (2)-abelian. Torus knots admit infinitely many surgeries with cyclic fundamental group, and therefore they are examples of SU (2)-abelian knots. Conversely, iterated torus knots Cp1,q 1 Cp2,q 2 (Tp3,q 3 ) with 

pi ≥ 2 are examples of knots that are not SU (2)-abelian by [SZ22b, Theorem 4.1]. As a general fact, it is worth mentioning that if a knot K is not SU (2)-abelian, then it is abundant in the sense of [LY21, Definition 1.1]. We expect composite knots in S3, namely those obtained as the connected sum of two non-trivial knots, to be non-SU (2)-abelian knots. Indeed, it is known that composite knots are not L-space knots [BGH24, Corollary 3.11]. Moreover, Zhang’s conjecture [Zha19, Conjecture 4] predicts that a non-L-space knot should be non-SU (2)-abelian. In light of these results, one naturally expects that composite knots provide examples of knots that are not SU (2)-abelian. This work provides a partial answer to this question. To obtain a concrete result, we introduce an additional hypothesis. Namely, we consider SU (2) -clean knots and prove that for a non-trivial knot K ⊂ S3, the connected sum K2 = K#K is not

The SU (2)-clean condition appears naturally in the theory. For instance, if K is a Berge knot and r ∈ Z is a cyclic surgery slope for K, then the Alexander polynomial of K does not vanish at any r-th root of unity (see Theorem 5.5). More generally, every cyclic surgery is SU (2)-abelian, while cyclic surgery slopes are necessarily integral by the Cyclic Surgery Theorem of Culler, Gordon, Luecke, and Shalen. In this sense, the SU (2)-clean condition may be viewed as a natural extension of a phenomenon already present in the classical theory of cyclic surgeries. At present, the author is not aware of any knot that is not SU (2)-clean. On the contrary, as shown in Corollary 5.4, torus knots, 2-bridge knots, and the particularly important pretzel knot P (−2, 3, 7) are SU (2)-clean. Moreover, any knot that admits no non-trivial SU (2)-abelian surgery is automatically SU (2)-clean. Let K be a knot in S3, we denote by E(K) the manifold S3 \ ν(K), where ν(K) is an open tubular neighborhood of the knot K. For a given knot K ⊂ S3, the space T (K, ∂ ) is defined as the set of representations π1 (∂E (K)) → U (1) that extend to π1 (E (K)). Here U (1) is the subgroup of diagonal matrices of SU (2). Details can be found in [Bas25]. In particular, T (K, ∂ ) makes it possible to study SU (2)-representations via geometric methods, by looking at subsets of the torus Hom( π1 (∂E (K)) , U (1)), without resorting to gauge theory or instanton Floer homology. The final part of this paper is devoted to the problem of detecting non-SU (2)-abelian knots among knots with small crossing number. A quick search in KnotInfo [LM25] shows that every L-space knot with at most nine crossings is a torus knot. Since torus knots are classical examples of SU (2)-abelian knots, this suggests that the remaining knots with at most 9 crossings should fail to be SU (2)-abelian. By combining the results on SU (2)-abundant knots in [LY21] with the existence of epimorphisms established in [KS08], we obtain the following result, leaving only two exceptions. 

Corollary 1.3. Let K be a knot with at most 9 crossings. If K is neither a torus knot nor in 

{947 , 949 }, then K is not SU (2) -abelian.

2. Notation 

In this section, we fix the notation and conventions that will be used throughout this work. We begin by recalling the standard notation for satellite knots and their associated spaces. Let P (J)denote the satellite knot with pattern P and companion knot J. The winding number of P is defined as the algebraic intersection number between P and an essential disc in V = S1 ×D2. The exterior of the satellite P (J) is obtained by gluing VP to E (J), where VP denotes the exterior of 

P considered as a knot in V . We refer to VP as the pattern space associated with P . Details of these constructions can be found in [Gor83, Section 2]. Let M be a manifold with torus boundary. A (rational) slope on the boundary M is an element [α] of the projective space of H1(∂M ; Q), where α ∈ H1(∂M ; Q) \ { 0}. Slopes can be identified as either 

• a ±-pair of primitive elements of H1(∂M ; Z) ≡ π1 (∂M ); 

• a ∂M -isotopy class of essential simple closed curves on ∂M .For further details, see [BC22, Subsection 4.2]. Let Σ be a torus, and {μ, λ } a basis of H1(Σ; Z). We use the convention, depending on the choice of the base {μ, λ }, such that an element p/q ∈ Q∪{ 1/0} corresponds to the slope pμ +qλ ∈ H1(Σ; Z). {μi, λ i} for each H1(Σ i; Z), then 

Y (Σ 1, · · · , Σn; r1/sn, · · · rn/sn)denotes the closed 3-manifold obtained by performing Dehn fillings along a simple closed curve representing ri/si on Σ i for each i = 1 , · · · , n . If Y has a torus boundary component, we define the rational longitude of Y as the unique slope λY ⊂ Y such that [ λY ] is a torsion element of 

H1(Y ; Z).

The following theorem appears to be well known to the author, although no explicit reference could be found. For the reader’s convenience, we include a proof. This result provides some justification for the assumption that the knot under consideration is SU (2)-clean in our study of composite knots. As a consequence of Proposition 3.4, if a knot K1 is not SU (2)-abelian, then the connected sum K1#K2 is not SU (2)-abelian for any knot K2. Therefore, one is naturally led to consider pairs of knots for which each factor admits a non-trivial SU (2)-abelian surgery. This is precisely the setting in which the SU (2)-clean assumption becomes relevant. Indeed, 

SU (2)-abelian knots may be regarded as the SU (2) analogue of knots admitting a cyclic surgery. 10 

In particular, any knot admitting a non-trivial cyclic surgery admits a non-trivial SU (2)-abelian surgery. It is not known whether the converse holds, or whether there exists a knot admitting a non-trivial SU (2)-abelian surgery and only the trivial cyclic surgery. Since cyclic surgeries are necessarily integral by the celebrated Cyclic Surgery Theorem of Culler, Gordon, Luecke, and Shalen, the analogy is especially close for integer surgeries.

Lemma 6.5. Let r ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} be an integer and K a non-trivial knot. The manifold K(r/s) is not SU (2) -abelian. Proof. Since r ≤ 4, we obtain that |r/s| ∈ [0 , 4]. Definition 6.8 ([LY21, Defintion 1.1]) . A knot K ⊂ S3 is called SU (2)-abundant if the following two conditions hold: 

• For all but finitely many r ∈ Q, the manifold K(r) is not SU (2)-abelian,` u

• For any r = p/q ∈ Q such that K(r) is SU (2)-abelian, there is some p-th root of unity 

ζ ∈ C such that ∆ K (ζ2) = 0. 

Remark 6.9. There are exactly 84 prime knots with crossing number at most 9. Among them, only the following 7 knots have Alexander polynomial with all coefficients in {− 1, 0, 1}:31, 51, 71, 819 , 91, 947 , 949 .

Consequently, by [LY21, Theorem 1.3], every prime knot with crossing number at most 9 that is not in the above list is SU (2)-abundant. Among the 7 exceptional knots, the knots 31 = T (2 , 3) , 51 = T (2 , 5) , 71 = T (2 , 7) , 819 = T (3 , 4) , 91 = T (2 , 9) are torus knots, whereas 9 47 and 9 49 are non-torus knots, each of bridge number 3. 

Remark 6.10. For an integer n ≥ 2, we denote by Φ n(t) ∈ Z[t] the n-th cyclotomic polynomial. This is an irreducible polynomial over Q whose roots are the primitive n-th root of unity. In particular Φ 6(t) = t2 − t + 1, its roots are the primitive 6-th roots of unity e±iπ/ 3. We record into Table 1 the Alexander polynomial factored over Q of some knots. In particular, for a knot 

K ⊂ S3 its Alexander polynomial ∆ K (t) has a primitive root of unity as a root of order p if and only if Φ p(t) appers in the decomposition of ∆ K (t).
